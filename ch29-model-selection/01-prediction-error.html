29.1  Prediction error and optimism

Chapter 19 compared a short regression with a long one and found that neither wins everywhere. Leaving out regressors whose coefficients are small buys a drop in variance that can outweigh the bias it causes, and Theorem 19.1.5 located the break-even point exactly: at the design points the short fit is better iff the noncentrality Eq. 19.1.1 of the omitted block is less than the number of columns omitted. The noncentrality is unknown, so the theorem does not choose a model. It does say what a good choice should aim at, namely the error of the fitted values as predictions, and this chapter builds that into procedures.

The residual mean square describes how well a fit reproduces the data it was fitted to, and every fitting procedure bends towards those data. This section makes the size of that bend exact for any procedure, and turns it into a definition of the degrees of freedom a procedure spends.

29.1.1. In-sample prediction error

Throughout the chapter the data follow

𝛍𝛆𝛆𝛆Y=μ+ε,E(ε)=0,Cov(ε)=σ2In,
(29.1.1)

where the mean vector 𝛍μ∈Rn is unrestricted. A candidate linear model C(X) may or may not contain 𝛍μ. That is the point of model selection: we want procedures whose properties do not depend on a candidate being right. A fitting procedure is any function 𝛍𝛍μ^=μ^(Y) from Rn to Rn with 𝛍E‖μ^(Y)‖2<∞: least squares on a fixed model, least squares on a model chosen from the data, ridge regression, thresholding.

Definition 29.1.1 (Training error, prediction error, optimism)

Let 𝛍𝛆Y∗=μ+ε∗, where 𝛆ε∗ is independent of 𝛆ε and has the same distribution: new responses at the same design points. For a fitting procedure 𝛍μ^:

  • (a) the training error is 𝛍err―=n−1‖Y−μ^‖2;

  • (b) the in-sample prediction error is 𝛍Errin=n−1E(‖Y∗−μ^‖2|Y);

  • (c) the optimism is Errin−err―, and its mean ω=E(Errin−err―) is the expected optimism.

Expanding 𝛍𝛆𝛍𝛍Y∗−μ^=ε∗+(μ−μ^) and using the independence of 𝛆ε∗ and Y,

𝛍𝛍Errin=σ2+1n‖μ^−μ‖2.
(29.1.2)

So the in-sample prediction error is the irreducible σ2 plus the average squared error of the fitted values as estimates of the mean. Ranking procedures by E(Errin) is the same as ranking them by the risk 𝛍𝛍E‖μ^−μ‖2, which is the criterion of Theorem 19.1.5(c). In-sample refers to the design points: the responses are new, the regressor values old. New regressor values are taken up at the end of the section.

29.1.2. The covariance penalty

Theorem 29.1.1 (Optimism is a covariance)

Under Eq. 29.1.1, let 𝛍μ^ be a fitting procedure.

  • (a) The expected optimism is ω=2n∑i=1nCov(μ^i,Yi).

  • (b) If 𝛍μ^=SY for a fixed n×n matrix S, then ω=2σ2tr(S)/n and 𝛍𝛍𝛍E‖μ^−μ‖2=‖(I−S)μ‖2+σ2tr(SS⊤).

  • (c) For least squares on a model matrix X of rank p, whether or not 𝛍μ∈C(X), 𝛍𝛍E(err―)=σ2n−pn+‖(I−M)μ‖2n,E(Errin)=σ2n+pn+‖(I−M)μ‖2n, and ω=2σ2p/n.

Proof.

  • (a) Write 𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍Y−μ^=(Y−μ)−(μ^−μ). Then 𝛍𝛆𝛍𝛍𝛆𝛍𝛍‖Y−μ^‖2=‖ε‖2+‖μ^−μ‖2−2ε⊤(μ^−μ). Take expectations and compare with Eq. 29.1.2: 𝛍𝛍nE(Errin)=nσ2+E‖μ^−μ‖2, so 𝛆𝛍𝛍nω=2E[ε⊤(μ^−μ)]=2∑iE[(Yi−μi)μ^i], since 𝛍μ is constant and 𝛆E(ε)=0. Because E(Yi−μi)=0, the last expectation is Cov(μ^i,Yi). The moments exist by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
  • (b) Cov(SY,Y)=σ2S, whose diagonal sums to σ2tr(S). For the risk, 𝛍𝛆𝛍SY−μ=Sε−(I−S)μ has mean 𝛍−(I−S)μ and covariance σ2SS⊤, and Theorem 2.3.1 with A=I gives the sum of the squared mean and the trace.
  • (c) Take S=M, with tr(M)=p and MM⊤=M (Proposition 6.3.4). Then 𝛍E(Errin)=σ2+n−1{‖(I−M)μ‖2+σ2p}, and subtracting ω gives E(err―).

Part (c) with 𝛍μ∈C(X) is Proposition 14.2.7(a). What is new is that the optimism of least squares does not depend on 𝛍μ: a wrong model has larger training and prediction errors, but both grow by the same squared bias, so a penalty depending only on p is a sensible correction even for wrong models. Part (a) asks nothing of the procedure but finite second moments: a fit is optimistic to the extent that each fitted value follows its own response.

Idea. The training error of a procedure understates its prediction error by 2/n times the total covariance between fitted values and responses. For least squares on p columns the covariance is σ2p, right model or wrong.

Example (Optimism of a wrong model)

Take n=30 equally spaced points ti on [−1,1], a curved mean μi=1+ti+1.5ti2, σ=1, and fit by least squares a straight line in t together with two columns of pure noise, so p=4. The model is wrong: its squared bias per observation is 𝛍‖(I−M)μ‖2/n=0.2054. Over 20000 simulated data sets the average optimism is 0.2688, against 2σ2p/n=0.2667 from Theorem 29.1.1(c). Ridge regression (Theorem 27.2.2) on the same columns with penalty λ=5 is a linear smoother with tr(S)=3.207; its simulated optimism is 0.2163, against 2×3.207/30=0.2138 from part (b).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(2901)
n, sigma, reps = 30, 1.0, 20_000

t = np.linspace(-1, 1, n)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), t, rng.normal(size=(n, 2))])   # line + 2 irrelevant columns
mu = 1 + t + 1.5 * t**2                                         # the true mean is curved
p = X.shape[1]
M = X @ np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T)                           # projection onto C(X)

Y = mu + sigma * rng.normal(size=(reps, n))                     # one data set per row
fit = Y @ M                                                     # M is symmetric
train = np.mean((Y - fit) ** 2, axis=1)                         # training error
err_in = sigma**2 + np.mean((fit - mu) ** 2, axis=1)            # in-sample prediction error
print(f"average optimism {np.mean(err_in - train):.4f},  2 sigma^2 p / n = {2 * sigma**2 * p / n:.4f}")
lam = 5.0
S = X @ np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + lam * np.eye(p), X.T)         # ridge smoother
fit_r = Y @ S.T
train_r = np.mean((Y - fit_r) ** 2, axis=1)
err_in_r = sigma**2 + np.mean((fit_r - mu) ** 2, axis=1)
omega_r = np.mean(err_in_r - train_r)
print(f"ridge: optimism {omega_r:.4f},  2 sigma^2 tr(S) / n = {2 * np.trace(S) / n:.4f}")

29.1.3. Degrees of freedom of a procedure

Theorem 29.1.1 suggests measuring the complexity of any procedure by the covariance it creates.

Definition 29.1.2 (Degrees of freedom)

The degrees of freedom of a fitting procedure 𝛍μ^ under Eq. 29.1.1 is 𝛍df(μ^)=1σ2∑i=1nCov(μ^i,Yi), so that 𝛍ω=2σ2df(μ^)/n.

For least squares on a fixed model df=p, the number of free coefficients, and for a linear smoother df=tr(S). For ridge regression with singular values dj of X this is ∑jdj2/(dj2+λ), the effective degrees of freedom of Theorem 27.2.2, which falls from rank(X) to 0 as λ grows. If df is known,

𝛍Err^in=err―+2σ2ndf(μ^)
(29.1.3)

is an unbiased estimate of E(Errin) by Theorem 29.1.1(a). Section 29.2 turns this into Mallows’ Cp. For a procedure that searches, the covariance is not a count and has to be computed. Under normal errors it is computed by Stein’s lemma, Lemma 27.5.1(b), proved in Section 27.5 for the James–Stein theorem: if 𝛉Z∼Np(θ,σ2I) and each gi is absolutely continuous in zi with the integrability stated there, then 𝛉E[(Z−θ)⊤g(Z)]=σ2E∑i∂gi(Z)/∂zi.

Proposition 29.1.2 (Degrees of freedom as a divergence)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,σ2I). Suppose that for each i, for almost every value of the other coordinates, yi↦μ^i(y) is absolutely continuous, and that E|∂μ^i/∂yi| and Eμ^i2 are finite. Then 𝛍df(μ^)=E∑i=1n∂μ^i∂yi(Y), and err―+2σ2n−1∑i∂μ^i/∂yi is an unbiased estimate of E(Errin) that does not involve 𝛍μ.

Proof. Since E(Yi−μi)=0, 𝛍𝛍∑iCov(μ^i,Yi)=E[(Y−μ)⊤μ^(Y)], and each term is integrable by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Lemma 27.5.1(b) with 𝛍g=μ^ turns this into σ2E∑i∂μ^i/∂yi. The unbiasedness statement is then Theorem 29.1.1(a).

The estimate in the proposition is Stein’s unbiased risk estimate (Stein 1981). For a linear smoother the divergence is tr(S), and nothing is new. The interesting cases are adaptive.

Soft thresholding. Take an orthonormal design, X⊤X=Ip, and put 𝛍Z=X⊤Y∼Np(X⊤μ,σ2I). For a fit 𝛍𝛃μ^=Xβ^(Z), 𝛃𝛃∑iCov(μ^i,Yi)=trCov(Xβ^,Y)=trCov(β^,X⊤Y)=∑jCov(β^j,Zj), so the degrees of freedom can be computed coordinate by coordinate in the sequence model Zj∼N(θj,σ2). The lasso in this design is soft thresholding, β^j=sign(Zj)(|Zj|−λ)+ (Theorem 27.4.2). It is Lipschitz, with derivative 1{|Zj|>λ} except at ±λ, so by Proposition 29.1.2 its degrees of freedom are the expected number of nonzero coefficients, as an exercise of Section 27.4 found by direct integration. The shrinkage exactly pays for the search.

Hard thresholding. Subset selection in the same design keeps or drops each coordinate whole, β^j=Zj1{|Zj|>λ} (Theorem 27.4.2 again). This jumps by λ at ±λ, so Stein’s lemma does not apply, and the degrees of freedom exceed the expected count.

Proposition 29.1.3 (Degrees of freedom of hard thresholding)

Let Z∼N(θ,σ2) and λ>0. Then Cov(Z1{|Z|>λ},Z)σ2=Pr(|Z|>λ)+λσ[ϕ(λ−θσ)+ϕ(λ+θσ)], where ϕ is the standard normal density.

Proof. Write Z=θ+σW with W∼N(0,1), and put a=(λ−θ)/σ, b=(−λ−θ)/σ, so that |Z|>λ iff W>a or W<b. The covariance is E[σW(θ+σW)1{W>a or W<b}]. The truncated moments of the standard normal are E[W1{W>a}]=ϕ(a), E[W1{W<b}]=−ϕ(b), E[W21{W>a}]=1−Φ(a)+aϕ(a) and E[W21{W<b}]=Φ(b)−bϕ(b), each by one integration by parts. Collecting terms, σθ(ϕ(a)−ϕ(b))+σ2(1−Φ(a)+Φ(b)+aϕ(a)−bϕ(b)). Since σθ+σ2a=σλ and −σθ−σ2b=σλ, this is σ2Pr(|Z|>λ)+σλ(ϕ(a)+ϕ(b)), and ϕ(b)=ϕ((λ+θ)/σ).

The first term is the expected number of coefficients kept. The second is the price of the search: the fit follows Z most closely just where Z decides whether to keep the coordinate. At the threshold λ=σ2 (which Section 29.2 shows is where Cp and AIC put it) and θ=0, the expected count is 0.157 but the degrees of freedom are 0.572: the search term 0.415 is more than twice the count. At θ=σ the degrees of freedom are 0.896, and at θ=3σ they are 1.104, more than one full parameter for a single coordinate.

Example (Best subset of a given size)

Keep the k largest of m=20 values |Zj| and set the rest to zero, with Zj∼N(θj,1) independent. For a subset fixed in advance the degrees of freedom would be k. Figure 29.1.1 shows them by simulation. When all θj=0, the best single coordinate already costs 4.91 degrees of freedom, the best five cost 13.94 and the best ten 18.27. When five means equal 6 and the rest are zero, choosing the best five is almost free (5.06), because the five are obvious; choosing a sixth means choosing among fifteen pure-noise coordinates, and the degrees of freedom jump to 9.47. Searching among many candidates of equal merit is expensive, and choosing between clear winners and clear losers is cheap.

m, reps_s = 20, 20_000
Zs = rng.normal(size=(reps_s, m))


def subset_df(theta, k):
    """Monte Carlo df of 'keep the k largest |Y_j|' when Y ~ N(theta, I)."""
    Yb = theta + Zs
    keep = np.argsort(-np.abs(Yb), axis=1)[:, :k]
    fit = np.zeros_like(Yb)
    np.put_along_axis(fit, keep, np.take_along_axis(Yb, keep, axis=1), axis=1)
    return np.mean(np.sum(fit * Zs, axis=1))                   # sum_j Cov(fit_j, Y_j)


null = np.zeros(m)
sparse = np.r_[np.full(5, 6.0), np.zeros(m - 5)]               # five large means
for k in (1, 5, 10):
    print(f"k = {k:2d}: df null {subset_df(null, k):5.2f}, df sparse {subset_df(sparse, k):5.2f}")

So a penalty of 2σ2p/n is right for a model chosen in advance and too small for a model chosen by search, a point Section 29.5 returns to.

29.1.4. New regressor values

The in-sample error keeps the design fixed. For a new case with its own regressors the error is larger, and for least squares with k normal regressors and a correct model Theorem 14.2.6 gives it exactly; since E(s2)=σ2, s2(1+1/n)(n−2)/(n−k−2) estimates it without bias. Breiman and Spector (1992) found that fixed-design estimates understate the error when the design is random, and recommended five- or ten-fold cross-validation. Cross-validation (Section 29.3) estimates the random-design error directly.

29.1.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

The procedure 𝛍μ^(Y)=Y interpolates the data. Compute its training error, its in-sample prediction error and its degrees of freedom, and check Theorem 29.1.1(a).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let Sλ=X(X⊤X+λI)−1X⊤ be the ridge smoother, whose trace ∑jdj2/(dj2+λ) was found in Theorem 27.2.2. Show that tr(SλSλ⊤)<tr(Sλ) for every λ>0 with X≠0, and that for a symmetric smoother S with eigenvalues in [0,1], tr(SS⊤)=tr(S) iff S is an orthogonal projection.

Solution. A symmetric S with eigenvalues sj∈[0,1] has tr(SS⊤)=tr(S2)=∑jsj2≤∑jsj=tr(S), with equality iff every sj∈{0,1}, that is, iff S is idempotent, an orthogonal projection (Theorem 6.3.3). The ridge smoother is symmetric with eigenvalues dj2/(dj2+λ), which lie in (0,1) for dj>0, and at least one dj is positive. So the variance term of Theorem 29.1.1(b), σ2tr(SS⊤), is strictly smaller than the optimism term σ2tr(S).

Exercise (B2)

Arrange n observations on a circle, and let μ^i be the average of Yi−q,…,Yi+q (indices modulo n, 2q+1≤n). Find the degrees of freedom and the expected optimism, and explain why a wider window is “simpler”.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In the sequence model Zj∼N(θj,σ2), j=1,…,p, independent, soft thresholding at λ has Stein’s unbiased risk estimate SURE(λ)=∑j=1pmin(Zj2,λ2)−pσ2+2σ2#{j:|Zj|>λ} by Proposition 29.1.2, because Zj−θ^j is Zj when |Zj|≤λ and ±λ otherwise. Let 0=a0≤a1≤⋯≤ap be 0 and the ordered |Zj|. Show that SURE is nondecreasing on each interval [ak,ak+1), so it is minimized over λ≥0 at one of a0,…,ap. Compute SURE(0), and explain why the minimized value is biased downwards. (Minimizing SURE is the SureShrink rule of Donoho and Johnstone 1995.)

Solution. On [ak,ak+1) exactly p−k of the |Zj| exceed λ (for distinct values), so SURE(λ)=∑|Zj|≤akZj2+(p−k)λ2−pσ2+2σ2(p−k), which is nondecreasing in λ and smallest at ak. At λ=0 nothing is shrunk and SURE(0)=pσ2, the exact risk of Z. Each SURE(ak) is unbiased for its own risk, so their minimum has expectation at most the smallest risk: the optimism of a minimized estimate studied in Section 29.5.

Exercise (C2)

In Example (Best subset of a given size) with all θj=0 and k=1, show that the degrees of freedom equal EmaxjZj2 (with σ=1). Deduce that they are at least 1, and use EmaxjZj2≥(Emaxj|Zj|)2 to explain why they grow without bound as m→∞.

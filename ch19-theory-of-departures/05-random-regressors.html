19.5  Random regressors and a curved mean

When the regressors are random, as in almost every observational study, the linear model is read conditionally on X. Section 14.1 settled the case in which the conditional model is right. If 𝛃Y∣X∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I), every t and F procedure keeps its exact level unconditionally, whatever the distribution of the regressors (Theorem 14.1.2), and 𝛃β^ is unbiased with covariance σ2E[(X⊤X)−1] (Corollary 5.5.5). Nothing further needs to be proved about that case.

Random regressors create new problems only when the conditional model fails, and it can fail in two essentially different ways. The regressors can be correlated with the errors, as when they are measured with error or when a common cause of regressor and response is omitted. Then least squares does not even estimate a coefficient of interest, a problem taken up in Chapter 24 and Chapter 25. Or the conditional mean E(Y∣x) is not linear in x. This section treats the second case. Its message is that a curved mean, fitted by a linear model with random regressors, behaves like heteroscedastic error, and the usual standard errors are wrong even when the true errors are homoscedastic.

19.5.1. What least squares estimates, conditionally

Proposition 19.5.1 (The design-dependent target)

Let (X,Y) be random with rank(X)=p<n almost surely, 𝛍E(Y∣X)=μ(X) for some function 𝛍μ with values in Rn, not necessarily in C(X), and Cov(Y∣X)=σ2In. Put 𝛃𝛍βX=(X⊤X)−1X⊤μ(X).

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃E(β^∣X)=βX and 𝛃Cov(β^∣X)=σ2(X⊤X)−1.

  • (b) If the second moments below are finite, 𝛃𝛃Cov(β^)=σ2E[(X⊤X)−1]+Cov(βX).

  • (c) 𝛍E(s2∣X)=σ2+‖(I−M)μ(X)‖2/(n−p).

Proof.

  • (a) Given X, apply Proposition 5.5.7 with 𝛅𝛍𝛃δ=μ(X)−XβX, which satisfies 𝛅X⊤δ=0, and Theorem 5.5.1(b). (b) is the law of total covariance (Proposition 2.5.4) applied to (a). (c) is Eq. 2.3.1 given X.

Given the design, 𝛃β^ scatters around 𝛃βX exactly as the usual formula says. But 𝛃βX, the least squares coefficient of the true mean over the design drawn, changes from design to design. When 𝛍𝛃μ(X)=Xβ it is constant (Corollary 5.5.5). Otherwise (b) adds a between-design component that no conditional calculation can see, while (c) inflates s2 by the misfit, which compensates only partly.

19.5.2. The large-sample picture

The natural fixed target is the population projection of Section 6.11, 𝛃β∗=Σ−1E(xY), which least squares estimates consistently whatever the form of the mean (Proposition 6.11.3). Its sampling distribution is the following.

Theorem 19.5.2 (Least squares for the population projection)

Let (xi,Yi), i=1,…,n, be independent copies of (x,Y), where x∈Rp (whose first component may be the constant 1) and Y have finite fourth moments and Σ=E(xx⊤) is positive definite. Let 𝛃β∗=Σ−1E(xY), 𝛃U=Y−x⊤β∗ and Ω=E(U2xx⊤). Then E(xU)=0, and as n→∞:

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃n(β^−β∗)→Np(0,Σ−1ΩΣ−1) in distribution;

  • (b) s2→E(U2) and ns2(X⊤X)−1→E(U2)Σ−1 in probability;

  • (c) if E(U2∣x)=E(U2) almost surely, the two limiting covariances agree. Otherwise the usual interval for 𝛃a⊤β∗ of nominal level 1−α has coverage tending to 2Φ(zα/2ρa)−1, with ρa=E(U2)a⊤Σ−1a/a⊤Σ−1ΩΣ−1a.

Proof. 𝛃E(xU)=E(xY)−Σβ∗=0. Write 𝛃U=Y−Xβ∗, so that 𝛃𝛃n(β^−β∗)=(X⊤Xn)−1X⊤Un. By the weak law of large numbers X⊤X/n=n−1∑ixixi⊤→Σ in probability, so it is invertible with probability tending to one and its inverse tends to Σ−1, inversion being continuous at Σ. The vectors xiUi are independent with mean 0 and covariance Ω, which is finite because |E(U2xjxk)|≤(EU4Exj2xk2)1/2 and U has a finite fourth moment. By the central limit theorem X⊤U/n=n⋅n−1∑ixiUi→Np(0,Ω). If a random matrix An tends in probability to a constant A and a random vector Tn tends in distribution to T, then AnTn→AT: the difference (An−A)Tn tends to zero in probability because Tn is bounded in probability, ATn→AT by continuous mapping, and Slutsky’s lemma combines the two. This proves (a).

  • (b) (n−p)s2=U⊤(I−M)U=U⊤U−Qn, where Qn=(X⊤U/n)⊤(X⊤X/n)−1(X⊤U/n) converges in distribution by the argument for (a) and continuous mapping. Hence Qn/n→0 in probability, while U⊤U/n→E(U2) by the law of large numbers. Then ns2(X⊤X)−1=s2(X⊤X/n)−1→E(U2)Σ−1.

  • (c) If E(U2∣x)=c, then Ω=E[E(U2∣x)xx⊤]=cΣ, with c=E(U2), and Σ−1ΩΣ−1=cΣ−1. In general, the t statistic for 𝛃a⊤β∗ is 𝛃𝛃na⊤(β^−β∗) divided by (ns2a⊤(X⊤X)−1a)1/2. By (a), (b) and Slutsky’s lemma it tends to N(0,1/ρa), and the coverage follows as in Theorem 19.3.2(c).

The condition in (c) holds when the mean is linear and the errors are homoscedastic. It typically fails when either assumption fails (Exercise (B2) gives an exception), and a curved mean alone is enough. If E(Y∣x)=m(x) and Var(Y∣x)=σ2, then 𝛃U=(m(x)−x⊤β∗)+ε and 𝛃E(U2∣x)=(m(x)−x⊤β∗)2+σ2, which varies with x wherever the linear approximation is poor, exactly like a heteroscedastic error. The correct covariance Σ−1ΩΣ−1 is a population version of the sandwich Eq. 5.5.3, and Chapter 21 shows how to estimate it.

Example (A line through a parabola)

Let X be uniform on (0,2), E(Y∣X)=X2 and Var(Y∣X)=0.04, with normal errors. A straight line is fitted. The population line has slope β∗=Cov(X,X2)/Var(X)=2 and intercept −2/3, and the approximation error is U02−1/3 with U0=X−1 uniform on (−1,1). The slope’s component of Σ−1ΩΣ−1 is E[(X−1)2U2]/Var(X)2 (Exercise (A1)), which equals 9(44/945+0.04/3)=0.5390. The usual formula estimates E(U2)/Var(X)=3(4/45+0.04)=0.3867 instead. The true standard error is 1.181 times what the usual one estimates, and by Theorem 19.5.2(c) the nominal 95% interval for β∗ has limiting coverage 0.903.

Simulation agrees. With 40000 samples the interval covered β∗=2 in 0.896 of samples of size 50 and 0.900 of size 400; with 10000 samples of size 1600, in 0.908. Over the same samples, n times the variance of the slope was 0.584, 0.546 and 0.530, approaching the sandwich value 0.5390, not the usual 0.3867. As a statement about the design-dependent target 𝛃βX, the same intervals are conservative. They covered it in 0.999 of the samples of size 50, because s2 estimates E(U2)=0.1289 rather than σ2=0.04. In a larger simulation of 200000 samples of size 50, the variance of the slope, 0.01163, splits as in Proposition 19.5.1(b) into 0.00249 from the errors and 0.00913 from the variation of βX between designs.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from fractions import Fraction as Fr
sigma2 = Fr(1, 25)                                   # sigma = 0.2
EU2, EU4, EU6 = Fr(1, 3), Fr(1, 5), Fr(1, 7)         # moments of U = X - 1 ~ Uniform(-1, 1)
Eu2 = EU4 - Fr(2, 3) * EU2 + Fr(1, 9) + sigma2       # u = U^2 - 1/3 + error
sandwich = (EU6 - Fr(2, 3) * EU4 + Fr(1, 9) * EU2 + sigma2 * EU2) / EU2**2
usual = Eu2 / EU2
print("n Var(slope): sandwich", float(sandwich), " usual", float(usual))
z = stats.norm.ppf(0.975)
print("limiting coverage of the usual interval:",
      2 * stats.norm.cdf(z * np.sqrt(float(usual / sandwich))) - 1)
def one_run(n, reps, rng):
    x = rng.uniform(0, 2, size=(reps, n))
    y = x**2 + 0.2 * rng.normal(size=(reps, n))
    xc = x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    Sxx = np.sum(xc**2, axis=1)
    b = np.sum(xc * y, axis=1) / Sxx                          # least squares slope
    b_X = np.sum(xc * x**2, axis=1) / Sxx                     # its conditional mean given X
    a = y.mean(axis=1) - b * x.mean(axis=1)
    s2 = np.sum((y - a[:, None] - b[:, None] * x) ** 2, axis=1) / (n - 2)
    half = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 2) * np.sqrt(s2 / Sxx)
    return np.mean(np.abs(b - 2.0) <= half), np.mean(np.abs(b - b_X) <= half), n * b.var()

rng = np.random.default_rng(1905)
for n in [50, 400]:
    c_star, c_X, nvar = one_run(n, 4000, rng)
    print(f"n = {n}: covers beta* {c_star:.3f}, covers beta_X {c_X:.3f}, n Var(slope) {nvar:.3f}")

Which target is right depends on the question: 𝛃β∗ summarizes the population, 𝛃βX this design. With a curved mean, neither describes E(Y∣x) itself.

19.5.3. Controlled regressors

One situation with random regressors is more benign than it looks. In a controlled experiment the analyst sets a nominal value xi, but the value actually delivered is xi+δi, with an error δi independent of xi. If the response follows β0+β1(xi+δi)+εi, then regressing on the nominal values gives Yi=β0+β1xi+(β1δi+εi), a correct linear model with a larger error variance (Exercise (B1)). Least squares on the nominal settings is unbiased, as Berkson (1950) pointed out. Contrast the classical measurement error model, in which the true value is fixed and the recorded value carries the error. There the error is correlated with the recorded regressor, and least squares is biased towards zero (Theorem 24.1.1).

Rounding can fall on either side. If the grid value is what was set, as with a dial, the model is Berkson’s. If a continuous true value is recorded rounded to a grid of width w, the rounding error is roughly uniform with variance w2/12 and roughly independent of the true value, so the error is classical. In simple regression, classical error of variance τ2 multiplies the slope by Var(x)/(Var(x)+τ2) (Chapter 24). So measurement error, rounding included, can be ignored when its variance is a small fraction of Var(x).

Idea. With random regressors, what matters is whether the conditional model holds. If it does, nothing changes; if the mean is curved, s2(X⊤X)−1 is not the covariance.

19.5.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

With x=(1,X)⊤, show that the slope entries of Σ−1ΩΣ−1 and E(U2)Σ−1 are E[(X−μX)2U2]/Var(X)2 and E(U2)/Var(X).

Solution. Σ=(1μXμXEX2) has determinant Var(X), and the second row of Σ−1 is (−μX,1)/Var(X). So e2⊤Σ−1x=(X−μX)/Var(X), and e2⊤Σ−1ΩΣ−1e2=E[U2(X−μX)2]/Var(X)2. Similarly [Σ−1]22=1/Var(X).

Exercise (A2)

In Proposition 19.5.1, show that 𝛃Cov(βX)=0 when 𝛍𝛃μ(X)=Xβ, and give an example of a nonlinear 𝛍μ for which 𝛃Cov(βX)=0 anyway.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In the controlled-regressor model, let δi and εi be independent, with variances τ2 and σ2, independent of the settings. Show that least squares on the nominal xi is unbiased for (β0,β1), that the usual standard errors are valid with error variance σ2+β12τ2, and that s2 estimates this variance.

Solution. The composite errors ηi=β1δi+εi are independent of the fixed settings, with mean 0 and variance σ2+β12τ2, and uncorrelated across i. The model Yi=β0+β1xi+ηi therefore satisfies the second-moment assumptions of Chapter 5, so Theorem 5.5.1 and Theorem 5.6.1 apply with σ2 replaced by σ2+β12τ2. If δi and εi are normal, so is ηi, and the exact theory of Part III applies too.

Exercise (B2)

In Theorem 19.5.2, show that Σ−1ΩΣ−1=E(U2)Σ−1 iff E[(E(U2∣x)−E(U2))xx⊤]=0. Give an example of a nonconstant E(U2∣x) for which the usual standard errors are nevertheless consistent.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In Theorem 19.5.2, let E(Y∣x)=m(x), Var(Y∣x)=σ2, and put 𝛃d(x)=m(x)−x⊤β∗. Show that 𝛃𝛃n(βX−β∗)→Np(0,Σ−1E(d2xx⊤)Σ−1), and that Σ−1ΩΣ−1=σ2Σ−1+Σ−1E(d2xx⊤)Σ−1. Interpret the two terms in the light of Proposition 19.5.1(b).

Solution. 𝛃𝛃βX−β∗=(X⊤X)−1X⊤d with d=(d(x1),…,d(xn))⊤, and E(xd(x))=E(xU)=0 because E(U∣x)=d(x). The argument of Theorem 19.5.2(a) with U replaced by d(x) gives the first limit. Since U=d(x)+ε with E(ε∣x)=0, E(U2∣x)=d(x)2+σ2, and Ω=σ2Σ+E(d2xx⊤). The first term, σ2Σ−1, is the asymptotic version of nσ2E[(X⊤X)−1], the within-design part; the second is the asymptotic version of 𝛃nCov(βX), the between-design part. (They are limits of those moments only under uniform integrability, and for some designs the finite-sample moments do not even exist.)

19.2  An incorrect covariance matrix

Suppose the mean is right but the errors are correlated or have unequal variances. Write

𝛃E(Y)=Xβ,Cov(Y)=σ2V,
(19.2.1)

with V positive definite and X of full rank p<n. The analyst fits ordinary least squares and reports s2(X⊤X)−1 as if V=I. Two things go wrong: 𝛃β^ is no longer the best linear unbiased estimator (Corollary 7.2.3), and s2(X⊤X)−1 no longer estimates its covariance. The second failure is usually much more serious.

19.2.1. What happens to the estimator and its standard error

The bounds below need a fact about traces of projections, a special case of an inequality of Ky Fan.

Lemma 19.2.1 (Trace of a compressed matrix)

Let A be symmetric with eigenvalues λ1≥⋯≥λn, and let P be a symmetric idempotent matrix of rank k. Then ∑i=n−k+1nλi ≤ tr(PA) ≤ ∑i=1kλi.

Proof. By Theorem 1.7.2, A=∑iλiqiqi⊤ with orthonormal qi. So tr(PA)=∑iλiwi with wi=qi⊤Pqi=‖Pqi‖2. Each wi lies in [0,1] because the eigenvalues of P are 0 or 1 (Proposition 6.3.4), so 0≤qi⊤Pqi≤qi⊤qi=1, and ∑iwi=tr(P∑iqiqi⊤)=trP=k. Then ∑iλiwi−∑i≤kλi=∑i≤kλi(wi−1)+∑i>kλiwi≤λk∑i≤k(wi−1)+λk∑i>kwi=λk(∑iwi−k)=0, since wi−1≤0 and λi≥λk for i≤k, while wi≥0 and λi≤λk for i>k. The lower bound follows by applying this to −A.

Theorem 19.2.2 (Least squares under a wrong covariance)

Under Eq. 19.2.1, let a∈Rp be nonzero and let v=s2a⊤(X⊤X)−1a be the usual estimate of 𝛃Var(a⊤β^).

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃E(β^)=β and 𝛃Cov(β^)=σ2(X⊤X)−1X⊤VX(X⊤X)−1.

  • (b) E(s2)=σ2tr((I−M)V)/(n−p).

  • (c) With u=X(X⊤X)−1a/‖X(X⊤X)−1a‖, a unit vector in C(X),

    𝛃E(v)Var(a⊤β^)=tr((I−M)V)/(n−p)u⊤Vu.
    (19.2.2)

  • (d) Let λ1≥⋯≥λn>0 be the eigenvalues of V, let λ¯− be the mean of the n−p smallest and λ¯+ the mean of the n−p largest. Then 𝛃λ¯−λ1≤E(v)Var(a⊤β^)≤λ¯+λn.

Proof.

  • (a) is Proposition 5.5.7(b) with Σ=σ2V. (b) is Theorem 2.3.1 with the mean term vanishing, since 𝛃(I−M)Xβ=0. (c) Put c=X(X⊤X)−1a. By (a), 𝛃Var(a⊤β^)=σ2c⊤Vc, and c⊤c=a⊤(X⊤X)−1a. By (b), E(v)=σ2c⊤ctr((I−M)V)/(n−p). Divide, and write u=c/‖c‖.
  • (b) I−M is symmetric idempotent of rank n−p, so by Lemma 19.2.1 the numerator of Eq. 19.2.2 lies in [λ¯−,λ¯+]. By Theorem 1.7.7 the denominator lies in [λn,λ1].

Part (c) shows what the usual standard error gets wrong. The numerator of Eq. 19.2.2 averages V over the residual space, which is what s2 measures. The denominator evaluates V in the one direction u that 𝛃a⊤β^ uses. When the errors vary more along u than on average, the usual standard error is too small. The bounds in (d), due to Swindel (1968), hold for every design and linear function and can be attained (Exercise (C1)). They are typically wide.

19.2.2. When ordinary least squares loses little

Generalized least squares has variance σ2a⊤(X⊤V−1X)−1a for 𝛃a⊤β (Corollary 7.2.3). How much larger can the variance of 𝛃a⊤β^ be? The answer again depends only on the extreme eigenvalues of V.

Theorem 19.2.3 (Efficiency of least squares)

Under Eq. 19.2.1, with λ1 and λn the largest and smallest eigenvalues of V, a⊤(X⊤V−1X)−1aa⊤(X⊤X)−1X⊤VX(X⊤X)−1a ≥ 4λ1λn(λ1+λn)2 for every a≠0. The bound is attained for p=1 and X=x=(q1+qn)/2, where q1,qn are unit eigenvectors for λ1,λn.

Proof. Write X=QR with Q having orthonormal columns and R nonsingular (Theorem 1.5.2), and put b=R−⊤a, A=Q⊤VQ and B=Q⊤V−1Q, both positive definite. Then (X⊤X)−1X⊤VX(X⊤X)−1=R−1AR−⊤ and (X⊤V−1X)−1=R−1B−1R−⊤, so the ratio is b⊤B−1b/b⊤Ab. It suffices to show A≤κB−1 in the nonnegative definite order, with κ=(λ1+λn)2/(4λ1λn).

Every eigenvalue λ of V satisfies (λ−λ1)(λ−λn)≤0, that is, λ+λ1λn/λ≤λ1+λn. By the spectral decomposition this says V+λ1λnV−1≤(λ1+λn)I. Multiplying by Q⊤ on the left and Q on the right preserves the order, so A≤(λ1+λn)I−λ1λnB. Now let μ>0 be any eigenvalue of B. By the inequality between arithmetic and geometric means, κ/μ+λ1λnμ≥2κλ1λn=λ1+λn. Applied along the eigenvectors of B, this gives (λ1+λn)I−λ1λnB≤κB−1, and hence A≤κB−1.

For the attainment, with x=(q1+qn)/2 we have x⊤x=1, x⊤Vx=(λ1+λn)/2 and x⊤V−1x=(1/λ1+1/λn)/2. The ratio is 1/((x⊤Vx)(x⊤V−1x))=4λ1λn/(λ1+λn)2.

The scalar inequality behind the proof, (x⊤Vx)(x⊤V−1x)≤κ(x⊤x)2, is Kantorovich’s inequality, and the proof above is a matrix form of it. Bloomfield and Watson (1975) and Knott (1975) found the corresponding sharp bound when efficiency is measured by the determinant of the whole covariance matrix.

The bound is a worst case over all designs. For particular designs ordinary least squares can be fully efficient. Kruskal’s theorem (Theorem 6.9.3) says that 𝛃β^ equals the generalized least squares estimate for every Y iff C(VX)⊆C(X), and then the efficiency is one. Even then the standard errors may be wrong.

Example (Equicorrelated errors)

Let V=(1−ρ)I+ρ11⊤, with −1/(n−1)<ρ<1 so that V is positive definite, and let the model contain an intercept, X=[1,X1]. It arises when all observations share one random shock. Since VX=(1−ρ)X+ρ1(1⊤X) has columns in C(X), Kruskal’s condition holds and 𝛃β^ is the best linear unbiased estimator. Its covariance is not σ2(X⊤X)−1, however. Because Xe1=1 for the first standard basis vector e1, we have (X⊤X)−1X⊤1=e1, and Theorem 19.2.2(a) gives 𝛃Cov(β^)=σ2(1−ρ)(X⊤X)−1+σ2ρe1e1⊤. By Exercise (B2), E(s2)=σ2(1−ρ). So s2(X⊤X)−1 is exactly unbiased for the covariance of the slopes, whose information comes from comparisons in which the common shock cancels. For the intercept it misses the term σ2ρ. When ρ>0 this term does not shrink as n grows, so the intercept is not even consistent, and its reported standard error goes to zero anyway (Exercise (B2)). The same holds for any fitted mean 𝛃x0⊤β^, since x0⊤e1=1 whenever x0 has a leading 1.

19.2.3. Autocorrelated errors

Example (A trend with autocorrelated errors)

Take n=20 equally spaced times and errors with vst=ρ|s−t|, the covariance of a stationary first-order autoregression, with ρ=0.6. Fit a straight line in two designs, a linear trend xt=t and an alternating regressor xt=(−1)t. For the trend, the expected usual variance of the slope is 0.256 times its true variance, so the root of the expected usual variance is 0.506 of the true standard error. Yet ordinary least squares is nearly efficient: generalized least squares has 0.905 of its variance. For the alternating regressor the error goes the other way. The ratio is 3.271, so the standard error is too large by a factor 1.809, and the efficiency is 0.950. Swindel’s bounds for this V are 0.193 and 4.31, so both designs lie well towards the extremes. The Kantorovich bound, 0.2351 from λ1=3.757 and λn=0.2514, is far below either efficiency.

In 100000 simulated series with normal errors, the nominal 95% t interval for the slope covered the true value in 0.693 of them for the trend and 0.9976 for the alternating regressor. Figure 19.2.1 shows the whole range of ρ.

import numpy as np
n = 20
t = np.arange(1, n + 1)
designs = {"trend": t.astype(float), "alternating": (-1.0) ** t}

def ar1(rho):
    return rho ** np.abs(t[:, None] - t[None, :])

def slope_summary(x, V):
    """True variance, mean of the usual estimate, GLS variance of the slope (sigma = 1)."""
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])
    G = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
    A = G @ X.T                                     # beta_hat = A y
    true_var = (A @ V @ A.T)[1, 1]                  # the sandwich
    M = X @ A
    Es2 = np.trace((np.eye(n) - M) @ V) / (n - 2)   # E(s^2)
    usual = Es2 * G[1, 1]                           # E of s^2 [(X'X)^{-1}]_{11}
    gls = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ np.linalg.solve(V, X))[1, 1]
    return true_var, usual, gls

rho = 0.6
for name, x in designs.items():
    true_var, usual, gls = slope_summary(x, ar1(rho))
    print(f"{name:12s} usual/true = {usual / true_var:.3f}   GLS/OLS variance = {gls / true_var:.3f}")
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(1902)
reps = 100_000
L = np.linalg.cholesky(ar1(rho))
E = rng.normal(size=(reps, n)) @ L.T              # rows are AR(1) error vectors
tq = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 2)
for name, x in designs.items():
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])
    G = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
    B = E @ (G @ X.T).T                             # beta_hat - beta, one row per data set
    s2 = np.sum((E - B @ X.T) ** 2, axis=1) / (n - 2)
    cover = np.mean(np.abs(B[:, 1]) <= tq * np.sqrt(s2 * G[1, 1]))
    print(f"{name:12s} coverage of the nominal 95% interval: {cover:.4f}")

Eq. 19.2.2 explains the pattern. A smooth regressor puts u along slowly varying directions, where positively correlated errors have their largest variance; an alternating one puts it where positive correlation cancels. Most time-series regressors are smooth, which is why positive autocorrelation usually makes ordinary standard errors too small.

Idea. A wrong covariance matrix is mainly a problem for standard errors, not estimates: s2(X⊤X)−1 can be wrong by a large factor in either direction, and nothing in the fitted model reveals this. Chapter 21 shows how to estimate the correct covariance Eq. 5.5.3 without knowing V, and how to detect serial correlation. Chapter 31 treats generalized least squares when V is known up to a few parameters.

19.2.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let V=diag(v1,…,vn). Show that E(s2)=σ2∑i(1−hii)vi/(n−p), a weighted average of the variances σ2vi.

Exercise (A2)

Show that the bounds of Theorem 19.2.2(d) do not change when V is replaced by cV for c>0, and that both equal 1 iff V is a multiple of I.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

For the regression through the origin E(Yi)=βxi with Var(Yi)=σ2vi, show that Var(β^)=σ2∑ihivi/∑ixi2 and E(v)=σ2∑i(1−hi)vi/((n−1)∑ixi2), where hi=xi2/∑jxj2 are the leverages. Conclude that the usual standard error is too small on average iff the leverage-weighted average of the vi exceeds the (1−hi)-weighted average.

Solution. β^=∑ixiYi/S with S=∑jxj2, so Var(β^)=σ2∑ixi2vi/S2=σ2∑ihivi/S. Exercise (A1) with p=1 gives E(s2), and v=s2/S. The weights hi sum to 1, and so do (1−hi)/(n−1). The usual variance is too small on average iff ∑ihivi>∑i(1−hi)vi/(n−1). In particular, when the error variance grows with |xi|, the points that dominate β^ are the noisiest, and the usual standard error understates the truth.

Exercise (B2)

In Example (Equicorrelated errors) with ρ>0, show that Var(β^0)≥σ2ρ for every n, while E(v)→0 for the intercept when [(X⊤X)−1]11→0. Explain the result by writing εi=ρσZ+1−ρσUi with independent standardized Z,U1,…,Un.

Solution. From the covariance in the example, Var(β^0)=σ2(1−ρ)[(X⊤X)−1]11+σ2ρ≥σ2ρ, while E(v)=σ2(1−ρ)[(X⊤X)−1]11. The representation has covariance σ2ρ11⊤+σ2(1−ρ)I=σ2V. The shared term ρσZ1 lies in C(X) and is absorbed entirely by the intercept, since (X⊤X)−1X⊤1=e1. It never averages out, and it leaves no trace in the residuals, so s2 cannot see it.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that both bounds of Theorem 19.2.2(d) are attained. Hint: take C(X) spanned by eigenvectors of V, and choose a so that u is one of them.

Solution. Let q1,…,qn be orthonormal eigenvectors for λ1≥⋯≥λn. If X=[q1,…,qp], then I−M=∑i>pqiqi⊤ and tr((I−M)V)=∑i>pλi=(n−p)λ¯−. With a=e1, u=q1 and u⊤Vu=λ1, so the ratio Eq. 19.2.2 equals λ¯−/λ1. Symmetrically, X=[qn−p+1,…,qn] and u=qn give λ¯+/λn.

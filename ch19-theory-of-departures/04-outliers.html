19.4  A single bad observation

Some failures affect a single case, such as a misplaced decimal point. How much one bad case matters depends on its leverage and on how far its response is from the rest of the data. This section makes that exact; Chapter 20 turns it into diagnostics.

19.4.1. The effect of contaminating one response

Theorem 19.4.1 (One contaminated response)

Let X have full column rank. For a data vector y, a case k and a shift Δ, let yΔ=y+Δek, where ek is the kth standard basis vector.

  • (a) The estimate moves by 𝛃𝛃β^(yΔ)−β^(y)=Δ(X⊤X)−1x(k). The fitted vector moves by ΔMek, a vector of length |Δ|hkk. The fitted value of case k moves by Δhkk, and its residual by only Δ(1−hkk).

  • (b) If 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ and Cov(Y)=σ2I, then 𝛃β^(YΔ) has mean 𝛃β+Δ(X⊤X)−1x(k), and the residual sum of squares has mean σ2(n−p)+Δ2(1−hkk).

  • (c) Suppose hkk<1, and let 𝛃β^(k) be the estimate without case k. Then for every y,

    𝛃𝛃‖X(β^−β^(k))‖2=hkk1−hkk⋅ε^k21−hkk.
    (19.4.1)

  • (d) Under the conditions of (c), adding ek to the model matrix as an extra column gives the coefficients 𝛃β^(k) for X and 𝛃δ^=yk−x(k)⊤β^(k)=ε^k/(1−hkk) for the new column.

Proof.

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃β^(yΔ)−β^(y)=Δ(X⊤X)−1X⊤ek=Δ(X⊤X)−1x(k). Multiplying by X gives ΔMek, with squared length Δ2ek⊤Mek=Δ2hkk, since M is symmetric and idempotent. Its kth entry is Δhkk, and the residual vector moves by Δ(I−M)ek, whose kth entry is Δ(1−hkk).
  • (b) The mean follows from (a) and Theorem 5.5.1(a). The residual vector (I−M)YΔ has mean Δ(I−M)ek, so Eq. 2.3.1 gives σ2(n−p)+Δ2‖(I−M)ek‖2=σ2(n−p)+Δ2(1−hkk).
  • (c) By Proposition 10.6.2(a), 𝛃𝛃β^−β^(k)=(X⊤X)−1x(k)ε^k/(1−hkk), and ‖X(X⊤X)−1x(k)‖2=x(k)⊤(X⊤X)−1x(k)=hkk.
  • (d) With the extra column, the criterion is ∑i≠k(yi−x(i)⊤b)2+(yk−x(k)⊤b−δ)2. For any b the choice δ=yk−x(k)⊤b makes the last term zero, so the minimizing b minimizes the first sum alone, which is 𝛃β^(k). The augmented matrix has full rank because hkk<1 means ek∉C(X) (Proposition 6.8.1). Then 𝛃δ^=yk−x(k)⊤β^(k), which is ε^k/(1−hkk) by Proposition 10.6.2(b).

Part (a), which needs no model, says that a high-leverage case hides its own contamination: of a shift Δ, the fraction hkk goes into its fitted value and only 1−hkk shows in its residual. Raw residuals therefore miss the cases that matter most, and Chapter 20 rescales them.

Part (c) makes “influence is leverage times residual” exact: a leverage factor hkk/(1−hkk) times the squared deleted residual ε^k2/(1−hkk). Either factor alone is harmless. Divided by ps2, Eq. 19.4.1 is Cook’s distance (Definition 20.4.1). Part (d) is the mean-shift outlier model: giving case k its own parameter is the same as deleting it. Part (b) adds a second cost: s2 is inflated by about Δ2(1−hkk)/(n−p), which weakens every test, even for coefficients the bad case hardly moves.

Example (The last day of the stack loss data)

In the stack loss data of Example (Stack loss), n=21 days of a plant oxidizing ammonia, regress the stack loss on air flow, water temperature and acid concentration with an intercept. The average leverage is p/n=0.190. By Eq. 19.4.1, the case whose deletion moves the fitted vector most is day 21, with leverage 0.285 and residual −7.238. Deleting it moves the fitted vector by a distance of 5.396. The next largest move, for day 1, is 2.543, and the median over all days is 1.228. The largest leverage, 0.412 for day 17, comes with a residual of only −1.520, and that day has little influence.

Without day 21, the air-flow coefficient rises from 0.716 to 0.889, the water temperature coefficient falls from 1.295 to 0.817, and s drops from 3.243 to 2.569. The mean-shift estimate for day 21 is δ^=−10.116. The listing computes every deletion effect from the single fit (Theorem 19.4.1(c)).

Adding Δ=10 to the response of the lowest-leverage day, day 5 with h55=0.052, moves the fitted vector by 2.285 and its own residual by 9.478. The same shift on day 17 moves the fitted vector by 6.420 and its residual by only 5.879.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.stackloss.load_pandas().data
y = data["STACKLOSS"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), data[["AIRFLOW", "WATERTEMP", "ACIDCONC"]].to_numpy()])
n, p = X.shape

G = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
beta_hat = G @ X.T @ y
e_hat = y - X @ beta_hat
h = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", X, G, X)             # leverages

# the change in beta_hat from deleting case k, without refitting
change = (G @ X.T) * (e_hat / (1 - h))            # column k is beta_hat - beta_hat_(k)
shift = h * e_hat**2 / (1 - h) ** 2               # ||X (beta_hat - beta_hat_(k))||^2
k = int(np.argmax(shift))
print("most influential case:", k + 1, " leverage", round(h[k], 3), " residual", round(e_hat[k], 3))
print("beta_hat           ", np.round(beta_hat, 3))
print("beta_hat without it", np.round(beta_hat - change[:, k], 3))
print("mean-shift estimate e_k/(1-h_kk):", round(e_hat[k] / (1 - h[k]), 3))
Delta = 10.0
for j in [int(np.argmin(h)), int(np.argmax(h))]:
    y_c = y.copy()
    y_c[j] += Delta                                        # contaminate one response
    b_c = G @ X.T @ y_c
    print(f"case {j + 1:2d}: h = {h[j]:.3f}, ||X(b_c - b)|| = {np.linalg.norm(X @ (b_c - beta_hat)):.3f},"
          f" |Delta| sqrt(h) = {Delta * np.sqrt(h[j]):.3f}")

19.4.2. Breakdown

By Theorem 19.4.1(a) the effect of one case grows without bound with Δ. Contaminated regressors can be as bad: in panel (b) of Figure 19.4.1 a single case moved far out in x takes control of the slope. The following definition, from Donoho and Huber (1983), makes this precise.

Definition 19.4.1 (Breakdown point)

Let T be an estimator that maps a data set Z of n cases (x(i),yi) to a vector in Rp. For m≤n, let b(m;T,Z) be the supremum of ‖T(Z′)−T(Z)‖ over all data sets Z′ obtained from Z by replacing any m of its cases with arbitrary values, for which T(Z′) is defined. The finite-sample breakdown point of T at Z is ε∗(T,Z)=min{mn: b(m;T,Z)=∞}.

Proposition 19.4.2 (Least squares breaks down at one case)

For least squares and every data set with a full-rank model matrix, ε∗=1/n.

Proof. Since X has full rank, some row x(k) is nonzero, and then (X⊤X)−1x(k)≠0. Replace case k by (x(k),yk+Δ). The model matrix is unchanged, so the estimate is defined, and by Theorem 19.4.1(a) it moves by |Δ|‖(X⊤X)−1x(k)‖, which is unbounded in Δ. So b(1)=∞. No smaller fraction than 1/n is possible.

So least squares offers no protection against gross errors. The sample median, by contrast, has breakdown point close to one half (Exercise (A1)). High-breakdown regression estimators exist too, at a cost in efficiency discussed with robust fitting in Section 20.6. Hampel (1974) introduced the related influence function, the effect of an infinitesimal contamination at a point (x0,y0). For least squares it is unbounded in both x0 and y0 (Exercise (C1)).

Idea. High leverage makes a case both more influential and better hidden, and one case can move the estimate arbitrarily far.

19.4.3. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that the sample median of n numbers has breakdown point ⌊(n+1)/2⌋/n, in the sense of Definition 19.4.1 with p=1 and no regressors.

Exercise (A2)

In Theorem 19.4.1(a), show that the residual of every other case i moves by −Δmik, where mik is an entry of M. Which cases’ residuals are most affected by a contaminated high-leverage case?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In a straight-line regression, move the x value of case k to t while keeping yk fixed. Show that the fitted slope tends to 0 as t→∞. If instead yk=ct+d moves with t, show that the slope tends to c.

Solution. Let x¯t and y¯ be the means with case k at t. The slope is ∑i(xi−x¯t)(yi−y¯)/∑i(xi−x¯t)2. As t→∞, t−x¯t=(1−1/n)t+O(1) while the other deviations xi−x¯t are −t/n+O(1). The denominator is (1−1/n)t2+O(t). With yk fixed, the numerator is O(t), since each term is a deviation of order t times a bounded yi−y¯, so the slope is O(1/t). With yk=ct+d, put ri=yi−cxi, so that rk=d and all the ri stay bounded. Since yi=cxi+ri and least squares is linear in the response, the slope is c plus the slope of r on x, which is O(1/t) as before. The far case dictates the slope.

Exercise (B2)

Suppose case k is contaminated by Δ, and consider the t statistic for a coefficient βj whose estimate is unaffected, [(X⊤X)−1x(k)]j=0. Using Theorem 19.4.1(b), explain how the contamination still changes the test, and show that its effect on E(s2) is largest for low-leverage cases.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let (x,Y) have a joint distribution F with Σ=E(xx⊤) positive definite, and let 𝛃β∗(F)=Σ−1E(xY) be the population least squares coefficient. For the mixture Fϵ=(1−ϵ)F+ϵδ(x0,y0), which puts mass ϵ at a single point, show that 𝛃𝛃ddϵβ∗(Fϵ)|ϵ=0=Σ−1x0(y0−x0⊤β∗(F)), and compare with Theorem 19.4.1(c) for a sample of size n.

Solution. Under Fϵ, Σϵ=(1−ϵ)Σ+ϵx0x0⊤ and mϵ=(1−ϵ)E(xY)+ϵx0y0, and 𝛃Σϵβ∗(Fϵ)=mϵ. Differentiate at ϵ=0, writing d for the derivative: 𝛃(x0x0⊤−Σ)β∗+Σd=x0y0−E(xY). Since 𝛃Σβ∗=E(xY), this gives 𝛃Σd=x0(y0−x0⊤β∗). The influence is the product of a leverage-like factor Σ−1x0 and a residual, unbounded in both. In a sample, replacing F by the empirical distribution and ϵ by 1/n gives n−1(X⊤X/n)−1x(k)ε^k, which is Proposition 10.6.2(a) without the factor 1/(1−hkk).

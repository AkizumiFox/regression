19.3  Non-normal errors

Normality entered Part III at one point only: it turned the moments of 𝛃β^ and s2 into exact distributions (Theorem 7.5.1). Everything that needs only two moments holds for any error distribution with finite variance. Inference about the mean structure survives in large samples, provided no single observation dominates the fit. Inference about σ2 does not survive at any sample size, and neither does prediction of a single new response.

Throughout, the errors ε1,…,εn are independent and identically distributed with mean 0 and variance σ2∈(0,∞), from a distribution that does not change with n. Where fourth moments are needed, the excess kurtosis is κ=μ4/σ4−3, where μ4=E(εi4). It is zero for normal errors, 6 for centred exponential errors, −1.2 for uniform errors, and at least −2 for every distribution, since μ4≥σ4 by Jensen’s inequality.

19.3.1. Asymptotic normality of least squares

Large-sample statements concern a sequence of models 𝛃𝛆Yn=Xnβ+εn, n=1,2,…, with p fixed and Xn of rank p. We write hn∗=maxihii for the largest leverage of Xn. Besides the three limit theorems recalled in Section 14.4 (the central limit theorem, Slutsky’s lemma and the delta method), we need two more standard results of probability theory.

Remark (Two more limit theorems).

  • (i) Lindeberg–Feller. For each n let Zn1,…,Znn be independent with mean zero and ∑iVar(Zni)=1. If ∑iE[Zni21{|Zni|>η}]→0 for every η>0, then ∑iZni→N(0,1) in distribution (Billingsley 1995, section 27).

  • (ii) Cramér–Wold and continuous mapping. Random vectors Tn in Rq converge in distribution to T iff c⊤Tn→c⊤T for every c∈Rq, and then g(Tn)→g(T) for every continuous g (van der Vaart 1998, chapter 2).

Lemma 19.3.1 (Weighted sums of errors)

Let wn=(wn1,…,wnn)⊤ be constant vectors with ‖wn‖=1 and mn=maxi|wni|→0. Then σ−1∑iwniεi→N(0,1) in distribution.

Proof. Put Zni=wniεi/σ, so that ∑iVar(Zni)=∑iwni2=1. With U=ε1/σ, for every η>0, ∑iE[Zni21{|Zni|>η}]=∑iwni2E[U21{|wniU|>η}]≤E[U21{|U|>η/mn}], because |wni|≤mn and the weights wni2 sum to one. Since EU2=1 and η/mn→∞, the right side tends to zero by dominated convergence. The Lindeberg–Feller theorem applies.

Theorem 19.3.2 (Least squares with non-normal errors)

In the sequence of models above, suppose hn∗→0. Let s2=‖(I−M)Yn‖2/(n−p).

  • (a) For every sequence of nonzero vectors an∈Rp, 𝛃𝛃an⊤β^−an⊤βσan⊤(Xn⊤Xn)−1an ⟶ N(0,1)in distribution.

  • (b) s2→σ2 in probability. This part does not need hn∗→0.

  • (c) The t statistic, with s in place of σ in (a), tends to N(0,1), and the nominal 1−α interval 𝛃an⊤β^±tn−p,α/2san⊤(Xn⊤Xn)−1an has coverage tending to 1−α.

  • (d) Let Λ be a fixed p×q matrix of rank q, and suppose 𝛃Λ⊤β=d. The statistic 𝛃𝛃Fn=(Λ⊤β^−d)⊤[Λ⊤(Xn⊤Xn)−1Λ]−1(Λ⊤β^−d)qs2 satisfies qFn→χ2(q) in distribution, and Pr{Fn>Fα(q,n−p)}→α.

Proof.

  • (a) Put Gn=(Xn⊤Xn)−1 and τn2=an⊤Gnan. Since 𝛃𝛃𝛆β^−β=GnXn⊤εn, the numerator is ∑icniεi with cni=an⊤Gnx(i), and ∑icni2=an⊤GnXn⊤XnGnan=τn2. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the inner product ⟨u,v⟩=u⊤Gnv (Proposition 1.1.1 applied to Gn1/2an and Gn1/2x(i)) gives cni2≤(an⊤Gnan)(x(i)⊤Gnx(i))=τn2hii. So wni=cni/τn has unit length and maxi|wni|≤hn∗→0. Apply Lemma 19.3.1.

  • (b) 𝛆𝛆𝛆𝛆(n−p)s2=εn⊤εn−εn⊤Mεn, since 𝛃(I−M)Xnβ=0. By the weak law of large numbers, 𝛆𝛆εn⊤εn/n→σ2 in probability. The second term is nonnegative with 𝛆𝛆E(εn⊤Mεn)=σ2trM=σ2p (Theorem 2.3.1), so by Markov’s inequality 𝛆𝛆Pr(εn⊤Mεn/n>δ)≤σ2p/(nδ)→0. Since n/(n−p)→1, s2→σ2. Under a finite fourth moment this is Corollary 5.6.4; the argument here needs only a finite variance.

  • (c) The t statistic is the ratio in (a) multiplied by σ/s→1, so Slutsky’s lemma gives the limit. The quantile tn−p,α/2 tends to zα/2, because the t(ν) distribution tends to N(0,1) and the normal distribution function is continuous and strictly increasing. Hence |Tn|−tn−p,α/2→|Z|−zα/2, and the coverage tends to Pr(|Z|≤zα/2)=1−α.

  • (d) Let Wn=Λ⊤GnΛ, which is positive definite, and 𝛃𝛃Zn=Wn−1/2Λ⊤(β^−β)/σ. Under the hypothesis the numerator of Fn is σ2‖Zn‖2. For a unit vector c∈Rq, 𝛃𝛃c⊤Zn=an⊤(β^−β)/σ with an=ΛWn−1/2c, and an⊤Gnan=c⊤Wn−1/2WnWn−1/2c=1. By (a), c⊤Zn→N(0,1), which is the law of c⊤Z for Z∼Nq(0,I). By Cramér–Wold, Zn→Z, and by continuous mapping ‖Zn‖2→χ2(q). Then qFn=‖Zn‖2σ2/s2→χ2(q) by (b) and Slutsky’s lemma. Finally, qF(q,ν) is χ2(q) divided by an independent χ2(ν)/ν, which tends to one, so qFα(q,n−p) tends to the upper α point of χ2(q), and the rejection probability tends to α as in (c).

Part (a) allows an to depend on the design. The condition hn∗→0 says that no observation keeps a non-negligible share of the fit. For a straight line at x=1,…,n the largest leverage is below 4/n (Exercise (A1)). The condition is not a technicality, as the next example shows.

Example (An observation with its own parameter)

Fit an intercept together with an indicator of the first observation, Xn=[1,e1], so that h11=1 for every n. The least squares estimates are μ^=Y¯(1), the mean of observations 2,…,n, and δ^=Y1−Y¯(1). Hence δ^−δ=ε1−ε¯(1),Var(δ^)=σ2(1+1n−1), and since ε¯(1)→0 in probability, δ^−δ→ε1 in distribution. The limit is the error distribution itself. No amount of other data can average away the error of one observation.

19.3.2. How far from normal in a finite sample

Theorem 19.3.2 is a limit statement. A finite-sample measure of the approach comes from the third and fourth cumulants of the numerator, which leverage controls directly.

Proposition 19.3.3 (Skewness and kurtosis of a linear estimate)

Let the errors have finite fourth moment, skewness γ1=μ3/σ3 with μ3=E(εi3), and excess kurtosis κ. For a≠0 let 𝛃𝛃𝛃Z=(a⊤β^−a⊤β)/Var(a⊤β^), and let wi=ci/‖c‖ with c=X(X⊤X)−1a. Then E(Z3)=γ1∑iwi3,E(Z4)−3=κ∑iwi4, and |∑iwi3|≤h∗, ∑iwi4≤h∗, where h∗=maxihii.

Proof. Z=σ−1∑iwiεi with ∑iwi2=1. Expanding the cube, every term with some index appearing exactly once has mean zero, by independence and Eεi=0. Only the terms wi3εi3 remain, so E(Z3)=σ−3μ3∑iwi3. In the fourth power the surviving terms are wi4εi4 and, for i≠j, the 3 pairings of wi2εi2wj2εj2. So E(Z4)=μ4σ4∑iwi4+3∑i≠jwi2wj2=(κ+3)∑iwi4+3(1−∑iwi4), using ∑i≠jwi2wj2=(∑iwi2)2−∑iwi4. As in the proof of Theorem 19.3.2(a), wi2≤hii≤h∗. Hence |∑iwi3|≤maxi|wi|∑iwi2≤h∗ and ∑iwi4≤maxiwi2≤h∗.

So skewness is averaged away more slowly than heavy tails, although with symmetric weights it can cancel exactly (Exercise (B1)). The denominator s of the t statistic adds terms of the same order, so the proposition describes the leading effect only.

Example (Coverage of the t interval)

Fit a straight line with normal, centred exponential and scaled t(3) errors, each with variance one, in two designs. In the spread design x=1,…,n, and hn∗ is 0.345, 0.096 and 0.025 at n=10, 40 and 160. In the far-point design the last value is moved to x=4n, and hn∗ is 0.954, 0.800 and 0.485. With 100000 data sets per case, the coverages of the nominal 95% interval for the slope are:

Design Errors n=10 n=40 n=160
spread normal 0.950 0.951 0.950
spread exponential 0.955 0.953 0.950
spread t(3) 0.955 0.952 0.951
far point normal 0.951 0.950 0.951
far point exponential 0.933 0.948 0.950
far point t(3) 0.931 0.944 0.953

The two-sided coverages hide unbalanced tails. With exponential errors and the far point, the interval misses above the slope in 0.067 of the data sets at n=10 and below it in only 0.001. At n=160 the two proportions are 0.038 and 0.012, still far from 0.025 each, as the slowly falling leverage predicts. A one-sided test in this design has nearly three times its nominal size at n=10. Panel (a) of Figure 19.3.1 shows the skewed t statistic at n=40. The t(3) errors have no fourth moment, but Theorem 19.3.2 still applies.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def errors(law, size, rng):
    """Errors with mean 0 and variance 1."""
    if law == "normal":
        return rng.normal(size=size)
    if law == "exponential":                       # skewed: skewness 2, excess kurtosis 6
        return rng.exponential(size=size) - 1.0
    if law == "t3":                                # heavy tails: variance 3 before scaling
        return rng.standard_t(3, size=size) / np.sqrt(3.0)
    if law == "uniform":                           # short tails: excess kurtosis -1.2
        return rng.uniform(-np.sqrt(3), np.sqrt(3), size=size)

def design(kind, n):
    x = np.arange(1.0, n + 1)
    if kind == "far point":
        x[-1] = 4.0 * n                            # one point far to the right
    return np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])
def slope_coverage(kind, law, n, reps, rng):
    """Proportion of nominal 95% t intervals for the slope that cover it."""
    X = design(kind, n)
    G = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
    A = G @ X.T
    E = errors(law, (reps, n), rng)
    B = E @ A.T                                    # beta_hat - beta
    s2 = np.sum((E - B @ X.T) ** 2, axis=1) / (n - 2)
    T = B[:, 1] / np.sqrt(s2 * G[1, 1])
    tq = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 2)
    return np.mean(np.abs(T) <= tq), np.mean(T > tq), np.mean(T < -tq)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1903)
for kind in ["spread", "far point"]:
    for law in ["normal", "exponential", "t3"]:
        cov, up, lo = slope_coverage(kind, law, 10, 20_000, rng)
        print(f"{kind:9s} {law:11s} n=10: coverage {cov:.3f} (misses above {up:.3f}, below {lo:.3f})")

19.3.3. Inference about the error variance

The interval for σ2 (Proposition 12.1.4) rests on SSE, a sum of squared errors, whose variance depends on the kurtosis (Proposition 5.6.3, Proposition 7.5.4). A central limit theorem applies, but with the wrong variance.

Proposition 19.3.4 (The interval for sigma squared)

In the sequence of models above, let the errors have finite fourth moment with κ>−2. Then n(s2/σ2−1)→N(0,2+κ), and the coverage of the nominal 1−α interval of Proposition 12.1.4 tends to

2Φ(zα/22/(2+κ))−1,
(19.3.1)

where Φ is the standard normal distribution function. The limit equals 1−α iff κ=0.

Proof. As in the proof of Theorem 19.3.2(b), 𝛆𝛆𝛆𝛆n(s2−σ2)=nn−pn(εn⊤εnn−σ2)+npσ2n−p−nn−pεn⊤Mεn. The εi2 are independent with mean σ2 and variance μ4−σ4=σ4(2+κ), so by the central limit theorem the first term tends to N(0,σ4(2+κ)). The second term tends to zero, and so does the third in probability, because 𝛆𝛆E(εn⊤Mεn)=σ2p and Markov’s inequality applies to 𝛆𝛆εn⊤Mεn/n. Slutsky’s lemma gives the first claim.

Write ν=n−p and let cν− and cν+ be the lower and upper α/2 points of χ2(ν). The interval covers σ2 iff cν−≤νs2/σ2≤cν+, that is, iff cν−−νν ≤ ν(s2σ2−1) ≤ cν+−νν. A χ2(ν) variable is a sum of ν independent squared standard normals, each with variance 2, so by the central limit theorem (cν±−ν)/2ν→±zα/2, and the bounds tend to ±zα/22. The middle term tends to N(0,2+κ) because ν/n→1. The coverage therefore tends to Pr(|Z|2+κ≤zα/22), which is Eq. 19.3.1.

For centred exponential errors, κ=6, the nominal 95% interval has limiting coverage 2Φ(1.96/2)−1=0.673. For uniform errors, κ=−1.2, it tends to 0.998. Panel (b) of Figure 19.3.1 shows simulated coverages with the spread design. For exponential errors they are 0.786 at n=10, 0.717 at n=40 and 0.672 at n=2560: more data make it worse. This answers Exercise (B3) and explains the failure reported in Section 12.1.

def sigma2_coverage(law, n, reps, rng):
    X = design("spread", n)
    E = errors(law, (reps, n), rng)
    B = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ E.T).T
    sse = np.sum((E - B @ X.T) ** 2, axis=1)     # sigma^2 = 1
    lo, hi = stats.chi2.ppf([0.025, 0.975], n - 2)
    return np.mean((sse >= lo) & (sse <= hi))

def limit(kappa, z=stats.norm.ppf(0.975)):
    """Limiting coverage of the nominal 95% chi-squared interval."""
    return 2 * stats.norm.cdf(z * np.sqrt(2 / (2 + kappa))) - 1

print("limits:", {k: round(limit(k), 3) for k in [0, 6, -1.2]})

Prediction intervals fail for the same reason: they must cover a single new error, which no averaging acts on (Proposition 12.3.3).

19.3.4. Why the F test is protected and the variance-ratio test is not

The F statistic is a ratio of two quadratic forms, each with a kurtosis-dependent variance. The effects can cancel, as Box and Watson (1962) and Atiqullah (1962) found. Since log⁡F is approximately the difference of the two mean squares divided by σ2, it suffices to study that difference.

Proposition 19.3.5 (Kurtosis and a ratio of mean squares)

Let P1 and P2 be symmetric idempotent with P1P2=0, ranks f1 and f2, and 𝛃𝛃P1Xβ=P2Xβ=0. Let the errors have finite fourth moment and excess kurtosis κ, put Sj2=Y⊤PjY/fj, and let pj be the vector of diagonal entries of Pj. Then L=(S12−S22)/σ2 has mean zero and Var(L)=2f1+2f2+κ‖d‖2,d=p1f1−p2f2. In particular, if both P1 and P2 have constant diagonals, then d=0 and Var(L) is the same as under normality.

Proof. Since 𝛆PjY=Pjε, 𝛆𝛆L=ε⊤Aε/σ2 with A=P1/f1−P2/f2, whose diagonal is d. By Theorem 2.3.1, E(L)=trA=1−1=0. By Theorem 2.3.3 with zero means, 𝛆𝛆Var(ε⊤Aε)=(μ4−3σ4)d⊤d+2σ4tr(A2), and tr(A2)=trP1/f12+trP2/f22−2tr(P1P2)/(f1f2)=1/f1+1/f2. Divide by σ4. If the diagonals are constant, pj=(fj/n)1 because trPj=fj, and d=0.

For the F test of a linear hypothesis, P1=M−M0 and P2=I−M. Designs in which both have constant diagonals are called quadratically balanced. Every balanced one-way or complete factorial layout is an example. In such designs the kurtosis of the errors does not affect the F statistic to first order. The variance-ratio test for comparing the error variances of two independent samples is the opposite case. With n1 and n2 observations, each sample centred at its own mean, d has entries 1/n1 on the first sample and −1/n2 on the second (Exercise (B2)), so Var(L)=2f1+2f2+κ(1n1+1n2), and the kurtosis term is as large as the normal-theory term. The test is not robust at any sample size. For exponential errors and n1=n2=20, the nominal 5% test rejected equal variances in 0.268 of 100000 simulated pairs of samples. Its large-sample limit is 0.327 (Exercise (B2)). The F test for equal means in a balanced one-way layout with three groups of eight had size 0.042, and with unbalanced groups of 2, 4 and 18 it had size 0.058. In the unbalanced layout Var(L) is 1.870 instead of 1.095, which overstates the effect on the size: in the tail, skewness and the curvature of the logarithm also matter.

Idea. Inference about 𝛃β is robust to the shape of the errors when no observation has large leverage. Inference about σ2, variance ratios or single future responses is not, at any sample size.

19.3.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For a straight line at x=1,…,n, show that the largest leverage is 1/n+3(n−1)/(n(n+1))<4/n.

Exercise (A2)

In the far-point design of Example (Coverage of the t interval), show that the leverage of the far point is approximately 147/(n+147) for large n. Why does the table still show nearly nominal two-sided coverage at n=160, when the leverage is almost one half?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In a one-way layout with m observations per group and skewed errors, compute ∑iwi3 of Proposition 19.3.3 for (i) a single group mean and (ii) the difference of two group means. What does this say about one-sample and two-sample t tests with skewed data?

Solution.

  • (i) The mean of group 1 has ci=1/m on its m observations, so wi=m−1/2 there and ∑iwi3=m⋅m−3/2=m−1/2. (ii) The difference has ci=±1/m on the two groups, so wi=±(2m)−1/2, and the cubes cancel: ∑iwi3=0. The numerator of a one-sample t statistic has skewness γ1/m. That of a two-sample comparison with equal group sizes has none, and its non-normality starts with the kurtosis term, of order 1/m. Equal-sized two-sample comparisons are therefore much better protected against skewness.
Exercise (B2)

Two independent samples of size m are each centred at their own mean. Let S12 and S22 be the sample variances. (i) Show that the vector d of Proposition 19.3.5 has entries 1/m and −1/m. (ii) Using the first part of Proposition 19.3.4 for each sample, show that when the two variances are equal the two-sided variance-ratio test of nominal size α has size tending to 2(1−Φ(zα/22/(2+κ))) as m→∞.

Solution.

  • (i) P1 is the centring matrix on the first sample and zero elsewhere, so its diagonal is 1−1/m=f1/m there, and p1/f1 has entries 1/m on the first sample. Likewise p2/f2 has entries 1/m on the second, which enter d with a minus sign. (ii) By Proposition 19.3.4 (with p=1), m(Sj2/σ2−1)→N(0,2+κ) for j=1,2, independently. By the delta method, mlog⁡(S12/S22)→N(0,2(2+κ)). Under normality the same statistic tends to N(0,4), and the critical values of the F(m−1,m−1) distribution satisfy mlog⁡Fα/2→2zα/2. The rejection probability therefore tends to Pr(|Z|2(2+κ)>2zα/2)=2(1−Φ(zα/22/(2+κ))). For κ=6 and α=0.05 this is 0.327.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let the errors be independent with mean zero, variances σi2∈[m0,M0] with m0>0, and supiE|εi|3<∞. Suppose hn∗→0. Show that for every nonzero an, 𝛃𝛃𝛃(an⊤β^−an⊤β)/Var(an⊤β^)→N(0,1), where the variance is the sandwich Eq. 5.5.3. Explain why this does not make the usual t statistic asymptotically standard normal.

Solution. With cni as in the proof of Theorem 19.3.2, 𝛃Var(an⊤β^)=∑icni2σi2≥m0τn2. Put 𝛃Zni=cniεi/Var(an⊤β^). Lyapunov’s bound E[Z21{|Z|>η}]≤E|Z|3/η gives the Lindeberg sum at most supiE|εi|3∑i|cni|3η(m0τn2)3/2≤supiE|εi|3ηm03/2maxi|cni|τn≤supiE|εi|3ηm03/2hn∗→0, using ∑i|cni|3≤maxi|cni|τn2. The usual t statistic divides by sτn, and s2τn2 estimates σ¯2τn2 with σ¯2 an average variance, not ∑icni2σi2. The ratio of the two is the quantity of Exercise (B1), and it need not tend to one. Chapter 21 replaces s2τn2 by a consistent estimate of the sandwich.

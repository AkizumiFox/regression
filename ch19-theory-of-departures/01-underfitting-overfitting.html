19.1  Omitting and adding regressors

The assumption 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ claims that the columns of X are the right ones. This section compares two ways of getting them wrong. Underfitting leaves out regressors that matter. Overfitting includes regressors that do not. This is a first look at the bias–variance trade-off of Part VI.

19.1.1. The setting

Throughout the section the truth is 𝛃𝛃E(Y)=X1β1+X2β2,Cov(Y)=σ2In, where X=[X1,X2] has full column rank p=p1+p2<n. The long regression fits both blocks and gives 𝛃𝛃𝛃β^=(β^1⊤,β^2⊤)⊤ and s2=‖(I−M)Y‖2/(n−p). The short regression fits X1 alone and gives 𝛃β^1S=(X1⊤X1)−1X1⊤Y,s12=‖(I−M1)Y‖2n−p1, where the superscript S marks the short fit and M1 projects onto C(X1). Two quantities recur. The first is Π=(X1⊤X1)−1X1⊤X2, the coefficients from regressing each column of X2 on X1, as in Proposition 5.7.2. The second is

𝛃𝛃𝛃γ=‖(I−M1)X2β2‖2σ2=β2⊤X2⊤(I−M1)X2β2σ2.
(19.1.1)

Since 𝛃𝛃𝛃(M−M1)Xβ=(I−M1)Xβ=(I−M1)X2β2, this is the noncentrality parameter Eq. 11.1.2 of the F test of 𝛃β2=0 in the long model. It measures how far the true mean lies from the short model’s column space, in units of σ2. It is zero iff 𝛃β2=0, because 𝛃X2β2∈C(X1) forces 𝛃β2=0 when X has full rank.

Both regressions are linked by an exact identity and by one covariance.

Lemma 19.1.1 (Short and long regressions)

In this setting:

  • (a) 𝛃β^2=(X2⊤(I−M1)X2)−1X2⊤(I−M1)Y and 𝛃𝛃𝛃β^1=β^1S−Πβ^2;

  • (b) 𝛃𝛃Cov(β^1S,β^2)=0 and 𝛃Cov(β^2)=σ2C, where C=(X2⊤(I−M1)X2)−1.

Proof.

  • (a) The first formula is Theorem 6.6.2(a) with the roles of the two blocks exchanged, and the second is Proposition 5.7.2(a). (b) Both estimators are linear in Y. By Theorem 2.2.1, 𝛃𝛃Cov(β^1S,β^2)=σ2(X1⊤X1)−1X1⊤(I−M1)X2C=0, because X1⊤(I−M1)=0. Similarly 𝛃Cov(β^2)=σ2CX2⊤(I−M1)2X2C=σ2C.

19.1.2. Underfitting

Theorem 19.1.2 (Omitted regressors)

Fit the short model when the long model is true.

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃𝛃E(β^1S)=β1+Πβ2 and 𝛃Cov(β^1S)=σ2(X1⊤X1)−1. The bias 𝛃Πβ2 vanishes iff 𝛃X1⊤X2β2=0, and it vanishes for every 𝛃β2 iff X1⊤X2=0.

  • (b) The fitted values have mean 𝛃𝛃Xβ−(I−M1)X2β2. The residuals have mean 𝛃(I−M1)X2β2 and covariance σ2(I−M1), as if the short model were right.

  • (c) E(s12)=σ2(1+γ/(n−p1)), so s12 overestimates σ2 unless 𝛃β2=0.

  • (d) At a new point with regressors (x10,x20), the short prediction 𝛃x10⊤β^1S has bias 𝛃(Π⊤x10−x20)⊤β2 for the mean 𝛃𝛃x10⊤β1+x20⊤β2.

Proof.

  • (a) The mean is Proposition 5.7.2(b). The covariance is Theorem 5.5.1(b) for the model matrix X1, which needs only Cov(Y)=σ2I. Since (X1⊤X1)−1 is nonsingular, 𝛃Πβ2=0 iff 𝛃X1⊤X2β2=0, and this holds for all 𝛃β2 iff X1⊤X2=0.
  • (b) 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E(M1Y)=X1β1+M1X2β2=Xβ−(I−M1)X2β2, and 𝛃E((I−M1)Y)=(I−M1)X2β2 because (I−M1)X1=0. The covariance is (I−M1)σ2I(I−M1)=σ2(I−M1).
  • (c) By Eq. 2.3.1 with M1 of rank p1 and mean 𝛃Xβ, 𝛃E‖(I−M1)Y‖2=σ2(n−p1)+‖(I−M1)X2β2‖2=σ2(n−p1+γ).
  • (d) Take expectations in 𝛃x10⊤β^1S using (a), and subtract the true mean.

Nothing in the second moments signals the omission: the covariances of 𝛃β^1S and of the residuals are exactly what the short model claims. The damage is in the first moments. The estimator is centred at the wrong place, and the residuals carry the systematic component 𝛃(I−M1)X2β2 that added-variable plots (Theorem 6.6.2) and lack-of-fit tests (Theorem 14.6.2) look for. Because s12 is inflated, the reported variances are on average too large, yet as n grows they shrink while the bias does not, so the coverage of intervals for 𝛃β1 tends to zero. Nothing requires a coefficient to be omitted: for any true mean 𝛃𝛅X1β1+δ, such as a curved mean fitted by a line, (a)–(c) hold with 𝛃X2β2 replaced by 𝛅δ (Proposition 5.5.7(a)).

19.1.3. Overfitting

Theorem 19.1.3 (Irrelevant regressors)

Suppose 𝛃β2=0, so the short model is correct, but the long model is fitted.

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃E(β^1)=β1, 𝛃E(β^2)=0 and E(s2)=σ2.

  • (b) 𝛃𝛃Cov(β^1)=Cov(β^1S)+σ2ΠCΠ⊤. Hence 𝛃𝛃Var(a⊤β^1)≥Var(a⊤β^1S) for every a∈Rp1, with equality iff Π⊤a=0, and 𝛃𝛃Cov(β^1)=Cov(β^1S) iff X1⊤X2=0.

  • (c) At a new point x0=(x10⊤,x20⊤)⊤, 𝛃𝛃Var(x0⊤β^)=Var(x10⊤β^1S)+σ2(x20−Π⊤x10)⊤C(x20−Π⊤x10), and summed over the design points 𝛃𝛃E‖Xβ^−Xβ‖2=σ2p, against σ2p1 for the short fit.

Proof.

  • (a) The long model is correct (with 𝛃β2=0), so Theorem 5.5.1(a) and Theorem 5.6.1 apply.
  • (b) By Lemma 19.1.1(a), 𝛃𝛃𝛃β^1=β^1S−Πβ^2 with the two terms uncorrelated, so 𝛃𝛃𝛃Cov(β^1)=Cov(β^1S)+ΠCov(β^2)Π⊤, and Lemma 19.1.1(b) gives 𝛃Cov(β^2). Because C is positive definite, a⊤ΠCΠ⊤a=0 iff Π⊤a=0, and ΠCΠ⊤=0 iff Π=0, iff X1⊤X2=0.
  • (c) Write 𝛃𝛃𝛃x0⊤β^=x10⊤β^1S+(x20−Π⊤x10)⊤β^2 using Lemma 19.1.1(a), and use the zero covariance of (b) of that lemma. For the sum, 𝛃Xβ^=MY is unbiased for 𝛃Xβ with covariance σ2M, so 𝛃E‖MY−Xβ‖2=σ2trM=σ2p. The short fit M1Y is also unbiased, and its trace is p1.

An irrelevant regressor costs no bias, only variance, and the price grows with its correlation with the relevant columns: for one extra column the variance of a coefficient is multiplied by 1/(1−r2), with r the partial correlation of the two regressors (Eq. 6.6.2).

19.1.4. Bias against variance

When 𝛃β2 is small but not zero, the short regression is biased but less variable. Which is better? Compare the two estimators of 𝛃β by their mean squared error matrices, writing 𝛃𝛃β^S=((β^1S)⊤,0⊤)⊤ for the short estimator, which sets 𝛃β2 to zero.

Lemma 19.1.4 (A rank-one comparison)

Let C be a positive definite q×q matrix, b∈Rq and c>0. Then cC−bb⊤ is nonnegative definite iff b⊤C−1b≤c.

Proof. By Lemma 12.2.3 with W=C and u=b, the inequality b⊤C−1b≤c holds iff (a⊤b)2≤ca⊤Ca for every a, that is, iff a⊤(cC−bb⊤)a≥0 for every a.

Theorem 19.1.5 (When the short regression wins)

In the setting of this section, let G=(−ΠIp2), a p×p2 matrix.

  • (a) The mean squared error matrix of 𝛃β^S is 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E[(β^S−β)(β^S−β)⊤]=Cov(β^)−G(σ2C−β2β2⊤)G⊤.

  • (b) 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃E(λ⊤β^S−λ⊤β)2≤Var(λ⊤β^) for every 𝛌λ∈Rp iff γ≤1. If γ>1, the reverse strict inequality holds for some 𝛌λ.

  • (c) Over the design points, 𝛃𝛃E‖Xβ^S−Xβ‖2=σ2(p1+γ), against σ2p for the long fit. The short fit is better there iff γ<p2.

Proof.

  • (a) By Lemma 19.1.1(a), 𝛃𝛃𝛃β^=β^S+Gβ^2, and 𝛃β^S is uncorrelated with 𝛃β^2 by part (b) of the lemma. Since 𝛃𝛃E(β^)=β and 𝛃𝛃E(β^2)=β2, 𝛃𝛃𝛃E(β^S)−β=−Gβ2. Expand 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃Cov(β^)=E[(β^S−β+Gβ^2)(β^S−β+Gβ^2)⊤]. The cross term is 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E[(β^S−β)β^2⊤]=Cov(β^S,β^2)+(Eβ^S−β)β2⊤=−Gβ2β2⊤, and 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E(β^2β^2⊤)=σ2C+β2β2⊤. So 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃Cov(β^)=E[(β^S−β)(β^S−β)⊤]+G(σ2C+β2β2⊤−2β2β2⊤)G⊤.
  • (b) Put 𝛃𝛃A=σ2C−β2β2⊤. The claimed inequality for all 𝛌λ says that GAG⊤ is nonnegative definite. The matrix L=[0,Ip2] satisfies LG=I, so A=L(GAG⊤)L⊤. Hence GAG⊤ is nonnegative definite iff A is, and by Lemma 19.1.4 iff 𝛃𝛃β2⊤C−1β2≤σ2, which is γ≤1. If γ>1, the same lemma gives a with a⊤Aa<0, and 𝛌λ=L⊤a satisfies 𝛌G⊤λ=a, so 𝛌𝛌λ⊤GAG⊤λ<0.
  • (c) 𝛃Xβ^S=M1Y, whose mean is 𝛃M1Xβ and covariance σ2M1. So 𝛃𝛃E‖M1Y−Xβ‖2=σ2p1+‖(I−M1)Xβ‖2=σ2(p1+γ). The long fit is Theorem 19.1.3(c), whose proof did not use 𝛃β2=0 for this part.

The threshold in (b) depends only on the noncentrality, not on the design or on n. It covers all linear functions at once; for a particular function the short estimator can win for larger γ, as (c) shows. Since γ is unknown, neither criterion applies directly. But the F statistic for 𝛃β2=0 depends on the parameters only through γ (Theorem 11.1.1), so a small F is evidence that γ is small. Turning this into a procedure, and paying for having chosen the model from the data, is the subject of Chapter 29.

Example (A biased estimator that wins)

Take n=30 observations of two centred regressors with unit sample variances and sample correlation r=0.9, an intercept, σ=1 and β2=0.301, chosen so that γ=0.5. Here Π has slope entry r, so the short slope on x1 has bias rβ2=0.271. Its standard deviation is 0.186, against 0.426 for the long slope, and the ratio of mean squared error to long variance is 0.595. A simulation with 200000 data sets gives 0.594.

For two standardized regressors the ratio has a closed form (Exercise (B2)):

E(β^1S−β1)2Var(β^1)=1−r2+r2γ=1+r2(γ−1),
(19.1.2)

which is 1−0.81+0.81×0.5=0.595. The short slope wins iff γ<1, as Theorem 19.1.5(b) requires, and the stakes, gain or loss, are proportional to r2. This is panel (a) of Figure 19.1.1. The short model’s s12 has mean 1+γ/(n−2)=1.0179, a bias too small to notice.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1901)
n, r, sigma = 30, 0.9, 1.0

# two centred, unit-variance regressors with sample correlation exactly r:
# orthonormalize (1, z1, z2) and keep the last two columns, which are centred
Z = np.linalg.qr(np.column_stack([np.ones(n), rng.normal(size=(n, 2))]))[0][:, 1:]
Z *= np.sqrt(n - 1)
x1 = Z[:, 0]
x2 = r * Z[:, 0] + np.sqrt(1 - r**2) * Z[:, 1]
X1 = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1])                # the short model
X = np.column_stack([X1, x2])                         # the long model

M1 = X1 @ np.linalg.solve(X1.T @ X1, X1.T)
S22_1 = x2 @ (np.eye(n) - M1) @ x2                    # ||(I - M1) x2||^2
beta2 = np.sqrt(0.5 * sigma**2 / S22_1)               # chosen so that gamma = 0.5
gamma = beta2**2 * S22_1 / sigma**2

var_long = sigma**2 * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)[1, 1]    # Var of the long slope on x1
Pi = np.linalg.solve(X1.T @ X1, X1.T @ x2)[1]         # slope of x2 on x1
var_short = sigma**2 * np.linalg.inv(X1.T @ X1)[1, 1]
mse_short = var_short + (Pi * beta2) ** 2             # variance + squared bias
print(f"gamma = {gamma:.3f}, MSE(short)/Var(long) = {mse_short / var_long:.4f}")
print(f"formula 1 - r^2 + r^2 gamma      = {1 - r**2 + r**2 * gamma:.4f}")
reps = 200_000
beta = np.array([1.0, 1.0, beta2])
Y = X @ beta + sigma * rng.normal(size=(reps, n))
b_long = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ Y.T)[1]
b_short = np.linalg.solve(X1.T @ X1, X1.T @ Y.T)[1]
print("simulated MSE ratio:", np.mean((b_short - 1) ** 2) / np.mean((b_long - 1) ** 2))
Example (Choosing a polynomial degree)

Part (c) of Theorem 19.1.5 applies to any nested sequence of models. Take n=40 equally spaced points on [−1,1], the mean μ(x)=exsin⁡3x, which is not a polynomial, and σ=0.25. A polynomial of degree d has p=d+1 columns, and its risk at the design points is 𝛍σ2(d+1)+‖(I−Md)μ‖2. Panel (b) of Figure 19.1.1 shows the two terms. In units of σ2 the risk is 161.41 for a straight line, falls to 5.52 at degree 4, and climbs back to 11.00 at degree 10, where the fit is almost unbiased and pays only for its variance. Exercise (B3) shows that adding one term lowers the risk iff that term’s own noncentrality exceeds 1.

Idea. Omitting a regressor that matters costs bias; keeping one that does not costs variance, and for γ<1 the bias is the cheaper. This does not make the biased coefficient interpretable: it still estimates 𝛃𝛃β1+Πβ2, and whether that answers the question is a matter for Chapter 25.

19.1.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

A straight line is fitted at x=0,1,2,3 when the true mean is β0+β1x+β2x2. Find the biases of the fitted intercept and slope.

Exercise (A2)

True or false: if the omitted regressors are orthogonal to the included ones (X1⊤X2=0), the short regression is harmless, including its estimate of σ2.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In Exercise (A1), show that ‖(I−M1)X2‖2=4. For which values of β2/σ does the straight line have a smaller mean squared error matrix than the quadratic fit, in the sense of Theorem 19.1.5(b)? For which values is it better at the four design points?

Exercise (B2)

Derive Eq. 19.1.2. Take an intercept and two centred regressors with ‖x1‖2=‖x2‖2=S and sample correlation r. Show that Var(β^1)=σ2/(S(1−r2)), that the short slope has variance σ2/S and bias rβ2, and that γ=β22S(1−r2)/σ2.

Solution. The long variance is Eq. 6.6.2 with R12=r2. The short slope is x1⊤Y/S, with variance σ2/S and mean β1+β2x1⊤x2/S=β1+rβ2. The residual of x2 after regression on (1,x1) has squared length S(1−r2), which gives γ. Then (σ2/S+r2β22)S(1−r2)/σ2=1−r2+r2γ.

Exercise (B3)

Let C(X1)⊂C(X2)⊂⋯ be nested model spaces with dimensions p1<p2<⋯, and let 𝛍μ=E(Y) be arbitrary. Show that the risk 𝛍E‖MkY−μ‖2 of model k exceeds that of model k+1 iff 𝛍γk=‖(Mk+1−Mk)μ‖2/σ2>pk+1−pk.

Solution. As in Theorem 19.1.5(c), the risk of model k is 𝛍σ2pk+‖(I−Mk)μ‖2. By Theorem 6.5.1, I−Mk=(I−Mk+1)+(Mk+1−Mk) with orthogonal ranges, so 𝛍𝛍‖(I−Mk)μ‖2=‖(I−Mk+1)μ‖2+σ2γk. The difference of the two risks is σ2(γk−(pk+1−pk)).

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Assume normal errors. Compare s12 (short) and s2 (long) as estimators of σ2 by mean squared error, when 𝛃β2≠0. Show that s12 is better iff 2(n−p1)+4γ+γ2<2(n−p1)2/(n−p), and interpret the condition when n is large.

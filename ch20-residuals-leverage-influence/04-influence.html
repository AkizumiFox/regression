20.4  Cook’s distance, DFFITS and DFBETAS

An outlier is a case the model fits badly; an influential case is one whose removal would change the conclusions. A remote case that follows the trend of the others exactly has no influence, and a large residual near the centroid moves the fit little. Influence measures, all from Theorem 20.3.1, quantify different consequences of deleting a case.

20.4.1. Four measures

Definition 20.4.1 (Cook’s distance, DFFITS, DFBETAS)

Let hii<1, and let cjj be the jth diagonal entry of (X⊤X)−1, so that s2cjj is the estimated variance of β^j.

  • (a) Cook’s distance is 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃Di=(β^(i)−β^)⊤X⊤X(β^(i)−β^)ps2.

  • (b) DFFITS is the change in case i’s own fitted value in units of its standard error, 𝛃DFFITSi=y^i−x(i)⊤β^(i)s(i)hii.

  • (c) DFBETAS is the change in one coefficient in units of its standard error, DFBETASij=β^j−β^j(i)s(i)cjj.

  • (d) COVRATIO compares the generalized variances of the estimates with and without case i, COVRATIOi=det(s(i)2(X(i)⊤X(i))−1)det(s2(X⊤X)−1).

Cook (1977) proposed Di; DFFITS, DFBETAS and COVRATIO are from Belsley, Kuh and Welsch (1980). DFFITS and DFBETAS use s(i), for the same reason as ti. None requires a refit.

Theorem 20.4.1 (Influence identities)

Let hii<1 and n−p≥2, and let x~j and ℓij be as in Definition 20.2.1.

  • (a) Di=‖Y^−Y^(i)‖2ps2=ri2p⋅hii1−hii.

  • (b) DFFITSi=tihii/(1−hii), and Di=DFFITSi2s(i)2/(ps2).

  • (c) DFBETASij=tix~ij‖x~j‖1−hii, so DFBETASij2=ti2ℓij/(1−hii).

  • (d) For every 𝛌λ≠0, 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌(λ⊤β^−λ⊤β^(i))2s(i)2λ⊤(X⊤X)−1λ≤DFFITSi2, with equality at 𝛌λ=x(i). In particular |DFBETASij|≤|DFFITSi| for every j.

  • (e) COVRATIOi=11−hii(n−pn−p−1+ti2)p.

Proof. Put 𝛃𝛃d=β^−β^(i)=(X⊤X)−1x(i)g with g=ε^i/(1−hii) (Theorem 20.3.1(b)).

  • (a) Y^−Y^(i)=Xd, and ‖Xd‖2=d⊤X⊤Xd=g2x(i)⊤(X⊤X)−1x(i)=g2hii. So pDis2=ε^i2hii/(1−hii)2, and dividing by s2 and using ri2=ε^i2/{s2(1−hii)} gives (a).

  • (b) The numerator of DFFITSi is x(i)⊤d=hiig, so DFFITSi=hiiε^i/{s(i)(1−hii)}=tihii/(1−hii). Then DFFITSi2s(i)2=ε^i2hii/(1−hii)2=pDis2.

  • (c) By Theorem 6.6.2(a) the jth row of (X⊤X)−1X⊤ is x~j⊤/‖x~j‖2, so the jth entry of (X⊤X)−1x(i), the ith column of that matrix, is x~ij/‖x~j‖2. By Eq. 6.6.1, cjj=1/‖x~j‖2. Hence DFBETASij=x~ijg‖x~j‖−2s(i)‖x~j‖−1=x~ijε^is(i)‖x~j‖(1−hii), which is the stated form.

  • (d) 𝛌𝛌λ⊤d=gλ⊤(X⊤X)−1x(i). Let K1/2 be the symmetric square root of K=(X⊤X)−1 (Theorem 1.7.6). The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (Proposition 1.1.1) for 𝛌K1/2λ and K1/2x(i) gives 𝛌𝛌𝛌(λ⊤(X⊤X)−1x(i))2≤λ⊤(X⊤X)−1λ⋅hii, with equality when 𝛌λ is a multiple of x(i). So the ratio is at most g2hii/s(i)2=DFFITSi2. Taking 𝛌λ=ej gives DFBETASij2.

  • (e) By Example (Deleting an observation), det(X(i)⊤X(i))=(1−hii)det(X⊤X), so COVRATIOi=(s(i)2/s2)p/(1−hii). From the proof of Theorem 20.3.3(c), s(i)2/s2=(n−p−ri2)/(n−p−1), and solving ti2=ri2(n−p−1)/(n−p−ri2) for ri2 gives n−p−ri2=(n−p)(n−p−1)/(n−p−1+ti2). Substitute.

20.4.2. What the measures mean

Cook’s distance has two readings. By (a) it is the squared distance the fitted vector moves when case i is deleted, in units of ps2. By Theorem 12.2.1 with Λ=I, the 100(1−α)% confidence ellipsoid for 𝛃β is 𝛃𝛃{b:(b−β^)⊤X⊤X(b−β^)≤ps2Fα(p,n−p)}, so 𝛃β^(i) lies on the boundary of the ellipsoid of level Pr{F(p,n−p)≤Di}.

By the second equality in (a), Di is misfit, ri2, times leverage, hii/(1−hii): a case is influential only if it has both. DFFITS factors the same way with ti. The influence plot of Figure 20.4.1 places each case by leverage and studentized residual, with the curves of constant Di, r2=pD(1−h)/h.

DFBETAS isolates one coefficient: by (c) the partial leverage ℓij replaces the leverage in the product, but the factor 1/(1−hii) still involves the overall leverage. By (d), the largest standardized change in any linear combination 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ is |DFFITSi|, so a small DFFITS rules out a large change in every coefficient and contrast. COVRATIO measures precision rather than location: by (e) it exceeds one when hii is large and ti small (the case adds precision) and falls below one when ti is large (the case inflates s2).

20.4.3. The null law of Cook’s distance and the choice of cut-offs

How large must an influence measure be before it matters? Since Di is a multiple of ri2, its law under the model is known exactly.

Proposition 20.4.2 (Cook’s distance under the model)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with n−p≥2 and all leverages below one. Then Di=n−pphii1−hiiBi with Bi∼Beta(12,n−p−12), so E(Di)=hiip(1−hii),1n∑i=1nE(Di)≥1n−p, with equality in the last bound iff all leverages equal p/n.

Proof. Combine Theorem 20.4.1(a) with Proposition 20.1.1(a), and use E(Bi)=1/(n−p). For the bound, u↦u/(1−u) is strictly convex on [0,1), so by Jensen’s inequality the average of hii/(1−hii) is at least h¯/(1−h¯)=p/(n−p), with equality iff the hii are equal.

So under the model a typical Di is of order 1/n, which explains the popular cut-off 4/n. For the state design of Example (How much does the District of Columbia matter?) the average of E(Di) is 0.0240, against the bound 1/(n−p)=0.0213. Comparing Di with the median of F(p,n−p), which is near one, is far stricter: it flags only cases whose deletion moves the estimate to the edge of a 50% confidence region. The usual cut-offs are these.

Measure Cut-off Reasoning
hii 2p/n twice the average leverage
Di 4/n, or the median of F(p,n−p) four times the typical value; or the edge of the 50% region
|DFFITSi| 2p/n |ti|≈2 at a case of average leverage p/n
|DFBETASij| 2/n |ti|≈2 at a case of average partial leverage 1/n
|COVRATIOi−1| 3p/n first-order expansion of (e) at |ti|≈2 or hii≈2p/n

All are arbitrary in the same way: each puts a case with studentized residual about two and typical leverage near the line. Calibrating Di exactly by Proposition 20.4.2 would not help: for a fixed design Di is a multiple of ri2, so the resulting test is an outlier test with a case-dependent level, discarding the leverage factor that is the point of an influence measure. Influence is a consequence to be assessed, not a hypothesis: the right threshold is whether deleting the case changes a conclusion.

Example (How much does the District of Columbia matter?)

In the murder-rate regression with all 51 jurisdictions, the District of Columbia has Di=3.430, seven times the next largest (Mississippi, 0.481); the median of F(4,47) is 0.851, and deleting the District moves 𝛃β^ to the boundary of the 0.985 confidence ellipsoid. Its DFFITS is 4.333 (cut-off 0.560) and its DFBETAS for poverty −1.090 (cut-off 0.280). Its partial leverage for poverty is only 0.032 (Example (Where the District of Columbia’s leverage comes from)), but by Theorem 20.4.1(c) it is multiplied by ti2/(1−hii): 4.2782×0.032/(1−0.506)≈1.19, so |DFBETAS|≈1.09. Alaska, with the largest partial leverage (0.141), has ti=−0.760 and DFBETAS 0.315.

The consequences are substantive. With the District, the poverty coefficient is 0.1551 (standard error 0.1059, t=1.47); without it, 0.2538 (standard error 0.0934, t=2.72), the fit of Example (Poverty and murder rates). One jurisdiction decides whether poverty has a detectable association with murder rates given the other regressors, and the single-parent coefficient moves from 0.6256 to 0.3980. The cut-offs flag 2 cases by Di>4/n=0.078, 3 by DFFITS, 5 by DFBETAS for poverty and 4 by leverage above 2p/n=0.157, but only the District changes a conclusion. Its COVRATIO is 0.578: it inflates the estimated generalized variance through its contribution to s2.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats
data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data           # District of Columbia kept
y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), data[["poverty", "single", "urban"]]])
names = np.array(data.index)
n, p = X.shape
Q, R = np.linalg.qr(X)
beta = np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ y)
e = y - X @ beta                                   # raw residuals
h = np.sum(Q ** 2, axis=1)                         # leverages
s2 = e @ e / (n - p)
r = e / np.sqrt(s2 * (1 - h))                      # internally studentized
s2_del = (e @ e - e ** 2 / (1 - h)) / (n - p - 1)  # s^2 without case i
t = e / np.sqrt(s2_del * (1 - h))                  # externally studentized

cook = r ** 2 * h / (p * (1 - h))                  # Cook's distance
dffits = t * np.sqrt(h / (1 - h))
K = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
C = K @ X.T                                        # row j: weights of y in beta_j
dfbetas = (C * e / (1 - h)).T / np.sqrt(np.outer(s2_del, np.diag(K)))
for i in np.argsort(-cook)[:3]:
    print(f"{names[i]:22s} D = {cook[i]:.3f}  DFFITS = {dffits[i]:6.3f}"
          f"  DFBETAS(poverty) = {dfbetas[i, 1]:6.3f}")
print("conventional cut-offs: D > 4/n =", round(4 / n, 3),
      " |DFFITS| > 2 sqrt(p/n) =", round(2 * np.sqrt(p / n), 3),
      " |DFBETAS| > 2/sqrt(n) =", round(2 / np.sqrt(n), 3))

20.4.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In a regression with p=3, case i has ri=2. Compute Di when hii=0.1 and when hii=0.6. Which case would you look at first, and why might neither be alarming?

Solution. By Theorem 20.4.1(a), Di=(4/3)hii/(1−hii): (4/3)(1/9)=4/27≈0.15 and (4/3)(1.5)=2. The second moves the coefficient vector to about the edge of a high-level confidence region. Neither is alarming by itself if the change it produces in the estimates of interest is small relative to their uncertainty, or if the case is known to be correct and the model to hold in its region.

Exercise (A2)

Show that Di=0 iff ε^i=0, whatever the leverage. Give an example of a case with leverage 0.9 and no influence.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let C=(X⊤X)−1X⊤. Show that the sum of squares of the jth row of C is cjj, and deduce that ∑iℓij=1 in a second way. Why does this suggest a cut-off of order 1/n for DFBETAS?

Solution. CC⊤=(X⊤X)−1X⊤X(X⊤X)−1=(X⊤X)−1, whose jth diagonal entry is cjj. The jth row of C is x~j⊤/‖x~j‖2 (proof of Theorem 20.4.1(c)), so ∑iℓij=∑ix~ij2/‖x~j‖2=cjj‖x~j‖2=1. The partial leverages for a coefficient average 1/n. With |ti|≈2 and hii small, |DFBETASij|≈2ℓij≈2/n for a typical case.

Exercise (B2)

For simple regression, show that the DFBETAS of case i for the slope is ti(xi−x¯)/Sxx(1−hii). Which cases can change the slope most?

Exercise (B3)

Use Theorem 20.4.1(e) to show that for large n and small p/n, COVRATIOi≈1+hii−p(ti2−1)/n. Deduce the cut-off |COVRATIOi−1|>3p/n from the two extreme cases hii≈0, |ti|=2 and hii=2p/n, ti=0.

Solution. 1/(1−h)≈1+h, and ((n−p)/(n−p−1+t2))p=(1+(t2−1)/(n−p))−p≈1−p(t2−1)/n. Multiplying, COVRATIO≈1+h−p(t2−1)/n to first order. With h≈0 and t2=4 this is 1−3p/n: a case of no leverage and a residual of two standard errors lowers the precision by that much. With t=0 and h=2p/n it is 1+3p/n: a well-fitting case of high leverage raises it by the same amount. Deviations beyond 3p/n in either direction are more extreme than both.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

How influential can one case be, whatever its response? Fix a design of full rank with hii<1 for all i, and let y range over Rn.

  • (a) Show that 𝛆ε^i={(I−M)ei}⊤ε^, and deduce from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (Proposition 1.1.1) that ri2≤n−p for every data vector, with equality iff 𝛆ε^ is a multiple of (I−M)ei. (Proposition 20.1.1(b) gives the same bound from the beta law; this argument needs no model at all.)

  • (b) Deduce Di≤n−pp⋅hii1−hii, and show that the bound is attained: take y=ei.

  • (c) Conclude that in a design with equal leverages hii=p/n no case can have Di>1, whatever the responses. Using the confidence-ellipsoid reading of Di, say what this means for how far one case can move 𝛃β^.

  • (d) Contrast a design with a leverage close to one, and explain how (b) makes the 2p/n cut-off for leverage a statement about the worst case rather than the observed one.

Solution.

  • (a) Since I−M is symmetric and idempotent and 𝛆ε^=(I−M)y, ε^i=ei⊤(I−M)y={(I−M)ei}⊤{(I−M)y}. By Cauchy–Schwarz, 𝛆ε^i2≤‖(I−M)ei‖2‖ε^‖2=(1−hii)SSE, with equality iff the two vectors are proportional. Divide by s2(1−hii)=SSE(1−hii)/(n−p) to get ri2≤n−p.

  • (b) Substitute in Theorem 20.4.1(a), Di=ri2hii/{p(1−hii)}. For y=ei the residual is 𝛆ε^=(I−M)ei, which is proportional to itself, so equality holds in (a) and Di attains the bound.

  • (c) With hii=p/n the bound is n−pp⋅p/n1−p/n=1. By the ellipsoid reading, deleting any one case moves 𝛃β^ at most to the boundary of the confidence ellipsoid of level Pr{F(p,n−p)≤1}, which is near 0.5 for moderate n−p: in a design that spreads leverage evenly, no single case, however wild its response, can move the estimate beyond a middling confidence region.

  • (d) The bound grows without limit as hii→1, and the same case would then also have a tiny residual, so the observed Di need not be large. Leverage is the design’s exposure to a bad response: a case with hii near one can do unbounded damage if its yi is wrong, and nothing in the data reveals whether it is. That is why leverage is screened on its own, before any residual is looked at.

Exercise (C2)

Cook’s distance and DFFITS differ only in using s or s(i), but they can rank cases differently. Take n−p=6 and two cases with h11=1019,r12=1,h22=110,r22=5.

  • (a) Using Theorem 20.3.3(c), show that t12=1 and t22=25.

  • (b) Show that pD1=10/9 and pD2=5/9, while DFFITS12=10/9 and DFFITS22=25/9. So D ranks case 1 first and DFFITS ranks case 2 first.

  • (c) Check that such a data set is possible: find p and n for which the constraints ∑ihii=p and ∑i(1−hii)ri2=n−p (Exercise (A2)) can be met by the remaining cases.

  • (d) Explain the disagreement in one sentence, and say which measure you would use to decide whether case 2 is an outlier and which to decide whether it matters.

Solution.

  • (a) ti2=ri2(n−p−1)/(n−p−ri2)=5ri2/(6−ri2), so t12=5/5=1 and t22=25/1=25. The second case has a residual near the algebraic maximum r2≤n−p=6 of Exercise (C1), so deleting it shrinks s2 sharply and t22 is much larger than r22.

  • (b) With gi=hii/(1−hii) we have g1=(10/19)/(9/19)=10/9 and g2=1/9. Then pDi=ri2gi by Theorem 20.4.1(a), giving 10/9 and 5/9; and DFFITSi2=ti2gi by Theorem 20.4.1(b), giving 10/9 and 25/9. So D1=2D2 while DFFITS22=2.5DFFITS12.

  • (c) Take p=3 and n=9. The two cases use 10/19+1/10=119/190 of the leverage budget ∑ihii=3, leaving 451/190 for the other seven, each of which can then have leverage well below one. They use 1⋅919+5⋅910=189/38 of the budget ∑i(1−hii)ri2=n−p=6, leaving 39/38 for the other seven. Both constraints can be met.

  • (d) Di measures the move against s, which the case’s own residual inflates, while DFFITS measures it against s(i), which it does not; so a case with a large residual looks worse to DFFITS, and a case with high leverage and a modest residual looks worse to Di. To ask whether case 2 is an outlier, use ti, which has an exact null law (Theorem 20.3.3(b)); to ask whether it matters, use Di, whose ellipsoid reading is a statement about the estimate rather than about the error.

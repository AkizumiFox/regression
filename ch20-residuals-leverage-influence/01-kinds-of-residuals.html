20.1  Kinds of residuals

The model makes its assumptions about the errors, which are never observed. The residuals are their visible stand-ins, imperfect in a precise way: they are the projection of the errors onto a subspace of dimension n−p, so they are correlated, have unequal variances, and are shrunk most at the cases that matter most. This section defines rescaled residuals, finds their laws, and asks what residual plots reveal.

20.1.1. The setting

Throughout the chapter, 𝛃𝛆𝛆𝛆Y=Xβ+ε,E(ε)=0,Cov(ε)=σ2I, with X of size n×p and full column rank p<n. Results that need normal errors say so and then assume Eq. 7.3.1. The hat matrix is M=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤, and hij denotes its (i,j) entry, so that hii is the leverage of case i (Definition 6.8.1). The ith row of X, written as a column, is x(i), and ei is the ith coordinate vector of Rn. The residual vector is 𝛆ε^=(I−M)Y, with entries ε^i, and 𝛆s2=‖ε^‖2/(n−p).

Everything in the chapter depends on X only through C(X). If X has rank r<p, replace p by r in every degree of freedom and every formula that involves M alone. Formulas that involve (X⊤X)−1 hold for any full-rank reparameterization (Theorem 6.7.1).

Two facts from earlier chapters drive everything that follows. First, by Proposition 6.4.3(e),

𝛆𝛆ε^=(I−M)ε,
(20.1.1)

so the residuals are a fixed linear image of the errors. Second, by Proposition 5.5.3(b), 𝛆Cov(ε^)=σ2(I−M). So Var(ε^i)=σ2(1−hii) and, for i≠j,

Corr(ε^i,ε^j)=−hij(1−hii)(1−hjj).
(20.1.2)

A case with leverage near one has a residual with variance near zero: the fit passes close to it whatever its response. Raw residuals are therefore not comparable across cases.

20.1.2. Four residuals

Definition 20.1.1 (Standardized and studentized residuals)

Let hii<1. For case i:

  • (a) the raw residual is 𝛃ε^i=yi−x(i)⊤β^;

  • (b) the standardized residual is zi=ε^i/(σ1−hii), which needs the true σ;

  • (c) the internally studentized residual is ri=ε^is1−hii;

  • (d) the externally studentized residual is ti=ε^is(i)1−hii, where s(i)2=SSE(i)/(n−p−1) is the residual mean square of the least squares fit to the n−1 cases other than i.

Under normal errors zi is exactly N(0,1), but it cannot be computed. The studentized residuals replace σ by an estimate: s, which includes case i, or s(i), which does not, so that a gross error in yi cannot inflate the yardstick it is measured against. Section 20.3 shows that s(i) costs nothing extra and that ti has an exact t law.

Remark (Names). Terminology varies. Some authors call ri, or even ε^i/s, the standardized residual, and ti is also called the studentized deleted residual or RSTUDENT. R’s rstandard and rstudent return ri and ti, as do resid_studentized_internal and resid_studentized_external in statsmodels.

20.1.3. The law of the internally studentized residual

The residual ri is not t-distributed, because ε^i is part of s. Its law is a rescaled beta.

Proposition 20.1.1 (Law of the internally studentized residual)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1, n−p≥2 and hii<1. Then:

  • (a) ri2/(n−p)∼Beta(12,n−p−12);

  • (b) |ri|≤n−p, E(ri)=0 and Var(ri)=1;

  • (c) the law of ri is the same for every case and does not depend on X, 𝛃β or σ2.

Proof. Put u=(I−M)ei/1−hii. Since ‖(I−M)ei‖2=ei⊤(I−M)ei=1−hii, u is a unit vector in C(X)⊥, and by Eq. 20.1.1 𝛆𝛆ε^i=ei⊤(I−M)ε=1−hiiu⊤ε. Let P1=uu⊤ and P2=I−M−uu⊤. Because (I−M)u=u, both are symmetric and idempotent, P1P2=0, and their ranks are 1 and n−p−1. They split the residual sum of squares: 𝛆𝛆𝛆𝛆SSE=ε⊤(I−M)ε=‖P1ε‖2+‖P2ε‖2. By Theorem 4.4.4, applied to 𝛆ε∼Nn(0,σ2I), the variables 𝛆U=(u⊤ε)2/σ2∼χ2(1) and 𝛆V=‖P2ε‖2/σ2∼χ2(n−p−1) are independent. Now ri2n−p=ε^i2(1−hii)SSE=UU+V, which is Beta(12,n−p−12) by Exercise (B4). This is (a). A beta variable lies in [0,1], which gives the bound in (b). Replacing 𝛆ε by 𝛆−ε changes the sign of ri and not its law, so E(ri)=0, and E(ri2)=(n−p)⋅12/n−p2=1. Part (c) holds because the law in (a), together with the symmetry, determines the law of ri.

The bound in (b) bites when n−p is small: with n−p=5, no |ri| can exceed 5≈2.236, however wild the case, because a gross error inflates s along with ε^i. The ri are not independent either; their correlations are those of Eq. 20.1.2 (Exercise (C1)).

20.1.4. What a residual plot can show

A residual plot shows residuals against the fitted values, a regressor, an omitted variable or the order of collection, to reveal structure the model has missed. Least squares removes some structure automatically, and it is worth knowing exactly which.

Proposition 20.1.2 (What least squares leaves in the residuals)

Suppose 1∈C(X).

  • (a) The residual vector is orthogonal to 1, to every column of X and to Y^. Hence the least squares line through a plot of the residuals against the fitted values, or against any regressor in the model, is the horizontal axis.

  • (b) The least squares line through a plot of the residuals against the responses yi has slope 1−R2.

  • (c) If in fact 𝛃𝛅E(Y)=Xβ+δ for some 𝛅δ∈Rn, and Cov(Y)=σ2I, then 𝛆𝛅E(ε^)=(I−M)δ and 𝛆Cov(ε^)=σ2(I−M).

Proof.

  • (a) is Proposition 6.4.3(a) and (b). A line fitted with an intercept to points (ai,ε^i) has slope ∑i(ai−a¯)ε^i/∑i(ai−a¯)2, and the numerator is 𝛆𝛆a⊤ε^−a¯1⊤ε^=0 when a is Y^ or a column of X.
  • (b) With a=y the numerator is 𝛆𝛆𝛆𝛆y⊤ε^=(Y^+ε^)⊤ε^=‖ε^‖2=SSE, and the denominator is ∑i(yi−y¯)2=SST. The slope is SSE/SST=1−R2.
  • (c) 𝛆ε^=(I−M)Y, and Theorem 2.2.1 gives mean 𝛃𝛅𝛅(I−M)(Xβ+δ)=(I−M)δ and covariance σ2(I−M)2=σ2(I−M).

By (a), a plot against fitted values or regressors has had its linear trend removed, so any remaining trend is curved, a sign of a misspecified mean. By (b), a plot against the responses always slopes upward, even when the model is right, so it is not used.

Part (c) is the sobering one. The residuals are centred at 𝛅(I−M)δ, the part of the error in the mean orthogonal to the model space. The part of 𝛅δ inside C(X) leaves no trace, yet it is exactly this part, 𝛅(X⊤X)−1X⊤δ, that biases the coefficients (Proposition 5.5.7(a)). An omitted regressor highly correlated with the included ones biases their coefficients (Theorem 19.1.2) while leaving the residuals almost clean. Residual plots can show that the fitted values are wrong, never that the coefficients are right.

The standard plots are these. Against fitted values: curvature suggests a misspecified mean, a fan shape variance that grows with the mean, isolated points outliers. Against each regressor: curvature points to that regressor. Against a variable not in the model: better replaced by the added-variable plot of Section 6.6, whose slope is the coefficient the variable would receive (Exercise (B1)). Against collection order: runs of equal sign suggest serial correlation, tested in Chapter 21 (Theorem 21.5.3). A normal quantile plot: curvature suggests skewed or heavy-tailed errors (Proposition 21.1.1). Studentized residuals are preferable to raw ones throughout, since raw residuals make high-leverage cases look better than they are.

Example (Four residual plots)

Each panel of Figure 20.1.1 comes from 80 simulated cases with x uniform on (0,10), fitted by a straight line. In (a) the model is right and the residuals form a structureless band. In (b) the mean has a quadratic term, and the residuals show the parabola the line could not absorb. In (c) the error standard deviation grows with x, and the band opens into a fan. In (d) one response has an error of 8σ; it stands out, and the line has been pulled slightly towards it. In every panel the residuals are orthogonal to the fitted values, as Proposition 20.1.2(a) requires; the listing prints the inner product.

import numpy as np

def ls_fit(X, y):
    beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
    return beta, X @ beta, y - X @ beta

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 80
x = rng.uniform(0, 10, n)
eps = rng.normal(size=n)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])
responses = {
    "(a) model correct": 1 + 0.5 * x + eps,
    "(b) curved mean": 1 + 0.5 * x + 0.08 * (x - 5) ** 2 + 0.6 * eps,
    "(c) variance grows": 1 + 0.5 * x + (0.1 + 0.25 * x) * eps,
    "(d) one gross error": 1 + 0.5 * x + eps + 8.0 * (np.arange(n) == 17),
}
for name, y in responses.items():
    beta, fitted, resid = ls_fit(X, y)
    print(f"{name:22s} slope {beta[1]:.3f}   residuals . fitted = {resid @ fitted:.1e}")

20.1.5. Partial residuals

A plot of residuals against a regressor xj in the model has a flat least squares line (Proposition 20.1.2(a)). The component-plus-residual plot, or partial residual plot, adds the fitted linear component back. It plots ε^i+β^jxijagainstxij,i=1,…,n. Compare it with the added-variable plot of Section 6.6, which plots y~=(I−M(j))y against x~j=(I−M(j))xj, where M(j) projects onto the span of the columns other than xj.

Proposition 20.1.3 (Two plots with the same line)

Let 1∈C(X), and let xj be a column of X that is not a multiple of 1.

  • (a) The least squares line of 𝛆ε^+β^jxj on [1,xj] has intercept 0, slope β^j, and residual vector 𝛆ε^.

  • (b) The least squares line through the origin of y~ on x~j has slope β^j and residual vector 𝛆ε^.

Proof.

  • (a) The vector 𝛆ε^ is orthogonal to 1 and to xj by Proposition 20.1.2(a). So 𝛆𝛆ε^+β^jxj=β^jxj+ε^ is the sum of a vector in C([1,xj]) and a vector orthogonal to that space. By Theorem 6.4.1 the first is the fitted vector and the second the residual.
  • (b) is Theorem 6.6.2(a) and (b) with X1=xj.

So the two plots share their line and their scatter about it, and differ only in the horizontal axis. The added-variable plot uses the part of xj not explained by the other regressors: its spread is the information for β^j, and cases far out along it pull on β^j (partial leverage, Section 20.2). The component-plus-residual plot uses xj on its own scale, the scale on which a transformation would be chosen, so it shows the form of the dependence better. It can mislead when xj is related nonlinearly to the other regressors.

Example (Curvature seen two ways)

In 120 simulated cases, x1 is uniform on (0,4), x2 is correlated with it (sample correlation 0.87), and E(Y)=1+x2+0.7(x1−2)2. The fitted model is linear in both, with coefficient −0.144 for x1, the slope of both panels of Figure 20.1.2. A quadratic fitted to the component-plus-residual plot (b) has leading coefficient 0.671, close to the true 0.7. In the added-variable plot (a) the curve is smeared, because its horizontal axis mixes x1 with x2, and the corresponding coefficient is 0.309.

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n2 = 120
x1 = rng.uniform(0, 4, n2)
x2 = 0.8 * x1 + rng.normal(0, 0.6, n2)             # correlated with x1
y2 = 1 + x2 + 0.7 * (x1 - 2) ** 2 + rng.normal(0, 0.4, n2)
X2 = np.column_stack([np.ones(n2), x1, x2])        # the fitted model is linear in x1
beta2, fitted2, resid2 = ls_fit(X2, y2)

component = resid2 + beta2[1] * x1                 # component-plus-residual for x1
Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(n2), x2])             # everything except x1
x1_tilde = x1 - Z @ np.linalg.lstsq(Z, x1, rcond=None)[0]
y_tilde = y2 - Z @ np.linalg.lstsq(Z, y2, rcond=None)[0]   # added-variable coordinates
print("coefficient of x1:", beta2[1])
print("slope in component-plus-residual plot:", ls_fit(np.column_stack([np.ones(n2), x1]), component)[0][1])
print("slope in added-variable plot:", (x1_tilde @ y_tilde) / (x1_tilde @ x1_tilde))

20.1.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

A straight line is fitted to n=3 points. Show that every internally studentized residual satisfies |ri|=1 (unless all three residuals are zero), whatever the data. What does this say about using ri to judge a fit with one residual degree of freedom?

Solution. Here n−p=1, so C(X)⊥ is spanned by one unit vector v, and 𝛆ε^=cv for a scalar c. Then ε^i=cvi, s2=c2 and 1−hii=vi2 (the ith diagonal entry of I−M=vv⊤). So ri2=c2vi2/(c2vi2)=1 whenever vi≠0. With one degree of freedom for error the studentized residuals carry no information at all about which case fits worst.

Exercise (A2)

Show that ∑i(1−hii)ri2=n−p for any data. Deduce that if every leverage is at most hmax<1, then at most (n−p)/((1−hmax)c2) cases can have |ri|>c.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let 1∈C(X), let z∉C(X) be a candidate regressor, let γ^ be its coefficient when it is added to the model, and let Rz2 be the R2 of the regression of z on X. Show that the least squares slope of the plot of 𝛆ε^ against z is γ^(1−Rz2). Why is the added-variable plot the better tool for judging whether z belongs in the model?

Solution. The slope is 𝛆∑i(zi−z¯)ε^i/Szz=z⊤ε^/Szz, since 𝛆1⊤ε^=0. Put z~=(I−M)z. Then 𝛆z⊤ε^=z⊤(I−M)y=z~⊤y, and by Theorem 6.6.2(a), γ^=z~⊤y/‖z~‖2. So 𝛆z⊤ε^=γ^‖z~‖2, and ‖z~‖2=Szz(1−Rz2) is the residual sum of squares of z on X (Exercise (B1)). The slope is γ^(1−Rz2). When z is highly correlated with the columns of X, the plot against z shrinks the effect towards zero, while the added-variable plot shows γ^ itself, against the horizontal spread ‖z~‖ that actually carries information about it.

Exercise (B2)

Simple linear regression is fitted to xi=i, i=1,…,n, but E(Yi)=β0+β1xi+γxi2. Use Proposition 20.1.2(c) to show that the expected residuals lie on a parabola in i, and find its vertex.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with n−p≥2 and leverages below one. Show that E(rirj)=−hij/(1−hii)(1−hjj) for i≠j. Hint: write ri=n−pui⊤W with 𝛆𝛆W=(I−M)ε/‖(I−M)ε‖, and show that E(WW⊤)=(I−M)/(n−p).

Solution. With ui as in the proof of Proposition 20.1.1, ri=ε^i/(s1−hii)=n−pui⊤W, because 𝛆𝛆ui⊤(I−M)ε=ui⊤ε. For an orthogonal Q with Q(I−M)=(I−M)Q that maps C(X)⊥ to itself, 𝛆Qε has the same law as 𝛆ε, so QW has the same law as W and S=E(WW⊤) satisfies QSQ⊤=S. Since W∈C(X)⊥, S vanishes on C(X); restricted to C(X)⊥ it commutes with every rotation of that space and is therefore a multiple of the identity there. Its trace is E‖W‖2=1, so S=(I−M)/(n−p). Hence E(rirj)=(n−p)ui⊤Suj=ui⊤uj=ei⊤(I−M)ej/(1−hii)(1−hjj), which is the stated value. Since E(ri)=0 and Var(ri)=1, this is also the correlation.

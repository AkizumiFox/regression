20.5  Masking and swamping

Every diagnostic so far removes one case. But bad cases often come in groups: a batch of records in the wrong units, or a sensor that failed for an afternoon. Masking is when a bad case escapes detection because others hide it: deleting it alone leaves its companions to hold the fit in place. Swamping is when good cases are flagged because the bad ones have dragged the fit away from them. This section deletes sets of cases and proves how a cluster masks itself and swamps the rest.

20.5.1. Deleting a set of cases

Let D⊂{1,…,n} be a set of d cases. Write XD for the d×p matrix of their rows, X(D) for X with those rows removed, 𝛆ε^D for their residuals in the full fit, and HD=XD(X⊤X)−1XD⊤ for the d×d block of the hat matrix that belongs to them. Let 𝛃β^(D) and SSE(D) come from the least squares fit to the cases not in D, and let ED be the n×d matrix whose columns are the coordinate vectors ei, i∈D.

Theorem 20.5.1 (Deleting a set of cases)

Let X and X(D) have full column rank. Then I−HD is nonsingular, and

  • (a) 𝛃𝛆yD−XDβ^(D)=(I−HD)−1ε^D;

  • (b) 𝛃𝛃𝛆β^−β^(D)=(X⊤X)−1XD⊤(I−HD)−1ε^D;

  • (c) 𝛆𝛆SSE(D)=SSE−ε^D⊤(I−HD)−1ε^D;

  • (d) if n−p−d≥1, then under Eq. 7.3.1 𝛆𝛆FD=ε^D⊤(I−HD)−1ε^D/dSSE(D)/(n−p−d)∼F(d, n−p−d), and FD is the F statistic for 𝛄γ=0 in the mean-shift model 𝛃𝛄E(Y)=Xβ+EDγ.

Proof. Let Z=[X,ED]. If Xb+EDc=0, the rows outside D give X(D)b=0, so b=0, and then c=0. So Z has full column rank. By Lemma 6.6.1(b) with X1=ED, the matrix (I−M)ED has full column rank, so its Gram matrix ED⊤(I−M)ED=I−HD is nonsingular.

Fit the mean-shift model. By Theorem 6.6.2(a) with X1=ED and X2=X, the estimated shifts are 𝛄𝛆γ^=(ED⊤(I−M)ED)−1ED⊤(I−M)y=(I−HD)−1ε^D. On the other hand, for any b the residual sum of squares ∑i∉D(yi−x(i)⊤b)2+‖yD−XDb−c‖2 is minimized over c by c=yD−XDb, which removes the second term. What remains is minimized by 𝛃b=β^(D), uniquely because X(D) has full column rank. So the mean-shift fit has coefficients 𝛃β^(D) and 𝛄𝛃γ^=yD−XDβ^(D), and its residual sum of squares is SSE(D). Comparing the two expressions for 𝛄γ^ gives (a). By Theorem 6.6.2(c), the coefficients of X in the mean-shift fit are 𝛄𝛃𝛄(X⊤X)−1X⊤(y−EDγ^)=β^−(X⊤X)−1XD⊤γ^, which gives (b). By Lemma 6.6.1(c) the projection onto C(Z) is M plus the projection onto C((I−M)ED), so the fall in the residual sum of squares from the fit of X to the fit of Z is 𝛆𝛆y⊤(I−M)ED(I−HD)−1ED⊤(I−M)y=ε^D⊤(I−HD)−1ε^D, which gives (c). Finally C(X)⊆C(Z) with dimensions p and p+d, and FD is the statistic Eq. 11.1.1 for this pair of nested models, so (d) is Theorem 11.1.1(d).

With d=1 this is Theorem 20.3.1, and FD=ti2 (Exercise (A2)). Part (d) is exact only for a set chosen in advance; a Bonferroni correction over all (nd) subsets is valid but very conservative.

20.5.2. How a cluster masks itself

The simplest mechanism is a group of identical bad cases: n0 clean cases plus m copies of one bad case.

Proposition 20.5.2 (Masking and swamping by a cluster)

Let X0 (n0×p, full column rank) and y0 be the clean cases, with least squares estimate 𝛃β^0, residuals 𝛃ε˘j=yj−x(j)⊤β^0 and residual sum of squares SSE0. Add m≥1 cases, each with row x⋆⊤ and response y⋆. Put A=X0⊤X0, 𝛃h⋆=x⋆⊤A−1x⋆,δ=y⋆−x⋆⊤β^0, the leverage of x⋆ relative to the clean design and the distance of the cluster from the clean fit. In the least squares fit to all n0+m cases:

  • (a) each cluster case has leverage h=h⋆/(1+mh⋆)<1/m;

  • (b) each cluster case has residual δ/(1+mh⋆);

  • (c) deleting one cluster case gives it prediction residual δ/(1+(m−1)h⋆) and changes the estimate by 𝛃𝛃β^−β^(i)=A−1x⋆δ/[(1+mh⋆)(1+(m−1)h⋆)];

  • (d) deleting the whole cluster gives each member prediction residual δ, and 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃β^−β^(D)=m(1+(m−1)h⋆)(β^−β^(i));

  • (e) clean case j has residual ε˘j−mδhj⋆/(1+mh⋆), where hj⋆=x(j)⊤A−1x⋆, and SSE=SSE0+mδ2/(1+mh⋆).

Proof. Let D be the cluster. Then X⊤X=A+mx⋆x⋆⊤, and (A+mx⋆x⋆⊤)A−1x⋆=(1+mh⋆)x⋆, so

(X⊤X)−1x⋆=A−1x⋆1+mh⋆.
(20.5.1)
  • (a) h=x⋆⊤(X⊤X)−1x⋆=h⋆/(1+mh⋆), and mh=mh⋆/(1+mh⋆)<1.
  • (b) and (d) Deleting D leaves the clean cases, so 𝛃𝛃β^(D)=β^0 and every cluster case has prediction residual δ. All rows of XD equal x⋆⊤, so HD=h11⊤, and by Theorem 20.5.1(a) 𝛆ε^D=(I−h11⊤)δ1=(1−mh)δ1=δ1/(1+mh⋆). By Theorem 20.5.1(b) and Eq. 20.5.1, 𝛃𝛃β^−β^0=(X⊤X)−1XD⊤δ1=mδ(X⊤X)−1x⋆=mδA−1x⋆/(1+mh⋆).
  • (c) By Theorem 20.3.1(d) and (b), with 1−h=(1+(m−1)h⋆)/(1+mh⋆), the prediction residual is δ/(1+(m−1)h⋆). By Theorem 20.3.1(b) and Eq. 20.5.1, 𝛃𝛃β^−β^(i)=(X⊤X)−1x⋆⋅δ/(1+(m−1)h⋆), which is the stated vector. Comparing with the expression for 𝛃𝛃β^−β^0 in the previous step gives the ratio in (d).
  • (d) 𝛃𝛃𝛃yj−x(j)⊤β^=ε˘j−x(j)⊤(β^−β^0), and 𝛃𝛃x(j)⊤(β^−β^0)=mδhj⋆/(1+mh⋆). For the sum of squares, Theorem 20.5.1(c) gives 𝛆𝛆SSE=SSE0+ε^D⊤(I−HD)−1ε^D, and 𝛆𝛆ε^D⊤(I−HD)−1ε^D=δ21⊤(I−h11⊤)1=mδ2(1−mh), using 𝛆(I−h11⊤)−1ε^D=δ1 from part (b).

Each part is a way of hiding. Leverage is masked (a): however remote the cluster, each copy has leverage below 1/m; a single copy would have h⋆/(1+h⋆), near one. Residuals are masked (b): each is only 1/(1+mh⋆) of the cluster’s distance from the clean fit. Deletion is masked (c, d): deleting one copy leaves m−1 to hold the fit, recovering only 1/(1+(m−1)h⋆) of δ. Good cases are swamped (e): clean residuals shift in proportion to their cross-leverage with the cluster, and s2 is inflated.

Example (Four identical bad records)

Thirty clean cases have x uniform on (0,10) and y=2+0.5x+ε, σ=0.5. Four identical records are added at x=20, y=2, where the clean line predicts about 12 (Figure 20.5.1(a)). The slope drops from 0.514 (clean cases) to 0.007, and s rises from 0.608 to 1.632. Here h⋆=1.126 (it can exceed one, since x=20 is outside the clean design, as in Proposition 12.3.2) and δ=−10.150. Each bad case has leverage 0.205, below 1/m=0.25, where a single copy would have 0.530; residual −1.844; prediction residual −2.318; and ti=−1.28. Deleting the group changes 𝛃β^ 17.51 times as much as deleting one member.

The single-case diagnostics fail as predicted. The Bonferroni test (critical value 3.49) flags nothing, and the only case with |ti|>2 is a clean one: case 30, the clean case with largest x (9.86), has ti=2.25, because the dragged line passes below it. Cook’s distance does a little better: each bad case has Di=0.206, above 4/n=0.118 (no clean case exceeds it; the largest is 0.100) but far below the median of F(2,32). A single bad record alone would have had ti=−11.45 and Di=13.46. Deleting the four together tells the truth: their prediction residuals are all δ, and Theorem 20.5.1(d) gives F=50.6 on 4 and 28 degrees of freedom, p-value 2.0×10−12. The difficulty is not in testing the cluster but in finding it.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def single_case(X, y):
    """Leverages, residuals, s^2, externally studentized residuals and Cook's distances."""
    n, p = X.shape
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(X)
    h = np.sum(Q ** 2, axis=1)
    e = y - Q @ (Q.T @ y)
    s2 = e @ e / (n - p)
    s2_del = (e @ e - e ** 2 / (1 - h)) / (n - p - 1)
    t = e / np.sqrt(s2_del * (1 - h))
    cook = e ** 2 * h / (p * s2 * (1 - h) ** 2)
    return h, e, s2, t, cook

rng = np.random.default_rng(20)
n0, m = 30, 4                                      # clean cases, size of the cluster
x_clean = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 10, n0))
y_clean = 2 + 0.5 * x_clean + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n0)
x0, y0 = 20.0, 2.0                                 # four identical records
x = np.r_[x_clean, np.full(m, x0)]
y = np.r_[y_clean, np.full(m, y0)]
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x)), x])
n, p = X.shape
cluster = np.arange(n0, n)

h, e, s2, t, cook = single_case(X, y)
crit = stats.t.isf(0.05 / (2 * n), n - p - 1)      # Bonferroni outlier test at 5%
print("slope with the cluster:", np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0][1])
print("cluster: h =", h[-1].round(3), " t =", t[-1].round(2), " Cook's D =", cook[-1].round(3))
i = int(np.argmax(np.abs(t)))
print(f"largest |t|: case {i + 1} at x = {x[i]:.2f}, t = {t[i]:.2f}; Bonferroni cut-off {crit:.2f}")
D = cluster                                        # delete the whole cluster at once
K = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
H_D = X[D] @ K @ X[D].T
pred_D = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(m) - H_D, e[D])    # y_D minus its prediction from the rest
sse = e @ e
sse_D = sse - e[D] @ pred_D
F = (e[D] @ pred_D / m) / (sse_D / (n - p - m))
print("group prediction residuals:", pred_D.round(3))
print(f"F = {F:.1f} on {m} and {n - p - m} degrees of freedom,"
      f" p-value {stats.f.sf(F, m, n - p - m):.1e}")

20.5.3. Finding the set

There are (nd) candidate sets of each size d, far too many to search. The natural shortcut, deleting the case with the largest |ti| and repeating, starts in the wrong place: in Example (Four identical bad records) it would first remove case 30, a clean case. Three ideas get around the combinatorics. First, start from a fit the cluster cannot capture, one determined by about half the data (the next section); the cases it flags form a candidate set to examine and test, remembering that it was chosen by looking. Second, grow a clean subset: the forward search of Atkinson and Riani (2000) adds cases one at a time in order of their residuals from a robust start, and a cluster enters late and all at once, as a jump in the monitored residuals and estimates; Hadi and Simonoff (1993) proposed a related procedure. Third, unmask leverage separately: by Proposition 20.5.2(a) the hat matrix is fooled by clusters, since leverage is a Mahalanobis distance built from the sample covariance, which a cluster inflates in its own direction. Rousseeuw and van Zomeren (1990) replaced the mean and covariance by estimates that ignore up to half the cases.

Idea. Single-case diagnostics answer “what if this case alone were different?”, the wrong question when bad cases come in groups. Group deletion asks the right one, but only for a suspected group; finding the group needs a fit it cannot capture.

20.5.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Check the leverage 0.205 and the residual −1.844 in Example (Four identical bad records) from h⋆=1.126, δ=−10.150 and m=4.

Exercise (A2)

Show that Theorem 20.5.1 with D={i} gives parts (b), (d) and (e) of Theorem 20.3.1, and that FD=ti2.

Solution. With d=1, HD=hii and 𝛆ε^D=ε^i, so (a) is the prediction residual ε^i/(1−hii), (b) is Theorem 20.3.1(b), and (c) is SSE(i)=SSE−ε^i2/(1−hii). Then FD=ε^i2/{(1−hii)s(i)2}=ti2.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that for HD=h11⊤ (d×d) with dh≠1, (I−h11⊤)−1=I+h11⊤/(1−dh), and use it to recompute the group term 𝛆𝛆ε^D⊤(I−HD)−1ε^D in the proof of Proposition 20.5.2(e) from 𝛆ε^D=δ1/(1+mh⋆).

Exercise (B2)

In simple regression with clean mean x¯0 and S0=∑(xj−x¯0)2, show that hj⋆=1/n0+(xj−x¯0)(x⋆−x¯0)/S0. In Example (Four identical bad records), with x⋆ far to the right and δ<0, which clean residuals does Proposition 20.5.2(e) push up, and which down? Explain why case 30 was flagged.

Solution. Use the centred rows (1,x−x¯0), which span the same column space; then A−1=diag(1/n0,1/S0), so hj⋆=1/n0+(xj−x¯0)(x⋆−x¯0)/S0. The shift in clean residual j is −mδhj⋆/(1+mh⋆), which has the sign of hj⋆ because δ<0. Since x⋆−x¯0>0, hj⋆ grows with xj: positive on the right of the clean data and negative on the far left (once (xj−x¯0)(x⋆−x¯0)/S0<−1/n0). Residuals on the right are pushed up and those on the far left down. Case 30 is the clean case with the largest x, so it has the largest upward shift, and its studentized residual crosses two.

Exercise (B3)

In the setting of Proposition 20.5.2, delete k<m of the cluster cases. Show that each deleted case has prediction residual δ/(1+(m−k)h⋆). What does this say about searching for a cluster of unknown size by deleting sets of size k?

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Place two clusters of m identical bad cases at the two ends of a straight-line design, one above the clean line and one below. Describe, using Theorem 20.5.1, what single-case deletion, deletion of one cluster, and deletion of both clusters report. Is it possible for each cluster to mask the other?

Exercise (C2)

For a set D of cases, define 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃DD=(β^(D)−β^)⊤X⊤X(β^(D)−β^)/(ps2). Using Theorem 20.5.1(b), show that 𝛆𝛆DD=ε^D⊤(I−HD)−1HD(I−HD)−1ε^D/(ps2).

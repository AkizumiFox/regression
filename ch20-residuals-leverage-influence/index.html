Residuals, Leverage and Influence

Every result of Parts II–IV was proved for a model that holds for all n cases. A record is mistyped, one unit is unlike the others, or the model is right for most of the range and wrong at its edge. Least squares treats every case as equally credible, so a single bad case can move the fit a long way, and it can do so without leaving a large residual behind. This chapter builds the tools for finding such cases. Residuals, rescaled so that they can be compared, show cases that the model fits badly. Leverage shows cases whose position among the regressors gives them a strong pull on the fit. Deleting one case at a time, cheap by the updating formulas of Chapter 10, turns these two ingredients into exact tests and into measures of influence. Two warnings run through the chapter. Diagnostics that look at one case at a time can be defeated by a few bad cases acting together. And a flagged case is a question to be investigated, not a verdict. The last section uses a robust fit as a second opinion: a fit that the bad cases cannot capture shows them by their residuals.

What you need. Chapter 4 (independent projections), Chapter 5 (moments of fitted values and residuals, Proposition 5.5.3), Chapter 6 (the hat matrix and its properties, Proposition 6.8.1 and Proposition 6.8.2; the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem, Theorem 6.6.2), Chapter 10 (the deletion formulas, Proposition 10.6.2), Chapter 11 (t and F tests), and Chapter 13 (the Bonferroni inequality). Chapter 19 describes in general terms what a contaminated case does to least squares (Theorem 19.4.1); this chapter is about finding such cases in a particular data set.

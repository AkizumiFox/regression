20.3  Deletion diagnostics

A case cannot fairly judge itself, since its own response pulled the fit towards it. So set the case aside, fit the other n−1 cases, and ask how well that fit predicts it and how far the fit moved. Chapter 10 showed that one regression gives all n answers (Proposition 10.6.2). This section collects the formulas and proves that the externally studentized residual has an exact t law, which turns it into a test for an outlier.

20.3.1. Leave-one-out formulas

Write 𝛃β^(i) for the least squares estimate from the cases other than i, 𝛃Y^(i)=Xβ^(i) for the fitted values it gives at all n cases (including case i), SSE(i) for its residual sum of squares and s(i)2=SSE(i)/(n−p−1).

Theorem 20.3.1 (Leave-one-out formulas)

Let X have full column rank, and let X(i) be X without row i.

  • (a) X(i) has full column rank iff hii<1. Assume this from now on.

  • (b) 𝛃𝛃β^−β^(i)=(X⊤X)−1x(i)ε^i/(1−hii).

  • (c) Y^−Y^(i)=X(X⊤X)−1x(i)ε^i/(1−hii); that is, the fitted value of case j moves by hjiε^i/(1−hii). In particular case i’s own fitted value moves by hiiε^i/(1−hii).

  • (d) The prediction residual of case i is 𝛃yi−x(i)⊤β^(i)=ε^i/(1−hii).

  • (e) (n−p−1)s(i)2=(n−p)s2−ε^i2/(1−hii).

Proof.

  • (a) X(i)⊤X(i)=X⊤X−x(i)x(i)⊤, whose determinant is (1−hii)det(X⊤X) by Example (Deleting an observation). So it is nonsingular iff hii≠1, and hii≤1 always. Parts (b), (d) and (e) are Proposition 10.6.2(a), (b) and (c), the last divided through by the degrees of freedom. Part (c) is (b) multiplied by X; its jth entry is x(j)⊤(X⊤X)−1x(i)ε^i/(1−hii), and x(j)⊤(X⊤X)−1x(i)=hji.

Every quantity is a residual times a function of the design: a case with zero residual changes nothing when deleted, and high leverage magnifies a residual by 1/(1−hii). When hii=1 the case determines one direction of C(X) by itself (Proposition 6.8.1(d)), and without it a parameter is not estimable; no diagnostic can check it.

20.3.2. Prediction residuals and PRESS

The prediction residual ε^i/(1−hii) is the error in predicting yi by a fit that never saw it. Its variance is σ2/(1−hii), and dividing it by an estimate of σ/1−hii gives back ri (with s) or ti (with s(i)). The sum of squares of the prediction residuals is the PRESS statistic of Allen (1974),

PRESS=∑i=1n(ε^i1−hii)2,
(20.3.1)

a leave-one-out estimate of prediction error that Chapter 29 uses for choosing between models (Theorem 29.3.2).

Proposition 20.3.2 (Expected PRESS)

Under the second-moment model of Section 20.1, E(PRESS)=σ2∑i=1n11−hii ≥ σ2n2n−p, with equality iff all leverages equal p/n. By comparison, the expected sum of squared errors in predicting new responses at the same n design points is σ2(n+p) (Proposition 14.2.7(a)), which is smaller than σ2n2/(n−p) by σ2p2/(n−p).

Proof. E(ε^i2)=Var(ε^i)=σ2(1−hii), and dividing by (1−hii)2 and summing gives the first identity. The function u↦1/(1−u) is strictly convex on [0,1), so by Jensen’s inequality n−1∑i1/(1−hii)≥1/(1−h¯) with h¯=p/n, with equality iff all hii are equal. This gives the bound. Finally n2−(n+p)(n−p)=p2.

So PRESS overcorrects the optimism of the in-sample error (Proposition 14.2.7) slightly, because each case is predicted from a design that lacks it. It also weights cases by (1−hii)−2: at hii=0.5, four times the squared raw residual. For the state regression with all 51 jurisdictions, SSE=142.53 and PRESS=230.52 (Example (Leaving out the District of Columbia)), and the District of Columbia alone contributes 9.0662, a share of 0.36. For this design E(PRESS)/(nσ2)=1.096, against the bound n/(n−p)=1.085 and (n+p)/n=1.078: the District’s residual, not the unequal leverages, inflates PRESS.

20.3.3. The externally studentized residual

As a test statistic, ri is bounded by n−p and has a nonstandard law (Proposition 20.1.1). The externally studentized residual repairs both, and its law is clearest from the alternative it is meant to detect.

The mean-shift outlier model for case i is

𝛃𝛆𝛆Y=Xβ+Δei+ε,ε∼Nn(0,σ2I),
(20.3.2)

in which case i alone has its mean displaced by an unknown Δ, as by a misrecorded digit. Testing Δ=0 tests whether case i is an outlier.

Theorem 20.3.3 (Law of the externally studentized residual)

Assume Eq. 20.3.2 with hii<1 and n−p≥2.

  • (a) In the model with columns [X,ei], the least squares estimate of the shift is 𝛃Δ^=ε^i/(1−hii)=yi−x(i)⊤β^(i), the estimate of 𝛃β is 𝛃β^(i), the residual mean square is s(i)2, and the t statistic for Δ=0 is ti.

  • (b) ti∼t(n−p−1, Δ1−hii/σ). In particular, if Δ=0, then ti∼t(n−p−1).

  • (c) ti=ri(n−p−1)/(n−p−ri2), an increasing function of ri that maps (−n−p,n−p) onto the whole real line.

  • (d) (Bonferroni outlier test.) If no case is shifted, that is under Eq. 7.3.1, and hjj<1 for every j, then Pr{maxj|tj|>tn−p−1,α/(2n)}≤α. Equivalently, the adjusted p-value min(1,np(1)), where p(1) is the smallest of the single-case two-sided p-values from (b), is valid.

Proof.

  • (a) Since hii<1, ei∉C(X) (Proposition 6.8.1(d)), so [X,ei] has full column rank p+1. By Exercise (B1), Δ^=ε^i/(1−hii), the enlarged fit reproduces yi exactly, and its estimate of 𝛃β is 𝛃β^(i). Its residuals are therefore zero at case i and equal to the residuals of the deletion fit elsewhere, so its residual sum of squares is SSE(i) on n−p−1 degrees of freedom, and its residual mean square is s(i)2. By Eq. 6.6.1, the diagonal entry of ([X,ei]⊤[X,ei])−1 belonging to ei is 1/‖(I−M)ei‖2=1/(1−hii). So the standard error of Δ^ is s(i)/1−hii, and Δ^s(i)/1−hii=ε^i(1−hii)⋅1−hiis(i)=ti.
  • (b) The enlarged model is a normal linear model of full rank p+1<n, and Δ is one of its coefficients. By Theorem 11.6.1(a) the t statistic for Δ=0 has the t(n−p−1,δ) law with δ=Δ/(σ1/(1−hii))=Δ1−hii/σ.
  • (c) By Theorem 20.3.1(e) and ε^i2/(1−hii)=ri2s2, (n−p−1)s(i)2=s2(n−p−ri2). Hence ti2=ri2s2/s(i)2=ri2(n−p−1)/(n−p−ri2), and ti has the sign of ri. The map r↦r(n−p−1)/(n−p−r2) is increasing on (−n−p,n−p) and tends to ±∞ at the ends.
  • (d) By (b) with Δ=0, each event Aj={|tj|>tn−p−1,α/(2n)} has probability exactly α/n. Apply Theorem 13.4.1(a) to their union. The p-value form is Theorem 13.4.1(c) with every αj=α/n.

By (c), ri and ti carry the same information, but ti is unbounded and has a standard law, so it is the one to report. Exercise (B1) gives a second proof of (b). Part (b) also shows how leverage hides outliers: the noncentrality is Δ1−hii/σ, because a shift at a high-leverage case is partly absorbed by the fit. At the state design, a shift of 4σ at the District of Columbia (hii=0.506) gives noncentrality 2.81, and the Bonferroni test of (d) detects it with probability 0.258; at Missouri, the case of lowest leverage (hii=0.021), the noncentrality is 3.96 and the probability 0.666.

20.3.4. Testing for an outlier

A case suspected in advance can be tested with Theorem 20.3.3(b). Usually the suspect is chosen because its residual is largest, and the question is whether maxi|ti| is surprising under a model with no outliers. The ti are dependent, but the Bonferroni test of Theorem 20.3.3(d) needs no joint law, and it is nearly exact because two cases rarely exceed the high cut-off together. At the state design (n=51, p=4), the simulated familywise level of the 5% test is 0.0497 (standard error 0.0005, from 200000 normal samples).

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats
data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(data)), data[["poverty", "single", "urban"]]])

def max_abs_t(X, Y):
    """max_i |t_i| for each column of Y (one simulated response vector per column)."""
    n, p = X.shape
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(X)
    h = np.sum(Q ** 2, axis=1)[:, None]
    E = Y - Q @ (Q.T @ Y)
    sse = np.sum(E ** 2, axis=0)
    s2_del = (sse - E ** 2 / (1 - h)) / (n - p - 1)
    return np.max(np.abs(E) / np.sqrt(s2_del * (1 - h)), axis=0)

n, p = X.shape
alpha = 0.05
crit = stats.t.isf(alpha / (2 * n), n - p - 1)
rng = np.random.default_rng(51)
reps = 4000
T = max_abs_t(X, rng.normal(size=(n, reps)))          # no outliers: errors N(0, 1)
print(f"Bonferroni bound {alpha}, simulated familywise level {np.mean(T > crit):.4f}")
Example (Is the District of Columbia an outlier?)

For the murder-rate regression with all 51 jurisdictions, the District of Columbia has raw residual 4.475, leverage 0.506, ri=3.658 and ti=4.278 (as in Example (Leaving out the District of Columbia)); deleting it lowers s=1.741 to s(i)=1.489. Its single-case p-value is 9.4×10−5, and the Bonferroni-adjusted value 0.0048 (critical value 3.522). The next case, Mississippi, has ti=−2.877 and adjusted p-value 0.310. Among the 50 states alone the most extreme is Louisiana, ti=3.299, adjusted p-value 0.095; such sequential testing is not covered by Theorem 20.3.3(d), and Section 20.5 shows it can fail badly.

The test says that the District’s murder rate is incompatible with the linear relation that holds for the states. It does not say the number is wrong or that the case should be dropped. The District is a city, with regressors outside the range of the states; a defensible analysis restricts the population to states and says so.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats
data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data           # District of Columbia kept
y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), data[["poverty", "single", "urban"]]])
names = np.array(data.index)

n, p = X.shape
Q, R = np.linalg.qr(X)
beta = np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ y)
e = y - X @ beta                                   # raw residuals
h = np.sum(Q ** 2, axis=1)                         # leverages
s2 = e @ e / (n - p)
r = e / np.sqrt(s2 * (1 - h))                      # internally studentized
s2_del = (e @ e - e ** 2 / (1 - h)) / (n - p - 1)  # s^2 without case i
t = e / np.sqrt(s2_del * (1 - h))                  # externally studentized

p_single = 2 * stats.t.sf(np.abs(t), n - p - 1)    # one case, chosen in advance
p_bonf = np.minimum(1, n * p_single)               # Bonferroni over all n cases
crit = stats.t.isf(0.05 / (2 * n), n - p - 1)      # 5% Bonferroni critical value
for i in np.argsort(-np.abs(t))[:3]:
    print(f"{names[i]:22s} h = {h[i]:.3f}  r = {r[i]:6.3f}  t = {t[i]:6.3f}"
          f"  Bonferroni p = {p_bonf[i]:.4f}")
print(f"critical value |t| > {crit:.3f}")

Warning. An outlier test tests the model at one case, including normality, constant variance and the mean function. A large |ti| can come from a gross error, from heavy-tailed errors, from variance that grows where case i lies, or from a wrong mean function there, and the test cannot tell these apart (Chapter 21, Chapter 22).

Exercise (A1)

Use Theorem 20.3.3(c) to compute the District of Columbia’s ti from its ri=3.658, with n=51 and p=4.

Solution. ti=3.65846/(47−3.6582)=3.65846/33.62=3.658×1.1697≈4.279, which agrees with the recorded 4.278 to the rounding of ri.

A case has leverage 0.5 and residual 1. By how much does its own fitted value change when it is deleted, and what is its prediction residual? What is the prediction residual of a case with leverage 0.9 and the same raw residual?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Prove Theorem 20.3.3(b) with Δ=0 directly. Show that 𝛃yi−x(i)⊤β^(i) is independent of s(i)2, that its variance is σ2(1+x(i)⊤(X(i)⊤X(i))−1x(i)), and that this equals σ2/(1−hii).

Solution. The fit without case i is a normal linear model for the other n−1 cases, and yi is independent of those cases. So 𝛃yi−x(i)⊤β^(i) is the error of predicting a new observation at x(i), and by Theorem 12.3.1(a) it is normal with mean zero and variance σ2(1+g), g=x(i)⊤(X(i)⊤X(i))−1x(i), independent of s(i)2, with 𝛃(yi−x(i)⊤β^(i))/(s(i)1+g)∼t(n−p−1). By Example (Deleting an observation), (X(i)⊤X(i))−1=(X⊤X)−1+(X⊤X)−1x(i)x(i)⊤(X⊤X)−1/(1−hii), so g=hii+hii2/(1−hii)=hii/(1−hii) and 1+g=1/(1−hii). Using Theorem 20.3.1(d), the statistic is [ε^i/(1−hii)]1−hii/s(i)=ti.

Exercise (B2)

Show that s(i)<s iff ri2>1. Interpret.

Exercise (B3)

In the setting of Theorem 20.3.3, the shift at case i is detected by the single-case test with probability Pr{|T|>c}, T∼t(n−p−1,Δ1−hii/σ). Show that for fixed Δ and c this probability decreases as hii increases. Hint: T2 has a noncentral F law; use Theorem 4.1.4.

1.7  Eigenvalues, the spectral theorem and positive definiteness

1.7.1. Eigenvalues

A scalar λ is an eigenvalue of the n×n matrix A, with eigenvector x≠0, if Ax=λx. Equivalently, A−λI is singular, that is, λ is a root of the characteristic polynomial p(λ)=det(λI−A). This polynomial has degree n and leading coefficient one, so over the complex numbers it factors as ∏i=1n(λ−λi). The eigenvalues λ1,…,λn are listed with multiplicity and may be complex when A is not symmetric.

Proposition 1.7.1

Let A be n×n with eigenvalues λ1,…,λn.

  • (a) detA=∏iλi and trA=∑iλi.

  • (b) If Ax=λx, then f(A)x=f(λ)x for every polynomial f. If A is nonsingular, A−1x=λ−1x.

  • (c) A and P−1AP have the same characteristic polynomial for nonsingular P.

  • (d) If C is m×n and D is n×m, then λndet(λIm−CD)=λmdet(λIn−DC). So CD and DC have the same nonzero eigenvalues, with the same multiplicities.

Proof.

  • (a) Setting λ=0 in det(λI−A)=∏(λ−λi) gives (−1)ndetA=(−1)n∏λi. For the trace, compare coefficients of λn−1. On the right it is −∑λi. On the left, in the permutation expansion of det(λI−A), only the product of diagonal entries ∏i(λ−aii) contains λn−1, because every other term omits at least two diagonal entries. Its coefficient is −∑aii.
  • (b) Akx=λkx by induction, and x=A−1Ax=λA−1x with λ≠0. (c) det(λI−P−1AP)=det(P−1(λI−A)P)=det(λI−A).
  • (c) For λ≠0, Theorem 1.6.3(c) gives det(Im−λ−1CD)=det(In−λ−1DC). Multiplying by λm+n gives the identity for λ≠0. Both sides are polynomials, so it holds for all λ.

1.7.2. The spectral theorem

Symmetric matrices have the best possible eigenstructure, and almost every matrix whose eigenvalues matter in statistics is symmetric.

Theorem 1.7.2 (Spectral theorem)

Let A be an n×n symmetric matrix. Then all eigenvalues of A are real, and there is an orthogonal matrix Q=[q1,…,qn] with

A=QΛQ⊤=∑i=1nλiqiqi⊤,Λ=diag(λ1,…,λn).
(1.7.1)

The columns of Q are an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, Aqi=λiqi. Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Proof. Real eigenvalues. Let Az=λz with z∈Cn nonzero, and let z¯ be the complex conjugate. The scalar s=z¯⊤Az is real, since its conjugate is z⊤Az¯=(z⊤Az¯)⊤=z¯⊤Az=s by symmetry. Also s=λz¯⊤z=λ∑i|zi|2, with the sum real and positive. So λ is real. The real singular matrix A−λI then has a real nonzero null vector, so there is a real eigenvector.

Diagonalization. By induction on n, the case n=1 being trivial. Let q1 be a real unit eigenvector for λ1 and extend it to an orthogonal matrix [q1,Q2] (Section 1.5). Since Q2⊤Aq1=λ1Q2⊤q1=0 and A is symmetric, [q1,Q2]⊤A[q1,Q2]=(λ10⊤0Q2⊤AQ2). The (n−1)×(n−1) block is symmetric, so by induction Q2⊤AQ2=PΛ2P⊤ with P orthogonal. Then Q=[q1,Q2P] is orthogonal (Proposition 1.5.3(b)) and Q⊤AQ=diag(λ1,Λ2).

Orthogonality. If Au=λu and Av=μv, then λu⊤v=u⊤Av=μu⊤v, so u⊤v=0 when λ≠μ.

Unless stated otherwise, the eigenvalues of symmetric A are ordered as λ1≥λ2≥⋯≥λn, and we write λmax=λ1 and λmin=λn. The following consequences are used without comment later on.

Corollary 1.7.3

Let A=QΛQ⊤ be symmetric.

  • (a) rank(A) counts the nonzero eigenvalues. C(A) is spanned by the qi with λi≠0, and N(A) by those with λi=0.

  • (b) Ak=QΛkQ⊤ for k=1,2,…. If A is nonsingular, A−1=QΛ−1Q⊤, and A+=QΛ+Q⊤ in general, where Λ+ inverts the nonzero diagonal entries.

  • (c) The eigenspace {x:Ax=λx} has dimension equal to the multiplicity of λ as a root of the characteristic polynomial.

Proof. Multiplying by the nonsingular Q and Q⊤ changes neither rank (Proposition 1.2.3(b)) nor, after the change of variable x=Qc, the structure of null spaces and eigenspaces. So each statement reduces to the diagonal matrix Λ, where it is immediate. For (b), Q⊤Q=I collapses the middle factors, and the four Penrose conditions for QΛ+Q⊤ reduce to those for Λ+.

Example (Eigenvalues of the equicorrelation matrix)

For E=(1−ρ)I+ρJn of Example (Equicorrelation), E1=(1+(n−1)ρ)1, and Ev=(1−ρ)v for every v⊥1. So the eigenvalues are 1+(n−1)ρ once and 1−ρ with multiplicity n−1, with eigenvectors 1/n and the last n−1 rows of the Helmert matrix (Example (Helmert matrices)). The determinant and inverse found earlier follow at once from Proposition 1.7.1 and Corollary 1.7.3(b).

1.7.3. Positive definite and nonnegative definite matrices

Definition 1.7.1

A symmetric matrix A is positive definite if x⊤Ax>0 for every x≠0, and nonnegative definite if x⊤Ax≥0 for every x. For symmetric A,B of the same size we write A⪰B if A−B is nonnegative definite, and A≻B if it is positive definite (the Loewner order).

In this book, “nonnegative definite” includes positive definite. Some texts, Rencher and Schaalje among them, reserve “positive semidefinite” for matrices that are nonnegative definite but singular. We use the terms only for symmetric matrices. Covariance matrices are nonnegative definite (Chapter 2), and Gram matrices X⊤X are too, since x⊤X⊤Xx=‖Xx‖2.

Theorem 1.7.4 (Characterizations)

For a symmetric n×n matrix A the following are equivalent:

  • (a) A is positive definite;

  • (b) every eigenvalue of A is positive;

  • (c) A=B⊤B for some matrix B with n columns and full column rank;

  • (d) A=LL⊤ with L lower triangular with positive diagonal entries (the Cholesky factorization; L is unique);

  • (e) every leading principal minor detA[k], k=1,…,n, is positive, where A[k] is the upper left k×k block.

Likewise, the following are equivalent: (a′) A is nonnegative definite; (b′) every eigenvalue is nonnegative; (c′) A=B⊤B for some B, which can be taken of size rank(A)×n; (e′) every principal minor (determinant of a submatrix with the same row and column indices) is nonnegative.

Proof. Write A=QΛQ⊤. (a)⇒(b): λi=qi⊤Aqi>0. (b)⇒(c): B=Λ1/2Q⊤ is nonsingular. (c)⇒(a): x⊤B⊤Bx=‖Bx‖2, which is positive for x≠0 by full column rank. (d)⇒(c) is immediate, with B=L⊤.

(a)⇒(d), by induction on n. Write A=(ab⊤bA2). Then a=e1⊤Ae1>0. The Schur complement A2−bb⊤/a is positive definite: for v≠0, put x=(−b⊤v/a, v⊤)⊤ and expand to get x⊤Ax=v⊤(A2−bb⊤/a)v>0. By induction A2−bb⊤/a=L2L2⊤, and L=(a0⊤b/aL2) satisfies LL⊤=A. For uniqueness, if LL⊤=KK⊤, then K−1L=K⊤(L⊤)−1 is both lower and upper triangular, hence diagonal, say D. From K−1L=D and LL⊤=KK⊤ we get DD⊤=I, so D=I because its diagonal is positive.

(a)⇒(e): A[k]=E⊤AE with E=[e1,…,ek] is positive definite, since x⊤A[k]x=(Ex)⊤A(Ex). By (b) and Proposition 1.7.1(a) its determinant is positive. (e)⇒(a), by induction on n. The block A[n−1] satisfies (e) and so is positive definite, so by (a)⇒(d), which is already proved, A[n−1]=LL⊤. Write A=(A[n−1]cc⊤d) and s=d−c⊤A[n−1]−1c. By Theorem 1.6.3(b), s=detA/detA[n−1]>0. With T=(IA[n−1]−1c0⊤1), a direct check gives A=T⊤diag(A[n−1],s)T=B⊤B with B=diag(L⊤,s)T nonsingular, so A is positive definite by (c)⇒(a).

The primed statements: (a′)⇔(b′)⇔(c′) exactly as above, taking for B the rows of Λ1/2Q⊤ that belong to nonzero eigenvalues. (a′)⇒(e′) as above, with an arbitrary index set in place of {1,…,k}. For (e′)⇒(a′), fix t>0. Each column of A[k]+tI is a column of A[k] plus t times a coordinate vector. Expanding the determinant by linearity in each column gives det(A[k]+tI)=∑Stk−|S|detASS, the sum over all subsets S⊆{1,…,k}, with detA∅∅=1. Every term is nonnegative and the term for S=∅ is tk>0. So A+tI is positive definite by (e)⇒(a), and x⊤Ax=limt→0x⊤(A+tI)x≥0.

Two warnings. Leading principal minors do not suffice for nonnegative definiteness: diag(0,−1) has leading minors 0 and 0. And (c′) holds with many matrices B. The Cholesky factor and the symmetric square root below are two convenient choices.

Proposition 1.7.5

Let A be n×n nonnegative definite.

  • (a) aii≥0, and if aii=0 then row i and column i of A are zero.

  • (b) x⊤Ax=0 iff Ax=0.

  • (c) B⊤AB is nonnegative definite for any n×q matrix B. If A is positive definite, B⊤AB is positive definite iff B has full column rank.

  • (d) Every principal submatrix of A is nonnegative definite, and positive definite if A is.

  • (e) If A is positive definite, it is nonsingular and A−1 is positive definite. If also A is partitioned as in Eq. 1.6.2, both Schur complements A11⋅2 and A22⋅1 are positive definite.

  • (f) If A⪰B≻0, then B−1⪰A−1.

Proof.

  • (b) Write A=B⊤B by Theorem 1.7.4(c′). Then x⊤Ax=‖Bx‖2, which vanishes iff Bx=0, which implies Ax=0.
  • (c) aii=ei⊤Aei≥0. If aii=0 then Aei=0 by (b), so column i is zero, and row i by symmetry.
  • (d) x⊤B⊤ABx=(Bx)⊤A(Bx) is nonnegative, and when A is positive definite it is zero only if Bx=0.
  • (e) is (c) with B a set of columns of I.
  • (f) The eigenvalues of A−1 are 1/λi>0. The inverse in Eq. 1.6.4 has A22⋅1−1 as a principal submatrix, which is positive definite by (d). The other Schur complement follows by symmetry of the argument.
  • (g) By Theorem 1.7.6 below, B=B1/2B1/2. Then C=B−1/2AB−1/2⪰I by (c), so every eigenvalue of C is at least 1 and every eigenvalue of C−1=B1/2A−1B1/2 is at most 1. So I⪰B1/2A−1B1/2, and multiplying on both sides by B−1/2 gives B−1⪰A−1 by (c).

Part (f) is used in Chapter 7: an estimator whose covariance matrix is smaller in the Loewner order has smaller variance for every linear combination of its components.

Theorem 1.7.6 (Square root)

A nonnegative definite matrix A=QΛQ⊤ has exactly one nonnegative definite matrix B with B2=A, namely A1/2=QΛ1/2Q⊤. It satisfies C(A1/2)=C(A). If A is positive definite, A1/2 is positive definite and its inverse is A−1/2=QΛ−1/2Q⊤.

Proof. A1/2 is symmetric with nonnegative eigenvalues and (QΛ1/2Q⊤)2=QΛQ⊤. Its column space is spanned by the qi with λi>0, as is C(A) (Corollary 1.7.3(a)). For uniqueness, let B be nonnegative definite with B2=A, and write B=PΔP⊤ with Δ=diag(δi), δi≥0. Then A=PΔ2P⊤. Let Av=λv and expand v=∑icipi. Comparing Av=∑iciδi2pi with λv gives ci(δi2−λ)=0, so ci≠0 only if δi=λ. Hence Bv=∑iciδipi=λv. Applying this to each qj gives BQ=QΛ1/2, that is, B=A1/2. The last statement is Corollary 1.7.3(b).

1.7.4. Extremal properties of eigenvalues

Theorem 1.7.7 (Rayleigh quotient)

Let A be symmetric with eigenvalues λ1≥⋯≥λn and orthonormal eigenvectors q1,…,qn. For every x≠0,

λn≤x⊤Axx⊤x≤λ1,
(1.7.2)

with equality on the right at x=q1 and on the left at x=qn. More generally, the maximum of x⊤Ax/x⊤x over nonzero x orthogonal to q1,…,qk−1 is λk, attained at qk.

Proof. Put c=Q⊤x, so that x⊤x=c⊤c and x⊤Axx⊤x=∑iλici2∑ici2, a weighted average of the eigenvalues with weights ci2/∑jcj2. It lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalue, and equals λ1 when all weight is on c1, that is, at x=q1. The condition x⊥q1,…,qk−1 means c1=⋯=ck−1=0, so the average is then over λk,…,λn, whose largest member is λk.

Figure 1.7.1 shows the theorem for A=(2.5111.5). The eigenvalues are 3.118 and 0.882, with eigenvectors at angles 31.7∘ and 121.7∘. As x turns around the unit circle, the quotient swings between the two eigenvalues. The ellipse x⊤Ax=1 has its principal axes along the eigenvectors, with semi-axis lengths λi−1/2. Consequently the Rayleigh quotient is largest in the direction in which the ellipse is narrowest.

Corollary 1.7.8 (Relative to a positive definite matrix)

Let A be symmetric and B positive definite, both n×n.

  • (a) maxx≠0x⊤Ax/x⊤Bx is the largest eigenvalue of B−1A, which has the same eigenvalues as the symmetric B−1/2AB−1/2.

  • (b) There is a nonsingular P with P⊤BP=I and P⊤AP diagonal.

  • (c) For a∈Rn, maxx≠0(a⊤x)2/x⊤Bx=a⊤B−1a, attained at x=B−1a.

Proof. Substitute x=B−1/2z. The quotient in (a) becomes z⊤B−1/2AB−1/2z/z⊤z, to which Theorem 1.7.7 applies, and B−1A=B−1/2(B−1/2AB−1/2)B1/2 is similar to it (Proposition 1.7.1(c)). For (b), if B−1/2AB−1/2=QΛQ⊤, take P=B−1/2Q. For (c), the matrix A=aa⊤ has B−1/2aa⊤B−1/2 of rank at most one, whose only nonzero eigenvalue is its trace a⊤B−1a. Substituting x=B−1a attains it.

Part (c) is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the inner product x⊤Bv. It is the algebra behind simultaneous confidence intervals (Section 12.2), and part (b) is used for linear discriminants and for comparing two covariance matrices.

1.7.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that (abbc) is positive definite iff a>0 and ac>b2, and nonnegative definite iff a≥0, c≥0 and ac≥b2. Why is a≥0 and ac≥b2 not enough?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let A⪰B≻0. Show that detA≥detB and trA≥trB.

Exercise (B2)

Let X=[X1,X2] have full column rank. Show that the leading block of (X⊤X)−1 satisfies [(X⊤X)−1]11⪰(X1⊤X1)−1, with equality iff X1⊤X2=0. Interpret this for the variances of least squares coefficients when regressors are added to a model.

Solution. Write A=X⊤X in blocks. By Theorem 1.6.4, [A−1]11=A11⋅2−1. Now A11−A11⋅2=A12A22−1A21 is nonnegative definite by Proposition 1.7.5(c,e), and A11⋅2 is positive definite. So A11⪰A11⋅2≻0, and Proposition 1.7.5(f) gives A11⋅2−1⪰A11−1. Equality forces A12A22−1A21=0, hence A21x=0 for all x by Proposition 1.7.5(b), that is, X2⊤X1=0. When Cov(Y)=σ2I, the covariance matrix of the coefficients of X1 is σ2[A−1]11 in the larger model and σ2A11−1 in the smaller one. Adding regressors never decreases the variance of any linear combination of the original coefficients, and leaves it unchanged only when the new regressors are orthogonal to the old ones.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let A be symmetric with eigenvalues λ1≥⋯≥λn. Prove the Courant–Fischer theorem λk=maxdim⁡S=k min0≠x∈Sx⊤Axx⊤x. Deduce that if B is obtained from A by deleting one row and the corresponding column, its eigenvalues μ1≥⋯≥μn−1 interlace: λk≥μk≥λk+1.

Exercise (C2)

Let S be positive definite of size p×p. Show that g(Σ)=−log⁡detΣ−tr(Σ−1S), over positive definite Σ, is maximized uniquely at Σ=S. Hint: write g in terms of the eigenvalues of S−1/2ΣS−1/2.

Solution. Let M=S−1/2ΣS−1/2, which is positive definite with eigenvalues μ1,…,μp>0. Then detΣ=detSdetM and tr(Σ−1S)=tr(S1/2Σ−1S1/2)=tr(M−1). So g(Σ)=−log⁡detS−∑i=1p(log⁡μi+1μi). The function μ↦log⁡μ+1/μ has derivative (μ−1)/μ2 and a unique minimum at μ=1. So g is maximized iff every μi=1, that is, M=I and Σ=S. The maximum is −log⁡detS−p.

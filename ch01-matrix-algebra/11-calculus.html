1.11  Vector and matrix calculus

1.11.1. Gradients of linear and quadratic functions

For a differentiable scalar function f of x∈Rn, the gradient ∂f/∂x is the column vector with entries ∂f/∂xi, and the Hessian ∂2f/∂x∂x⊤ is the n×n matrix of second derivatives. For a vector-valued g(x)∈Rm, the m×n Jacobian ∂g/∂x⊤ has (i,j) entry ∂gi/∂xj.

Theorem 1.11.1 (Derivatives of linear and quadratic forms)

Let a∈Rn, let A be n×n and B be m×n. Then ∂(a⊤x)∂x=a,∂(Bx)∂x⊤=B,∂(x⊤Ax)∂x=(A+A⊤)x,∂2(x⊤Ax)∂x∂x⊤=A+A⊤. In particular ∂(x⊤Ax)/∂x=2Ax when A is symmetric.

Proof. The first two are immediate from a⊤x=∑iaixi and (Bx)i=∑jbijxj. For the quadratic form, expand (x+h)⊤A(x+h)=x⊤Ax+h⊤(A+A⊤)x+h⊤Ah. The middle term is linear in h and the last is O(‖h‖2), so (A+A⊤)x is the gradient. The gradient is linear in x with matrix A+A⊤, which is the Hessian by the second formula.

Example (The least squares criterion)

Let S(b)=‖y−Xb‖2=y⊤y−2y⊤Xb+b⊤X⊤Xb. By Theorem 1.11.1, ∂S∂b=−2X⊤y+2X⊤Xb=−2X⊤(y−Xb),∂2S∂b∂b⊤=2X⊤X. Setting the gradient to zero gives the normal equations X⊤Xb=X⊤y. The Hessian is nonnegative definite, so S is convex and every solution is a global minimizer. The next result proves this without calculus, and covers the rank-deficient case.

Proposition 1.11.2 (Minimizing a quadratic function)

Let A be n×n nonnegative definite, b∈Rn and f(x)=x⊤Ax−2b⊤x. If b∈C(A), the minimizers of f are exactly the solutions of Ax=b, and the minimum value is −b⊤A−b for any generalized inverse A−. If b∉C(A), then f is unbounded below.

Proof. Let b∈C(A) and let x0 solve Ax0=b. Completing the square, f(x)=(x−x0)⊤A(x−x0)−x0⊤Ax0. The first term is nonnegative, and by Proposition 1.7.5(b) it is zero iff A(x−x0)=0, that is, iff Ax=b. The minimum is −x0⊤Ax0=−x0⊤AA−Ax0=−b⊤A−b. If b∉C(A), write b=b1+b2 with b1∈C(A) and 0≠b2∈C(A)⊥=N(A) (Proposition 1.5.1). Then f(tb2)=−2t‖b2‖2→−∞ as t→∞.

With A=X⊤X and b=X⊤y, the proposition gives least squares for every model matrix: the minimizers are the solutions of the normal equations, and the minimum of S is y⊤y−y⊤X(X⊤X)−X⊤y.

1.11.2. Constrained minimization

To minimize a differentiable f(x) subject to linear constraints Hx=h, with H of size q×n, the method of Lagrange multipliers looks for stationary points of 𝛉f(x)+2θ⊤(Hx−h) in 𝛉(x,θ). The factor 2 is only for convenience. For quadratic f the stationary point can be written down and checked directly.

Proposition 1.11.3

Let A be positive definite, H of size q×n with full row rank, and f(x)=x⊤Ax−2b⊤x. The unique minimizer of f subject to Hx=h is x∗=x0−A−1H⊤(HA−1H⊤)−1(Hx0−h),x0=A−1b.

Proof. HA−1H⊤ is positive definite by Proposition 1.7.5(c,e), so x∗ is well defined, and Hx∗=h by direct substitution. Stationarity of the Lagrangian gives 𝛉2Ax−2b+2H⊤θ=0. Indeed 𝛉Ax∗−b=−H⊤θ∗ with 𝛉θ∗=(HA−1H⊤)−1(Hx0−h). For any feasible x, put d=x−x∗, so that Hd=0. Then 𝛉f(x)=f(x∗)+2d⊤(Ax∗−b)+d⊤Ad=f(x∗)−2(Hd)⊤θ∗+d⊤Ad=f(x∗)+d⊤Ad, which exceeds f(x∗) unless d=0.

With A=X⊤X and b=X⊤y, this is least squares subject to a linear hypothesis 𝛃Hβ=h, the starting point of Chapter 11.

1.11.3. Derivatives with respect to matrices and scalars

Likelihoods for normal models involve log⁡detΣ and tr(Σ−1S). For a scalar function f of an m×n matrix W with functionally independent entries, ∂f/∂W is the m×n matrix with entries ∂f/∂wij.

Proposition 1.11.4

  • (a) ∂tr(AW)/∂W=A⊤.

  • (b) If detW>0, then ∂log⁡detW/∂W=(W−1)⊤.

  • (c) If A(t) is a nonsingular matrix whose entries are differentiable functions of a scalar t, with derivative A˙=∂A/∂t, then ∂A−1∂t=−A−1A˙A−1,∂log⁡|detA|∂t=tr(A−1A˙).

Proof.

  • (a) tr(AW)=∑i,jajiwij. (c) Differentiate AA−1=I by the product rule to get A˙A−1+A∂A−1/∂t=0. For the determinant, A(t+h)=A(t)(I+hA−1A˙+o(h)). In the permutation expansion of det(I+hC) only the diagonal product contributes terms of first order in h, so det(I+hC)=1+htrC+O(h2). Hence detA(t+h)=detA(t)(1+htr(A−1A˙)+o(h)), and taking logarithms gives the formula. (b) Apply (c) with A(t)=W+teiej⊤. Then tr(W−1eiej⊤)=ej⊤W−1ei, the (j,i) entry of W−1.

When W is constrained to be symmetric, wij and wji are the same variable, so the derivatives with respect to off-diagonal entries double. Some books (Rencher and Schaalje 2008) report formulas of the type 2W−1−diag(W−1) for this reason. The simplest way to avoid the issue is to differentiate along a symmetric direction W+tE, as in (c). This is the convenient route for the normal likelihoods of Chapter 32. The listing checks the gradient formulas against central finite differences, and the script also checks part (c) and Proposition 1.11.2. The largest discrepancy in the listing is 3×10−9, which is the size one expects from the finite-difference approximation.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
p = 4
def num_grad(f, x, h=1e-6):
    """Central-difference gradient of a scalar function of a vector or matrix."""
    g = np.zeros_like(x)
    for idx in np.ndindex(x.shape):
        e = np.zeros_like(x)
        e[idx] = h
        g[idx] = (f(x + e) - f(x - e)) / (2 * h)
    return g

a = rng.normal(size=p)
A = rng.normal(size=(p, p))                         # not symmetric
x = rng.normal(size=p)
checks = {
    "a'x":        (num_grad(lambda v: a @ v, x), a),
    "x'Ax":       (num_grad(lambda v: v @ A @ v, x), (A + A.T) @ x),
}
X = rng.normal(size=(20, p))
y = rng.normal(size=20)
b = rng.normal(size=p)
grad_ls = -2 * X.T @ (y - X @ b)
checks["||y - Xb||^2"] = (num_grad(lambda v: np.sum((y - X @ v) ** 2), b), grad_ls)

S = rng.normal(size=(p, p)); S = S @ S.T + p * np.eye(p)   # positive definite
checks["log det S"] = (num_grad(lambda W: np.linalg.slogdet(W)[1], S), np.linalg.inv(S).T)
checks["tr(AS)"] = (num_grad(lambda W: np.trace(A @ W), S), A.T)
for name, (numeric, formula) in checks.items():
    print(f"{name:14s} max error {np.abs(numeric - formula).max():.1e}")

1.11.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Observations fall in a clusters of size b, and Σ=σ2Ia⊗Eb, where Eb=(1−ρ)Ib+ρJb. Find the eigenvalues, determinant and inverse of Σ using Proposition 1.10.1, Example (Equicorrelation) and Example (Eigenvalues of the equicorrelation matrix).

Exercise (A2)

Use Proposition 1.11.2 to show that ‖y−Xb‖2+λ‖b‖2 with λ>0 has the unique minimizer (X⊤X+λI)−1X⊤y, whatever the rank of X.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let X have full column rank, 𝛃β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y, and let H be q×p with full row rank. Show that the minimizer of ‖y−Xb‖2 subject to Hb=h is 𝛃𝛃𝛃β^H=β^−(X⊤X)−1H⊤W−1(Hβ^−h),W=H(X⊤X)−1H⊤, and that the increase in the residual sum of squares is 𝛃𝛃(Hβ^−h)⊤W−1(Hβ^−h).

Solution. Apply Proposition 1.11.3 with A=X⊤X and b=X⊤y. Since ‖y−Xb‖2=y⊤y+f(b) and 𝛃x0=β^, this gives 𝛃β^H. By completing the square as in Proposition 1.11.2, 𝛃𝛃𝛃‖y−Xb‖2=‖y−Xβ^‖2+(b−β^)⊤X⊤X(b−β^) for every b. With 𝛃𝛃b−β^=−(X⊤X)−1H⊤W−1(Hβ^−h), the increase is 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃(Hβ^−h)⊤W−1H(X⊤X)−1H⊤W−1(Hβ^−h)=(Hβ^−h)⊤W−1(Hβ^−h). This quadratic form is the numerator of the F statistic in Chapter 11 (Theorem 11.3.2).

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let X be n×p of any rank, write A=X⊤X, and for λ>0 let 𝛃β^(λ)=(A+λI)−1X⊤y be the minimizer of Exercise (A2). Show that

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃∂β^∂λ=−(A+λI)−1β^(λ);

  • (b) 𝛃𝛃X⊤(y−Xβ^(λ))=λβ^(λ);

  • (c) 𝛃‖β^(λ)‖2 is strictly decreasing in λ, and the residual sum of squares 𝛃‖y−Xβ^(λ)‖2 is strictly increasing, unless X⊤y=0;

  • (d) 𝛃β^(λ)→0 as λ→∞.

So the ridge path trades fit for size monotonically, which is what makes a single tuning parameter a sensible thing to choose (Chapter 27).

Solution.

  • (a) By Proposition 1.11.4(c) with A(λ)=A+λI and A˙=I, the derivative of (A+λI)−1 is −(A+λI)−2, so 𝛃𝛃∂β^/∂λ=−(A+λI)−2X⊤y=−(A+λI)−1β^.

  • (b) 𝛃𝛃𝛃X⊤y=(A+λI)β^=X⊤Xβ^+λβ^, so 𝛃𝛃X⊤y−X⊤Xβ^=λβ^.

  • (c) Write K=(A+λI)−1, which is positive definite. Then 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃∂∂λ‖β^‖2=2β^⊤∂β^∂λ=−2β^⊤Kβ^<0 unless 𝛃β^=0, which by (b) happens only when X⊤y=0, and then 𝛃β^(λ)=0 for every λ. For the residual sum of squares, using (b), 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃∂∂λ‖y−Xβ^‖2=−2(y−Xβ^)⊤X∂β^∂λ=2λβ^⊤Kβ^>0 under the same proviso.

  • (d) Every eigenvalue of A+λI is at least λ, so ‖K‖2≤1/λ and 𝛃‖β^(λ)‖≤‖X⊤y‖/λ→0.

Exercise (C2)

Show that W↦log⁡detW is strictly concave on the positive definite matrices: for positive definite W and symmetric E≠0, the function f(t)=log⁡det(W+tE) has f″(t)<0 wherever W+tE is positive definite. Deduce that Ω↦log⁡detΩ−tr(ΩS), with S positive definite, is strictly concave in the concentration matrix Ω=Σ−1, that its only stationary point is Ω=S−1, and hence that the maximum found in Exercise (C2) is unique. Hint: differentiate along symmetric directions, as the text recommends, and use Theorem 1.7.6 and Theorem 1.6.1.

Solution. Write M(t)=(W+tE)−1. By Proposition 1.11.4(c), f′(t)=tr(ME) and M′(t)=−MEM, so f″(t)=tr(M′E)=−tr(MEME)=−tr((M1/2EM1/2)2)=−‖M1/2EM1/2‖F2, using the cyclic property to move one factor M1/2 from the front to the back and tr(F2)=tr(F⊤F)=‖F‖F2 for symmetric F. Since M1/2 is nonsingular, M1/2EM1/2=0 only for E=0, so f″(t)<0. The positive definite matrices form a convex set, and strict concavity along every line segment in it is exactly strict concavity.

Adding the linear term −tr(ΩS) does not change the second derivative, so g(Ω)=log⁡detΩ−tr(ΩS) is strictly concave. Differentiating along a symmetric direction E, ∂∂tg(Ω+tE)|t=0=tr(Ω−1E)−tr(SE)=tr{(Ω−1−S)E}. This vanishes for every symmetric E iff Ω−1=S: take E=Ω−1−S, which is symmetric, and the trace becomes ‖Ω−1−S‖F2. A strictly concave function has at most one stationary point and no other local maximum, so Ω=S−1, that is, Σ=S, is the unique maximizer, as Exercise (C2) found by a different route.

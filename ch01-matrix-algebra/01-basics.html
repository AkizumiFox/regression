1.1  Vectors, matrices and the operations we use

This chapter works in Rn and with real matrices only. It fixes the conventions, states the results later chapters cite, and proves them. A reader who already knows linear algebra can go straight to the results that are specific to statistics: generalized inverses (Section 1.3 and Section 1.4), Schur complements and the Woodbury identity (Section 1.6), nonnegative definite matrices (Section 1.7), conditioning (Section 1.8) and idempotent matrices (Section 1.9). Orthogonal projections, the geometric heart of least squares, get only the minimum here. Chapter 6 develops them.

1.1.1. Conventions

An m×n matrix A=(aij) has m rows and n columns. We write A=[a1,…,an] for its columns and a(i)⊤ for its ith row. A vector is always a column, so x∈Rn is an n×1 matrix and x⊤ is a row. Bold upright letters denote vectors and matrices, and italic letters denote scalars. The special matrices we use constantly are the identity In, the zero matrix 0, the vector of ones 1n, the matrix of ones Jn=1n1n⊤, and the coordinate vectors e1,…,en, the columns of In. Subscripts giving sizes are dropped when the size is clear. diag(d1,…,dn) is the diagonal matrix with the given diagonal entries. A square matrix is symmetric if A⊤=A, upper triangular if aij=0 for i>j, and lower triangular if A⊤ is upper triangular.

The transpose satisfies (A⊤)⊤=A, (A+B)⊤=A⊤+B⊤ and, most usefully,

(AB)⊤=B⊤A⊤.
(1.1.1)

1.1.2. Products as linear combinations

If A is m×n and B is n×q, the (i,j) entry of AB is ∑kaikbkj. Three equivalent readings of the product carry most of the arguments in this book:

Ab=∑j=1nbjaj,AB=[Ab1,…,Abq],AB=∑k=1nakb(k)⊤.
(1.1.2)

The first says that Ab combines the columns of A, with weights taken from b. The second says that each column of AB is such a combination. The third writes a product as a sum of n rank-one matrices (outer products), and is the form in which spectral and singular value decompositions are usually used. Transposing the first reading shows that each row of AB is a combination of the rows of B.

Matrices partitioned into conformable blocks multiply as if the blocks were scalars, as long as the order of factors is kept: (A11A12A21A22)(B11B12B21B22)=(A11B11+A12B21A11B12+A12B22A21B11+A22B21A21B12+A22B22). Multiplication is associative and distributive but not commutative, and AB=0 does not force A=0 or B=0. The Hadamard product A∘B=(aijbij) multiplies entrywise. It appears only occasionally.

1.1.3. Inner products, lengths and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

The inner product of u,v∈Rn is ⟨u,v⟩=u⊤v=∑iuivi, and the Euclidean norm is ‖u‖=(u⊤u)1/2. The entries of A⊤A are the inner products of the columns of A, and those of AA⊤ are the inner products of its rows. In particular

∑i,jaij2=∑j‖aj‖2,
(1.1.3)

so A⊤A=0 forces every column to be zero, that is, A=0. The square root of Eq. 1.1.3 is the Frobenius norm ‖A‖F.

Proposition 1.1.1 (Cauchy–Schwarz)

For u,v∈Rn, (u⊤v)2≤(u⊤u)(v⊤v), with equality iff one vector is a multiple of the other. Consequently ‖u+v‖≤‖u‖+‖v‖.

Proof. If v=0 there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, the quadratic q(t)=‖u−tv‖2=u⊤u−2tu⊤v+t2v⊤v is nonnegative for all t, so its discriminant 4(u⊤v)2−4(u⊤u)(v⊤v) is at most zero. Equality holds iff q has a real root, that is, iff u=tv for some t. The triangle inequality follows by expanding ‖u+v‖2 and bounding the cross term.

1.1.4. Quadratic forms

A quadratic form in x∈Rn is a function x⊤Ax=∑i,jaijxixj. Since x⊤Ax is a scalar it equals its own transpose x⊤A⊤x, so

x⊤Ax=x⊤(12(A+A⊤))x.
(1.1.4)

Every quadratic form therefore has a symmetric matrix, and that matrix is unique: if x⊤Sx=0 for all x with S symmetric, then taking x=ei gives sii=0, and x=ei+ej gives 2sij=0. From now on we always take the matrix in a quadratic form to be symmetric.

Example (Sums of squares as quadratic forms)

The sum of squared deviations of y∈Rn from its mean is ∑i=1n(yi−y¯)2=y⊤y−ny¯2=y⊤(In−1nJn)y, because ny¯2=(1⊤y)2/n=y⊤Jny/n. The centring matrix In−n−1Jn is symmetric and satisfies (In−n−1Jn)2=In−n−1Jn, because Jn2=nJn. Every sum of squares in an analysis of variance is a quadratic form whose matrix has these two properties (Section 1.9).

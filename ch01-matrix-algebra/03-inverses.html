1.3  Inverses and generalized inverses

1.3.1. The inverse of a square matrix

An n×n matrix A is nonsingular if there is a matrix A−1 with AA−1=A−1A=In. The inverse is unique: if B and C both qualify, then B=B(AC)=(BA)C=C.

Proposition 1.3.1

For an n×n matrix A the following are equivalent: (a) A is nonsingular; (b) rank(A)=n; (c) N(A)={0}; (d) BA=I for some B; (e) AC=I for some C. When they hold, B=C=A−1, and (A⊤)−1=(A−1)⊤,(AB)−1=B−1A−1 for nonsingular A,B of the same size. A nonsingular symmetric matrix has a symmetric inverse.

Proof. (b)⇔(c) is rank–nullity. (a)⇒(d) and (a)⇒(e) are trivial. (d)⇒(c): Ax=0 gives x=BAx=0. (e)⇒(b): I=AC has rank n, and rank(AC)≤rank(A). (b)⇒(a): since C(A)=Rn, we can solve Acj=ej for each j, giving AC=I. Then A(CA−I)=0, and because N(A)={0} each column of CA−I is zero. So CA=I as well. If BA=I then B=BAA−1=A−1, and similarly for C. The formulas are verified by multiplication: A⊤(A−1)⊤=(A−1A)⊤=I and (AB)(B−1A−1)=I. If A=A⊤, then A−1=(A⊤)−1=(A−1)⊤.

A rectangular matrix has no two-sided inverse. If X is n×p with full column rank, then X⊤X is nonsingular by Proposition 1.2.3(c), and (X⊤X)−1X⊤ is a left inverse: it multiplies X on the left to give Ip. This is the matrix that computes least squares coefficients.

1.3.2. Generalized inverses

When A is singular or rectangular we still want a matrix that inverts A on the vectors where inversion makes sense, namely those in C(A). The following weak requirement turns out to be exactly what solving equations needs.

Definition 1.3.1 (Generalized inverse)

A generalized inverse of an m×n matrix A is any n×m matrix A− with AA−A=A.

Read columnwise, the definition says that AA− acts as the identity on every column of A, hence on all of C(A): if u=Ab then A(A−u)=AA−Ab=u. So A−u is a solution of Ax=u whenever one exists. If A is nonsingular, multiplying the definition by A−1 on both sides gives A−=A−1. In general there are many generalized inverses. Chapter 6 uses them to write projections, and Chapter 8 uses them to describe estimable functions in models of less than full rank.

Theorem 1.3.2 (Existence)

Every matrix has a generalized inverse. Explicitly, if rank(A)=r≥1 and AIJ is the nonsingular r×r submatrix of Proposition 1.2.4, the n×m matrix G that holds AIJ−1 in the rows indexed by J and the columns indexed by I, and zeros elsewhere, is a generalized inverse of A.

Proof. For A=0 any matrix of the right size works. Otherwise let EI and EJ be the columns of Im and In indexed by I and J. Then G=EJAIJ−1EI⊤, AEJ=A⋅J and EI⊤A=AI⋅. So AGA=A⋅JAIJ−1AI⋅=A by Eq. 1.2.1.

Proposition 1.3.3 (Properties of generalized inverses)

Let A be m×n of rank r and let G be a generalized inverse of A.

  • (a) AG and GA are idempotent. Moreover C(AG)=C(A) and N(GA)=N(A), and rank(AG)=rank(GA)=r≤rank(G).

  • (b) G⊤ is a generalized inverse of A⊤.

  • (c) If G1,G2 are generalized inverses of A, so is G1AG2. In particular GAG is a generalized inverse which also satisfies (GAG)A(GAG)=GAG.

  • (d) If A is symmetric, then G⊤ and 12(G+G⊤) are generalized inverses of A, so a symmetric generalized inverse always exists.

  • (e) G+(I−GA)U+V(I−AG) is a generalized inverse of A for every U and V of size n×m.

Proof.

  • (a) (AG)2=(AGA)G=AG, and likewise for GA. Next, C(A)=C(AGA)⊆C(AG)⊆C(A), and N(A)⊆N(GA)⊆N(AGA)=N(A). The ranks follow from these and rank–nullity, and r=rank(AG)≤rank(G).
  • (b) Transpose AGA=A. (c) AG1AG2A=AG2A=A. The second statement is GAGAGAG=G(AGA)GAG=GAGAG=GAG.
  • (c) By (b), G⊤ is a generalized inverse of A⊤=A. The condition AGA=A is linear in G, so it is preserved by averaging.
  • (d) Multiply out, using A(I−GA)=0 and (I−AG)A=0.

A generalized inverse with the extra property in (c), GAG=G, is called reflexive. Exercise (B1) shows that the reflexive ones are exactly those of rank r. Part (e) in fact produces every generalized inverse from a single one as U and V vary. Idempotence in (a) means that AG and GA are projections, generally not orthogonal ones. Chapter 6 shows that the particular combination X(X⊤X)−X⊤ is symmetric, does not depend on the choice of generalized inverse, and is the orthogonal projection onto C(X).

1.3.3. The Moore–Penrose inverse

One generalized inverse is singled out by symmetry requirements.

Definition 1.3.2 (Moore–Penrose inverse)

The Moore–Penrose inverse of A is the matrix A+ satisfying AA+A=A,A+AA+=A+,(AA+)⊤=AA+,(A+A)⊤=A+A.

There is at most one such matrix. If G and H both satisfy the four conditions, then G=GAG=G(AG)⊤=GG⊤(AHA)⊤=G(AG)⊤(AH)⊤=GAGAH=GAH, where the steps use the second and third conditions for G, the first for H, and the third for both. The same argument with the fourth condition in place of the third gives H=(HA)⊤H=A⊤H⊤H=(AGA)⊤H⊤H=(GA)⊤(HA)⊤H=GAHAH=GAH, so G=H. Existence follows from the singular value decomposition, which gives A+ explicitly (Theorem 1.8.1).

1.3.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let A=(123246). (a) Check that rank(A)=1 and use the recipe of Theorem 1.3.2 with I={1} and J={1} to write down a generalized inverse G; verify AGA=A. (b) Show that AG is not symmetric, so G≠A+, although GAG=G. (c) Verify that A+=170(122436) satisfies the four conditions of Definition 1.3.2.

Solution.

  • (a) The second row is twice the first, so the rank is one, and the nonsingular 1×1 submatrix at I=J={1} is (1). The recipe puts 1 in the entry of the 3×2 matrix G indexed by row 1 and column 1, and zeros elsewhere. Then AG=(1020) and AGA=A.
  • (b) The matrix AG is visibly not symmetric. And G(AG)=G, because G has only its (1,1) entry nonzero, so G(AG) has the first row (1,0) of AG as its own first row and zeros elsewhere.
  • (c) Write A=uv⊤ with u=(1,2)⊤ and v=(1,2,3)⊤, so u⊤u=5, v⊤v=14 and A+=vu⊤/70. Then AA+=uu⊤/5 and A+A=vv⊤/14, both symmetric; AA+A=u(u⊤u)(v⊤v)v⊤/70=uv⊤=A; and A+AA+=v(v⊤v)(u⊤u)u⊤/702=A+.
Exercise (A2)

Let A=(Ir000) be m×n. Show that the generalized inverses of A are exactly the n×m matrices G=(IrUVW) with U,V,W arbitrary; that G is reflexive iff W=VU; and that A+=A⊤. How many generalized inverses does this A have when r<min(m,n), and how many when r=m=n?

Solution. Partition G conformably. Block multiplication gives AGA=(G11000), which equals A iff G11=Ir; the other blocks are unconstrained. With G11=Ir, GA=(Ir0V0) and GAG=(IrUVVU), so GAG=G iff W=VU. For the Moore–Penrose inverse, AG and GA are symmetric only if U=0 and V=0, and then reflexivity forces W=0. The remaining matrix is A⊤. When r<min(m,n) at least one of U,V,W is a nonempty block, so there are infinitely many generalized inverses; when r=m=n the three blocks are empty and G=In=A−1 is the only one, as it must be.

Exercise (A3)

Show that a symmetric idempotent matrix is its own Moore–Penrose inverse, and deduce (In−n−1Jn)+=In−n−1Jn for the centring matrix of Example (Sums of squares as quadratic forms). Show also that Jn+=n−2Jn.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let X be n×p with full column rank and write M=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤. Show that the left inverses of X, that is, the p×n matrices L with LX=Ip, are exactly L=(X⊤X)−1X⊤+K(In−M),K arbitrary, that every left inverse is a generalized inverse of X, and that (X⊤X)−1X⊤ is the only left inverse whose rows lie in C(X).

Solution. Since MX=X, every matrix of the stated form satisfies LX=Ip+K(X−X)=Ip. Conversely, if LX=Ip, then L=LM+L(I−M)=(LX)(X⊤X)−1X⊤+L(I−M), which is of the stated form with K=L. A left inverse satisfies XLX=X, so it is a generalized inverse. The rows of (X⊤X)−1X⊤ are the vectors X(X⊤X)−1ej, which lie in C(X); the rows of K(I−M) are of the form (I−M)c, and X⊤(I−M)=0 shows that such a vector lies in C(X) only if it is zero, since Xb=(I−M)c gives X⊤Xb=0 and hence Xb=0 by Exercise (A2).

Exercise (B2)

Let X be n×p of any rank and let G be any generalized inverse of X⊤X. Show that (a) XGX⊤X=X, so GX⊤ is a generalized inverse of X; (b) X⊤XGX⊤=X⊤; and (c) M=XGX⊤ is symmetric and idempotent and is the same matrix for every choice of G. Hints: for (a) use Exercise (A2); for (b) apply (a) to G⊤, which is also a generalized inverse of X⊤X by Proposition 1.3.3(b). The matrix M is the orthogonal projection onto C(X) of Chapter 6, and this exercise is what makes it well defined when X has less than full rank.

Solution.

  • (a) Because G is a generalized inverse of X⊤X, we have X⊤X(GX⊤X)=X⊤XIp. Cancelling one factor X⊤ on the left, which Exercise (A2) permits, gives XGX⊤X=X. Hence X(GX⊤)X=X.
  • (b) Since X⊤X is symmetric, G⊤ is a generalized inverse of it, so (a) gives XG⊤X⊤X=X; transposing yields X⊤XGX⊤=X⊤.
  • (c) Write M=XGX⊤, so M⊤=XG⊤X⊤. Using (b), M⊤M=XG⊤(X⊤XGX⊤)=XG⊤X⊤=M⊤. The left-hand side is symmetric, so M⊤ is symmetric, that is, M⊤=M; and then M2=M⊤M=M. If G1,G2 are two generalized inverses of X⊤X, then by (a) for G1 and (b) for G2, XG2X⊤=(XG1X⊤X)G2X⊤=XG1(X⊤XG2X⊤)=XG1X⊤.
Exercise (B3)

The text says that Proposition 1.3.3(e) produces every generalized inverse from a single one. Prove it: if G0 and G are generalized inverses of A, find U and V with G=G0+(I−G0A)U+V(I−AG0).

Solution. Put Z=G−G0, so that AZA=A−A=0. Take U=Z and V=G0AZ. Then (I−G0A)Z+G0AZ(I−AG0)=Z−G0AZ+G0AZ−G0(AZA)G0=Z, so G0+(I−G0A)U+V(I−AG0)=G0+Z=G.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Call G a least-squares generalized inverse of A if it satisfies the first and third conditions of Definition 1.3.2, namely AGA=A and (AG)⊤=AG. Show that (a) G0=(A⊤A)−A⊤ is one, for every choice of generalized inverse of A⊤A; (b) if G is a least-squares generalized inverse, then x=Gb minimizes ‖Ax−b‖, for every b; and (c) conversely, if Gb minimizes ‖Ax−b‖ for every b, then G is a least-squares generalized inverse. So these are exactly the matrices that solve least squares problems, whatever the rank of A.

Solution.

  • (a) is Exercise (B2) with X=A: part (a) there gives AG0A=A and part (c) gives the symmetry of AG0.

  • (b) First, A⊤(AG−I)=(AG)⊤A⊤−A⊤=(AGA)⊤−A⊤=0, using the symmetry of AG and then AGA=A. Hence for every x and b the cross term in ‖Ax−b‖2=‖A(x−Gb)‖2+2(x−Gb)⊤A⊤(AG−I)b+‖AGb−b‖2 vanishes, and ‖Ax−b‖2=‖A(x−Gb)‖2+‖AGb−b‖2≥‖AGb−b‖2, with equality at x=Gb.

  • (c) Let G0 be the matrix of (a). By (b) the minimum of ‖Ax−b‖ equals ‖AG0b−b‖, so the hypothesis says ‖AGb−b‖=‖AG0b−b‖ for every b. Applying the displayed decomposition with G0 in place of G and x=Gb, ‖AGb−b‖2=‖A(G−G0)b‖2+‖AG0b−b‖2, so A(G−G0)b=0 for every b, that is, AG=AG0. Then AGA=AG0A=A and (AG)⊤=(AG0)⊤=AG0=AG.

Exercise (C2)

Let A be m×k with full column rank and B be k×n with full row rank. Check the four conditions of Definition 1.3.2 to show that A+=(A⊤A)−1A⊤ and B+=B⊤(BB⊤)−1, and then that the reverse-order law (AB)+=B+A+ holds. (Without the rank conditions it can fail: Exercise (C1) asks for an example.)

Solution. Both A⊤A and BB⊤ are nonsingular by Proposition 1.2.3(c). Writing G=(A⊤A)−1A⊤, we have GA=Ik, so AGA=A and GAG=G, while AG=A(A⊤A)−1A⊤ is symmetric and GA=Ik is symmetric. Hence G=A+. Transposing the four conditions for A+ gives the four conditions for (A+)⊤ as a Moore–Penrose inverse of A⊤, so (A⊤)+=(A+)⊤; applying the first formula to B⊤, which has full column rank, gives B+=((B⊤)+)⊤=B⊤(BB⊤)−1.

Now put P=B+A+=B⊤(BB⊤)−1(A⊤A)−1A⊤. Then ABP=A(A⊤A)−1A⊤ and PAB=B⊤(BB⊤)−1B, both symmetric, because BB⊤(BB⊤)−1=I and (A⊤A)−1A⊤A=I collapse the middle factors. The same collapsing gives ABPAB=A(A⊤A)−1A⊤AB=AB and PABP=B⊤(BB⊤)−1BB⊤(BB⊤)−1(A⊤A)−1A⊤=P. All four conditions hold, so P=(AB)+.

1.5  Orthogonality and orthogonal matrices

1.5.1. Orthogonal vectors and complements

Vectors u,v are orthogonal, written u⊥v, if u⊤v=0. For orthogonal vectors the Pythagorean identity ‖u+v‖2=‖u‖2+‖v‖2 holds. A set of nonzero, mutually orthogonal vectors is independent: if ∑jcjvj=0, taking the inner product with vk leaves ck‖vk‖2=0. A set is orthonormal if, in addition, each vector has unit length. The columns of an n×r matrix Q are orthonormal iff Q⊤Q=Ir.

The orthogonal complement of a subspace S⊆Rn is S⊥={v:v⊤u=0 for all u∈S}. The next proposition is all that this chapter needs about complements. Chapter 6 builds the theory of orthogonal projections on it.

Proposition 1.5.1

Let A be m×n and let S⊆Rn be a subspace.

  • (a) N(A)=C(A⊤)⊥ and N(A⊤)=C(A)⊥.

  • (b) dim⁡S⊥=n−dim⁡S, and Rn=S⊕S⊥. In particular Rn=C(A⊤)⊕N(A) and Rm=C(A)⊕N(A⊤).

  • (c) (S⊥)⊥=S.

Proof.

  • (a) Av=0 says that v is orthogonal to every row of A, hence to their span. Apply this to A⊤ for the second identity.
  • (b) Put a basis of S (k vectors) into the columns of B. Then S⊥=N(B⊤), which has dimension n−k by rank–nullity. If u∈S∩S⊥ then u⊤u=0, so the sum is direct and has dimension n, so it is Rn.
  • (c) S⊆(S⊥)⊥, and both have dimension dim⁡S by (b).

1.5.2. Gram–Schmidt and the QR factorization

Theorem 1.5.2 (Gram–Schmidt; QR factorization)

Let X=[x1,…,xp] be n×p with full column rank. Define recursively wj=xj−∑k<j(uk⊤xj)uk,uj=wj/‖wj‖. Then u1,…,up are orthonormal and, for each j, span the same subspace as x1,…,xj. Equivalently, X=QR with Q=[u1,…,up] satisfying Q⊤Q=Ip and R upper triangular with positive diagonal entries.

Proof. By induction on j. Suppose u1,…,uj−1 are orthonormal and span span(x1,…,xj−1). Then wj≠0, since otherwise xj would lie in that span, contradicting independence. For i<j, ui⊤wj=ui⊤xj−ui⊤xj=0. So u1,…,uj are orthonormal, and they span span(x1,…,xj) because xj=‖wj‖uj+∑k<j(uk⊤xj)uk. That last identity, read column by column, is X=QR with rjj=‖wj‖>0 and rkj=uk⊤xj for k<j.

The factorization is unique (Exercise (B2)). Numerical software computes it by a more stable route, but the object is the same, and Chapter 6 and Chapter 10 rely on it. Two consequences are used repeatedly. First, any orthonormal set u1,…,ur in Rn extends to an orthonormal basis: apply Gram–Schmidt to u1,…,ur,e1,…,en, discarding each vector that depends on its predecessors. Second, every subspace has an orthonormal basis.

1.5.3. Orthogonal matrices

A square matrix Q is orthogonal if Q⊤Q=I. By Proposition 1.3.1, Q⊤ is then the inverse of Q, so also QQ⊤=I: the rows of an orthogonal matrix are orthonormal as well as its columns.

Proposition 1.5.3

Let Q and P be n×n orthogonal matrices.

  • (a) (Qx)⊤(Qv)=x⊤v and ‖Qx‖=‖x‖ for all x,v.

  • (b) Q⊤ and QP are orthogonal.

  • (c) Every entry satisfies |qij|≤1, and detQ=±1.

  • (d) If [Q1,Q2] is orthogonal with Q1 of size n×r, then Q1Q1⊤+Q2Q2⊤=In, and C(Q2)=C(Q1)⊥.

Proof.

  • (a) (Qx)⊤Qv=x⊤Q⊤Qv=x⊤v. (b) (QP)⊤QP=P⊤P=I.
  • (b) Each column has unit length. For the determinant, see Proposition 1.6.2: 1=det(Q⊤Q)=(detQ)2. (d) The first statement is QQ⊤=I written in blocks. For the second, Q1⊤Q2=0 gives C(Q2)⊆C(Q1)⊥, and both have dimension n−r.

Part (a) says an orthogonal matrix is a rigid motion that fixes the origin: a rotation, a reflection, or a composition of the two. Changing coordinates by an orthogonal matrix changes neither lengths nor angles. This is why the distribution theory of Chapter 4 can rotate a normal vector with independent components into a convenient coordinate system without changing its distribution.

Example (Helmert matrices)

The n×n Helmert matrix H has first row n−1/21⊤ and, for k=2,…,n, kth row hk⊤=1k(k−1)(1,…,1⏟k−1,−(k−1),0,…,0). Each row has unit length, since (k−1)+(k−1)2=k(k−1). Row k is orthogonal to row 1 because its entries sum to zero. For 2≤j<k, the nonzero entries of hj sit in positions 1,…,j, where hk is constant, so hj⊤hk is proportional to the sum of the entries of hj, which is zero. So H is orthogonal. For y∈Rn, (Hy)1=ny¯ and, by Proposition 1.5.3(a), ∑k=2n(hk⊤y)2=‖Hy‖2−ny¯2=∑i=1n(yi−y¯)2. The sum of squared deviations is a sum of n−1 squares of orthonormal contrasts. With independent normal observations this gives a short proof that the sample mean and variance are independent (Exercise (B1)).

Two other families appear later. Permutation matrices, the identity with its rows reordered, are orthogonal. For a unit vector u, the Householder reflection I−2uu⊤ is symmetric and orthogonal and reflects Rn in the hyperplane u⊥. Householder reflections are how QR factorizations are computed in practice (Exercise (B1)).

1.5.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that every 2×2 orthogonal matrix is either a rotation (cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ) (determinant 1) or a reflection (cos⁡θsin⁡θsin⁡θ−cos⁡θ) (determinant −1). Find the line fixed by the reflection.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let u be a unit vector and H=I−2uu⊤. Show that H is symmetric and orthogonal, that Hu=−u, and that Hv=v for v⊥u. Given x not a nonnegative multiple of e1, find u with Hx=‖x‖e1. Explain how repeating this column by column produces a QR factorization.

Solution. H⊤=H, and H⊤H=I−4uu⊤+4u(u⊤u)u⊤=I. Also Hu=u−2u=−u, and Hv=v when u⊤v=0. Let w=x−‖x‖e1≠0 and u=w/‖w‖. Then w⊤w=2‖x‖2−2‖x‖x1=2w⊤x, so Hx=x−2w(w⊤x)/(w⊤w)=x−w=‖x‖e1. Applying such a reflection H1 to the first column of X, then a reflection acting on coordinates 2,…,n to the second column of H1X, and so on, gives Hp⋯H1X=(R0) with R upper triangular. Hence X=QR, where Q holds the first p columns of the orthogonal matrix H1⋯Hp. In floating point one uses w=x+sgn(x1)‖x‖e1 instead, which avoids cancellation. It gives Hx=−sgn(x1)‖x‖e1, so diagonal entries of R may come out negative; changing the sign of such a row of R and of the matching column of Q at the end restores the positive diagonal of Theorem 1.5.2.

Exercise (B2)

Show that the QR factorization of a matrix with full column rank, with R having positive diagonal, is unique, and that R⊤ is the Cholesky factor of X⊤X.

Solution. If X=Q1R1=Q2R2, then X⊤X=R1⊤R1=R2⊤R2. Each Rk⊤ is lower triangular with positive diagonal, so both are the Cholesky factor of X⊤X, which is unique by Theorem 1.7.4(d). So R1=R2 and Q1=XR1−1=Q2.

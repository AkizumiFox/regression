1.6  Trace, determinants and partitioned matrices

1.6.1. Trace

The trace of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries, tr(A)=∑iaii. It is linear, tr(aA+bB)=atr(A)+btr(B), and tr(A⊤)=tr(A).

Theorem 1.6.1 (Cyclic property)

If A is m×n and B is n×m, then tr(AB)=tr(BA).

Proof. tr(AB)=∑i=1m∑k=1naikbki=∑k=1n∑i=1mbkiaik=tr(BA).

The two products need not have the same size. Applied repeatedly, the theorem allows cyclic permutations such as tr(ABC)=tr(CAB)=tr(BCA), but not arbitrary reorderings. Three consequences occur again and again:

tr(A⊤A)=∑i,jaij2=‖A‖F2,x⊤Ax=tr(Axx⊤),tr(P−1AP)=tr(A).
(1.6.1)

The middle one turns a scalar quadratic form into the trace of a matrix that is linear in xx⊤. Taking expectations then gives the mean of a random quadratic form (Chapter 2).

1.6.2. Determinants

The determinant of an n×n matrix is detA=∑πsgn(π)a1π(1)a2π(2)⋯anπ(n), the sum over all permutations π of {1,…,n}, where sgn(π)=±1 according to whether π is a product of an even or odd number of transpositions. We collect the standard properties. Parts (a)–(d) are proved in any linear algebra text (Harville 1997, chapter 13), and the others follow from them.

Proposition 1.6.2 (Determinants)

Let A,B be n×n.

  • (a) detA⊤=detA.

  • (b) detA is a linear function of each column separately. It changes sign when two columns are interchanged, and is unchanged when a multiple of one column is added to another. The same holds for rows.

  • (c) A triangular matrix has determinant equal to the product of its diagonal entries.

  • (d) det(AB)=detAdetB.

  • (e) A is nonsingular iff detA≠0, and then detA−1=1/detA.

  • (f) det(cA)=cndetA, and detQ=±1 for orthogonal Q.

Proof (Proof of (e) and (f)). If A is nonsingular, 1=detI=detAdetA−1 by (d). If A is singular, some column is a combination of the others. Subtracting that combination leaves a zero column without changing the determinant, by (b), and the determinant of a matrix with a zero column is zero, since every term of the sum contains a factor from that column. For (f), apply (b) to each of the n columns, and use (a) and (d) on Q⊤Q=I.

1.6.3. Schur complements and block determinants

Partitioned matrices arise whenever the regressors split into two groups, the model matrix X=[X1,X2] giving X⊤X=(X1⊤X1X1⊤X2X2⊤X1X2⊤X2), and whenever a random vector splits into two blocks (Chapter 3). In what follows

A=(A11A12A21A22),
(1.6.2)

with A11 of size k×k and A22 of size (n−k)×(n−k).

Definition 1.6.1 (Schur complement)

If A11 is nonsingular, the Schur complement of A11 in A is A22⋅1=A22−A21A11−1A12. If A22 is nonsingular, the Schur complement of A22 is A11⋅2=A11−A12A22−1A21.

Everything in this subsection comes from one identity, block Gaussian elimination. If A11 is nonsingular, multiplying out shows

A=(I0A21A11−1I)(A1100A22⋅1)(IA11−1A120I).
(1.6.3)

The outer factors are block triangular with identity blocks on the diagonal, and are nonsingular with inverses obtained by changing the sign of the off-diagonal block.

Theorem 1.6.3 (Block determinants)

Let A be partitioned as in Eq. 1.6.2.

  • (a) If A12=0 or A21=0, then detA=detA11detA22.

  • (b) If A11 is nonsingular, detA=detA11detA22⋅1. If A22 is nonsingular, detA=detA22detA11⋅2.

  • (c) For m×n matrices U and n×m matrices V, det(Im+UV)=det(In+VU). If B is m×m and nonsingular, det(B+UV)=detBdet(In+VB−1U).

Proof.

  • (a) Suppose A12=0 (otherwise transpose). In the permutation expansion, a term that is nonzero cannot use an entry of the zero block A12. So each of the first k rows takes its entry from the first k columns, and π maps {1,…,k} onto itself. Such a permutation splits into a permutation of the first k indices and one of the rest. Its sign is the product of their signs, so the sum factors as detA11detA22.
  • (b) Take determinants in Eq. 1.6.3, using (a) and Proposition 1.6.2(d). The outer factors have determinant 1. The second statement follows in the same way from the elimination that starts with A22.
  • (c) Apply (b) in both ways to (Im−UVIn), whose Schur complements are In+VU and Im+UV. For the second statement, write B+UV=B(Im+B−1UV) and apply the first.

Part (c) with n=1 is the matrix determinant lemma det(B+uv⊤)=detB(1+v⊤B−1u).

1.6.4. Inverses of partitioned matrices

Theorem 1.6.4 (Partitioned inverse)

Let A be partitioned as in Eq. 1.6.2 with A11 nonsingular. Then A is nonsingular iff A22⋅1 is, and in that case

A−1=(A11−1+A11−1A12A22⋅1−1A21A11−1−A11−1A12A22⋅1−1−A22⋅1−1A21A11−1A22⋅1−1).
(1.6.4)

If A22 is also nonsingular, the leading block of A−1 equals A11⋅2−1:

(A11−A12A22−1A21)−1=A11−1+A11−1A12A22⋅1−1A21A11−1.
(1.6.5)

Proof. The nonsingularity statement is Theorem 1.6.3(b). Inverting the three factors of Eq. 1.6.3 and multiplying them in reverse order gives A−1=(I−A11−1A120I)(A11−100A22⋅1−1)(I0−A21A11−1I), which multiplies out to Eq. 1.6.4. Eliminating with A22 first gives a second expression for A−1, whose leading block is A11⋅2−1. The inverse is unique, so the two leading blocks agree.

For a symmetric positive definite X⊤X (Section 1.7), the theorem says that the block of (X⊤X)−1 belonging to X1 is (X1⊤X1−X1⊤X2(X2⊤X2)−1X2⊤X1)−1. Chapter 6 derives the same formula geometrically, from the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem.

Identity Eq. 1.6.5 is a disguised form of a result that deserves its own name. Rename A11=B, A12=U, A21=V⊤ and A22=−C−1.

Theorem 1.6.5 (Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury)

Let B (n×n) and C (k×k) be nonsingular, and let U,V be n×k. If C−1+V⊤B−1U is nonsingular, then so is B+UCV⊤, and

(B+UCV⊤)−1=B−1−B−1U(C−1+V⊤B−1U)−1V⊤B−1.
(1.6.6)

In particular, for vectors u,v with 1+v⊤B−1u≠0,

(B+uv⊤)−1=B−1−B−1uv⊤B−11+v⊤B−1u.
(1.6.7)

Proof. Apply Theorem 1.6.4 to N=(BUV⊤−C−1). Both diagonal blocks are nonsingular. The Schur complement of B is −(C−1+V⊤B−1U), which is nonsingular by hypothesis, so N is nonsingular. Then the Schur complement of the lower block, B+UCV⊤, is nonsingular by the same theorem with the roles of the blocks exchanged, and Eq. 1.6.5 becomes Eq. 1.6.6. For Eq. 1.6.7 take k=1 and C=1.

Idea. Every result in this section comes from one block elimination, Eq. 1.6.3: a partitioned matrix is a unit triangular matrix times diag(A11,A22⋅1) times another. Taking determinants gives Theorem 1.6.3, inverting the factors gives Theorem 1.6.4, and eliminating in the other order and comparing gives the Woodbury identity. When a formula about a block is forgotten, redo the elimination.

The identity replaces the inversion of an n×n matrix by that of a k×k matrix. That matters when B−1 is already known or cheap, as in the following two examples.

Example (Deleting an observation)

Removing the ith row xi⊤ from a model matrix changes X⊤X into X⊤X−xixi⊤. With u=−xi, v=xi and hi=xi⊤(X⊤X)−1xi, Eq. 1.6.7 gives (X⊤X−xixi⊤)−1=(X⊤X)−1+(X⊤X)−1xixi⊤(X⊤X)−11−hi, valid whenever hi<1, and the determinant lemma gives det(X⊤X−xixi⊤)=(1−hi)det(X⊤X). The quantity hi is the leverage of observation i (Chapter 6), and these formulas are what make the deletion diagnostics of Chapter 20 cheap. For Brownlee’s stack-loss data (n=21, p=4), the listing updates the inverse for each of the 21 deletions. The largest relative discrepancy from direct inversion is 1×10−13. Observation 17 has the largest leverage, 0.412, and removing it multiplies det(X⊤X) by 0.588.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.stackloss.load_pandas().data
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(data)), data[["AIRFLOW", "WATERTEMP", "ACIDCONC"]]])
n, p = X.shape

A_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
h = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", X, A_inv, X)             # leverages x_i' (X'X)^{-1} x_i

worst = 0.0
for i in range(n):
    x = X[i]
    # Sherman-Morrison with u = -x, v = x:  (X'X - x x')^{-1}
    B_update = A_inv + np.outer(A_inv @ x, x @ A_inv) / (1 - h[i])
    X_del = np.delete(X, i, axis=0)
    B_direct = np.linalg.inv(X_del.T @ X_del)
    worst = max(worst, np.abs(B_update - B_direct).max() / np.abs(B_direct).max())
print(f"largest relative discrepancy over all {n} deletions: {worst:.1e}")
Example (Diagonal plus low rank)

In factor models and in the linear mixed models of Chapter 32, covariance matrices have the form Σ=D+LL⊤ with D diagonal and positive and L of size N×k, k≪N. By Eq. 1.6.6 and Theorem 1.6.3(c), Σ−1=D−1−D−1L(Ik+L⊤D−1L)−1L⊤D−1,detΣ=detDdet(Ik+L⊤D−1L). Each needs only a k×k factorization. The script checks this with N=1500 and k=4. The solution of Σw=v agrees with a direct solve to a relative error of 8×10−14.

Example (Equicorrelation)

The matrix E=(1−ρ)In+ρJn has unit diagonal and every off-diagonal entry equal to ρ. It is the correlation matrix of exchangeable observations. Taking B=(1−ρ)I and u=ρ1, v=1, for ρ≠1 and 1+(n−1)ρ≠0, E−1=11−ρ(In−ρ1+(n−1)ρJn),detE=(1−ρ)n−1(1+(n−1)ρ).

1.6.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that tr(ABC)=tr(ACB) when A,B,C are symmetric, and find 2×2 matrices for which the two traces differ.

Solution. For symmetric matrices, tr(ABC)=tr((ABC)⊤)=tr(CBA)=tr(ACB), using transposition and then the cyclic property. For a counterexample take A=(0100), B=(0010) and C=(1000). Then ABC=C has trace 1, while AC=0 gives tr(ACB)=0.

Exercise (A2)

Let A be nonsingular, a a vector and c a scalar with s=c−a⊤A−1a≠0. Write down the inverse and the determinant of (Aaa⊤c). Use it to update (X⊤X)−1 when a column is added to X.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y, ei=yi−xi⊤β^ and hi=xi⊤(X⊤X)−1xi<1. Show that the least squares coefficients computed without observation i are β^(i)=β^−(X⊤X)−1xiei1−hi.

Solution. Write K=(X⊤X)−1 and x=xi. Deleting row i turns X⊤y into X⊤y−xyi, so by Example (Deleting an observation) β^(i)=(K+Kxx⊤K1−hi)(X⊤y−xyi)=β^−Kxyi+Kx(x⊤β^−hiyi)1−hi=β^−Kxyi−x⊤β^1−hi, since (1−hi)yi−x⊤β^+hiyi=yi−x⊤β^.

Exercise (B2)

For an n×p matrix X and λ>0, show that (X⊤X+λIp)−1X⊤=X⊤(XX⊤+λIn)−1. Explain why the right-hand side is preferable when p is much larger than n, and derive the identity a second way from Theorem 1.6.5.

Solution. Both X⊤X+λI and XX⊤+λI are positive definite. The identity (X⊤X+λIp)X⊤=X⊤XX⊤+λX⊤=X⊤(XX⊤+λIn) gives the result after multiplying by the two inverses. When p≫n, the right-hand side inverts an n×n matrix instead of a p×p one. For the second derivation, apply Theorem 1.6.5 with B=λIp, U=X⊤, C=In and V=X⊤: (λIp+X⊤X)−1=λ−1Ip−λ−2X⊤W−1X with W=In+XX⊤/λ. Multiplying on the right by X⊤ gives λ−1X⊤[In−W−1(XX⊤/λ)]=λ−1X⊤W−1[W−XX⊤/λ]=λ−1X⊤W−1=X⊤(XX⊤+λIn)−1.

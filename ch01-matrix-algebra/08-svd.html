1.8  The singular value decomposition and conditioning

The spectral theorem needs a symmetric matrix. A model matrix is rectangular, and the decomposition that fits it is the singular value decomposition.

Theorem 1.8.1 (Singular value decomposition)

Let A be m×n of rank r≥1. There are an m×r matrix U and an n×r matrix V, both with orthonormal columns, and numbers σ1≥⋯≥σr>0, such that

A=UDV⊤=∑i=1rσiuivi⊤,D=diag(σ1,…,σr).
(1.8.1)

The singular values σi are the positive square roots of the nonzero eigenvalues of A⊤A (equivalently of AA⊤). The columns of V and U are eigenvectors of A⊤A and AA⊤, with C(V)=C(A⊤) and C(U)=C(A). Moreover A+=VD−1U⊤ is the Moore–Penrose inverse of A.

Proof. A⊤A is nonnegative definite of rank r (Proposition 1.2.3(c)). By the spectral theorem its eigenvalues are σ12≥⋯≥σr2>0 and n−r zeros, with orthonormal eigenvectors V=[v1,…,vr] for the nonzero ones and V0 for the zeros. Define U=AVD−1. Then U⊤U=D−1V⊤A⊤AVD−1=D−1D2D−1=Ir. For each column v of V0, ‖Av‖2=v⊤A⊤Av=0, so AV0=0. Since VV⊤+V0V0⊤=I (Proposition 1.5.3(d)), A=AVV⊤+AV0V0⊤=UDV⊤. Then AA⊤U=UDV⊤VDU⊤U=UD2, so the columns of U are eigenvectors of AA⊤. C(U)⊆C(A) by definition of U, and both have dimension r. The statement for V is the same argument applied to A⊤=VDU⊤. The four Penrose conditions for VD−1U⊤ follow from U⊤U=V⊤V=Ir. For instance, AA+=UU⊤ and A+A=VV⊤ are symmetric.

Completing U and V to orthogonal matrices gives the full SVD A=U~D~V~⊤, with D~ of size m×n carrying σ1,…,σr on its diagonal and zeros elsewhere. By convention, σr+1=⋯=σmin(m,n)=0. Geometrically, A maps the unit ball of Rn onto a solid ellipsoid in C(A) with semi-axes σiui. The input direction vi is stretched by the factor σi and turned into ui. The Moore–Penrose inverse undoes the stretching on C(A) and sends C(A)⊥ to zero. The matrix UU⊤ is the orthogonal projection onto C(A), whose theory is in Chapter 6.

Proposition 1.8.2 (Norms and extremal properties)

Let A be m×n with singular values σ1≥σ2≥⋯, and let ‖A‖2=maxx≠0‖Ax‖/‖x‖ be the spectral norm.

  • (a) ‖A‖2=σ1 and ‖A‖F2=∑iσi2.

  • (b) If A has full column rank n, then minx≠0‖Ax‖/‖x‖=σn, attained at vn.

  • (c) (Eckart–Young.) For k<r, let Ak=∑i≤kσiuivi⊤. Then ‖A−B‖2≥σk+1=‖A−Ak‖2 for every B with rank(B)≤k.

Proof.

  • (a) and (b): ‖Ax‖2/‖x‖2=x⊤A⊤Ax/x⊤x, and the eigenvalues of A⊤A are the σi2 (padded with zeros), so Theorem 1.7.7 applies. For the Frobenius norm, ‖A‖F2=tr(A⊤A)=∑σi2 by Proposition 1.7.1(a).
  • (b) A−Ak=∑i>kσiuivi⊤ has largest singular value σk+1. If rank(B)≤k, then dim⁡N(B)≥n−k, and since (n−k)+(k+1)>n, a basis of N(B) together with v1,…,vk+1 is dependent. So the two subspaces share a unit vector x=∑i≤k+1civi. Then ‖(A−B)x‖2=‖Ax‖2=∑i≤k+1σi2ci2≥σk+12.

The Frobenius version of (c), ‖A−B‖F2≥∑i>kσi2, also holds (Eckart and Young 1936). It is the basis of principal components.

1.8.1. The condition number

Definition 1.8.1 (Condition number)

The condition number of an m×n matrix A of full column rank is κ(A)=σmax(A)σmin(A)=σ1σn. For a nonsingular square matrix this equals ‖A‖2‖A−1‖2. A matrix without full column rank has κ(A)=∞ by convention.

By Proposition 1.8.2, κ(A) is the ratio of the largest to the smallest stretching that A applies to a unit vector. It is at least 1, is unchanged by scaling A or multiplying it on the left by a matrix with orthonormal columns, and equals 1 iff A⊤A=cI, that is, iff the columns are orthogonal with equal lengths. Two further facts explain its role in regression. First, the singular values of A⊤A are the σi2, so

κ(A⊤A)=κ(A)2.
(1.8.2)

Second, κ bounds how much relative errors in the data can grow when a linear system is solved. If A is square and nonsingular, Ax=b and A(x+δx)=b+δb, then δx=A−1δb, and ‖b‖≤σ1‖x‖ gives

‖δx‖‖x‖≤‖A−1‖2‖δb‖‖b‖/σ1=κ(A)‖δb‖‖b‖.
(1.8.3)

In floating point, relative errors of about 10−16 in the data can therefore become errors of about κ×10−16 in the solution. Solving the normal equations works with X⊤X and so, by Eq. 1.8.2, squares the damage. Chapter 6 and Chapter 10 take this up.

A small σmin has a direct statistical reading. By Proposition 1.8.2(b), ‖Xvn‖=σn with ‖vn‖=1. The coefficients vn combine the columns of X into a vector that is nearly zero, which is a near-dependence among the regressors. This is the basis of the collinearity diagnostics of Chapter 26. The size of κ depends on the units of the columns, so columns are usually rescaled before it is interpreted.

Example (A macroeconomic design)

Take the 203 quarterly US observations of real GDP, real investment, real government spending, real disposable income, population and unemployment, with an intercept, as a model matrix with p=7 columns. As recorded, κ(X)=5.10×105. Most of that is units: GDP is measured in billions and unemployment in percent. Rescaling each column to unit length reduces it to 383, and centring the regressors before rescaling reduces it further to 124. By Eq. 1.8.2 the corresponding cross-product matrix has condition number 15386.

What remains is not an artefact of units. Figure 1.8.1(b) shows the right singular vector for the smallest singular value, σmin=0.0175, of the centred and scaled design. Its weight is concentrated on GDP (0.78) and disposable income (−0.61). The combination of the scaled columns with these coefficients is a vector of length 0.0175, although every column has length one. The two series move almost in lockstep: their correlation is 0.9990. No rescaling can separate their coefficients well. Centring is not a neutral choice, however: it removes near-dependences that involve the intercept, and Belsley et al. (1980) recommend scaling without centring for that reason, so their diagnostics would use the unit-length figure 383. Chapter 26 returns to the choice. The listing computes the singular values, and the script also checks Eq. 1.8.2, the Penrose conditions for VD−1U⊤, and the Eckart–Young bound.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.macrodata.load_pandas().data
names = ["realgdp", "realinv", "realgovt", "realdpi", "pop", "unemp"]
labels = ["GDP", "investment", "government", "disp. income", "population", "unemployment"]
Z = data[names].to_numpy()
n = len(Z)

X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), Z])                          # as recorded
X_unit = X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=0)                        # unit-length columns
Xc = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), Z - Z.mean(axis=0)])      # regressors centred
X_std = Xc / np.linalg.norm(Xc, axis=0)                       # ... then length 1

for name, M in [("raw", X), ("unit length", X_unit), ("centred", X_std)]:
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(M, full_matrices=False)          # M = U diag(s) V'
    print(f"{name:12s} kappa = {s[0] / s[-1]:10.1f}   singular values / s_1:",
          np.array2string(s / s[0], precision=4))

1.8.2. Exercises

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let X=[x1,x2] with ‖x1‖=‖x2‖=1 and x1⊤x2=r, |r|<1. Show that κ(X)=(1+|r|)/(1−|r|). How large must |r| be for κ(X)>30?

Solution. X⊤X=(1rr1) has eigenvalues 1±r, with eigenvectors (1,±1)⊤. The singular values of X are their square roots, so κ(X)2=(1+|r|)/(1−|r|). For κ>30 we need (1+|r|)/(1−|r|)>900, that is, |r|>899/901≈0.998. For two columns, even κ(X)=100 requires |r|>9999/10001≈0.9998.

Exercise (B2)

Show that κ(X)≥maxj‖xj‖/mink‖xk‖, so that columns on very different scales force a large condition number. Show by example that rescaling the columns can also increase κ.

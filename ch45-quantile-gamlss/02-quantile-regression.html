45.2  Quantile regression

Least squares answers a question about a loss function: which constant minimizes expected squared error? Change the loss and the answer changes. The median minimizes expected absolute error, and one modification of absolute error — weighting the two sides differently — picks out any quantile at all. Koenker and Bassett (1978) built a regression method on that observation.

45.2.1. The check loss

Definition 45.2.1 (Check loss and quantile regression)

For τ∈(0,1) the check function is

ρτ(u)=u{τ−1{u<0}}={τu,u≥0,(τ−1)u,u<0,
(45.2.1)

a convex, piecewise linear, nonnegative function with a kink at the origin, equal to |u|/2 when τ=1/2. The linear quantile regression model at level τ asserts 𝛃QY(τ∣x)=x⊤βτ, and given data (yi,x(i)), i=1,…,n, a τ-regression quantile is any minimizer

𝛃β^τ∈argminb∈RpRτ(b)=∑i=1nρτ(yi−x(i)⊤b).
(45.2.2)

The subscript τ is there because the coefficients may differ from level to level: the model is a separate statement for each τ, not one model with a nuisance parameter. Nothing is assumed about the shape of the conditional distribution, not even that it has a density.

45.2.2. What the check loss estimates

Theorem 45.2.1 (The check loss elicits the quantile)

Let Y be a random variable with distribution function F and E|Y|<∞, and let G(q)=Eρτ(Y−q).

  • (a) Population characterization. G is finite and convex, and for q<q′

    G(q′)−G(q)=∫qq′{F(s)−τ}ds.
    (45.2.3)

    Consequently the set of minimizers of G is exactly the set of τ-quantiles of F, that is {q:F(q−)≤τ≤F(q)}.

  • (b) Linear program. Writing ui=(yi−x(i)⊤b)+ and vi=(x(i)⊤b−yi)+, problem Eq. 45.2.2 is equivalent to

    minb,u,v{τ1⊤u+(1−τ)1⊤v}subject toXb+u−v=y,  u≥0, v≥0,
    (45.2.4)

    a linear program in 2n+2p nonnegative variables with n equality constraints.

  • (c) Equivariance of the estimate. Let 𝛃β^τ(y,X) denote any solution of Eq. 45.2.2. For a>0, 𝛄γ∈Rp and nonsingular A, 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛄𝛃𝛄𝛃𝛃β^τ(ay,X)=aβ^τ(y,X),β^τ(−y,X)=−β^1−τ(y,X),β^τ(y+Xγ,X)=β^τ(y,X)+γ,β^τ(y,XA)=A−1β^τ(y,X).

  • (d) Equivariance under monotone maps. For strictly increasing left-continuous h, Qh(Y)(τ∣x)=h{QY(τ∣x)}.

Proof.

  • (a) Split the loss at the kink: ρτ(u)=τu++(1−τ)u−, where u−=(−u)+, so G(q)=τE(Y−q)++(1−τ)E(q−Y)+, finite because both terms are bounded by E|Y|+|q|. By Fubini’s theorem, E(Y−q)+=∫q∞{1−F(s)}ds,E(q−Y)+=∫−∞qF(s)ds. Subtracting the two expressions for q′ and q gives G(q′)−G(q)=−τ∫qq′{1−F(s)}ds+(1−τ)∫qq′F(s)ds=∫qq′{F(s)−τ}ds, which is Eq. 45.2.3; convexity follows because the integrand is nondecreasing. Let qτ=inf{q:F(q)≥τ}. If q<qτ then F(s)<τ for every s<qτ, so G(q)−G(qτ)=∫qqτ{τ−F(s)}ds>0 and q is not a minimizer. If q>qτ then F(s)≥F(qτ)≥τ for s≥qτ by right continuity, so G(q)≥G(qτ), with equality if and only if F≡τ almost everywhere on (qτ,q), that is F(q−)=τ. Hence qτ is a minimizer and the minimizers are exactly the points with F(q−)≤τ≤F(q).

  • (b) For any b the choices ui=(yi−x(i)⊤b)+ and vi=(x(i)⊤b−yi)+ are feasible with objective value Rτ(b), so the linear program’s optimum is at most minbRτ(b). Conversely, if (b,u,v) is feasible then ui−vi=yi−x(i)⊤b, and for a fixed difference the sum τui+(1−τ)vi is minimized by min(ui,vi)=0, which gives exactly ρτ(yi−x(i)⊤b). The two problems therefore have the same value and the same optimal b.

  • (c) For a>0, ρτ(au)=aρτ(u), so Rτ(b;ay)=aRτ(b/a;y) and the minimizers correspond under b↦ab. A direct check on Eq. 45.2.1 gives ρτ(−u)=ρ1−τ(u), whence Rτ(b;−y)=R1−τ(−b;y) and the second identity. For the shift, 𝛄𝛄yi+x(i)⊤γ−x(i)⊤b=yi−x(i)⊤(b−γ), so the objective is the old one at 𝛄b−γ. For the reparameterization, the residuals of XA at b are those of X at Ab.

  • (d) This is Lemma 45.1.1 applied conditionally on x.

Part (a) is the whole justification of the method, and it needs remarkably little: no density, no moments beyond the first, no continuity. Part (d) is a property least squares does not have: the median of log⁡Y is the logarithm of the median of Y, while the mean of log⁡Y is not the logarithm of the mean, the retransformation problem of Section 22.5. A quantile regression fitted on the log scale reads back without a smearing correction.

Remark (The influence of one observation). Away from the kink ρτ′(u)=τ−1{u<0} takes only the values τ and τ−1, so an observation enters the estimating equation only through the sign of its residual: moving one response from +10 to +106 changes nothing. This is the bounded-influence property that made the median robust in Section 20.6. It buys nothing against a bad design point, whose x(i) enters unbounded.

Remark (Without moments). Minimizing G0(q)=E{ρτ(Y−q)−ρτ(Y)} instead removes the moment condition: since |ρτ(u−v)−ρτ(u)|≤max(τ,1−τ)|v|, this is finite whatever the tails of Y, and differs from G by a constant whenever G is finite, so (a) holds verbatim.

45.2.3. Solving the problem

Proposition 45.2.2 (Interpolation and counting)

Let rank(X)=p and let 𝛃β^τ minimize Eq. 45.2.2.

  • (a) There is a minimizer whose residual vector vanishes at p observations whose rows of X are linearly independent: the fit passes exactly through p of the data points.

  • (b) If 1∈C(X), then for any minimizer, with n−,n0,n+ the numbers of negative, zero and positive residuals,

    n−≤τn≤n−+n0.
    (45.2.5)

Proof.

  • (a) Let b be any minimizer, H={i:yi=x(i)⊤b} the interpolated set, and suppose {x(i):i∈H} spans a space of dimension less than p. Choose d≠0 orthogonal to all of them. For small t the residuals in H stay zero and no other residual changes sign, so Rτ(b+td)=∑i∉H(ri−tx(i)⊤d){τ−1{ri<0}},ri=yi−x(i)⊤b, which is affine in t. An affine function minimized at t=0 is constant, so Rτ stays minimal as t grows, until some residual outside H reaches zero. Because rank(X)=p and d≠0, some x(i)⊤d≠0, so such a t exists and is finite; there the interpolated set is strictly larger and the objective still minimal. Repeating raises the rank of {x(i):i∈H} to p.

  • (b) Let 1=Xc and consider 𝛃ϕ(t)=Rτ(β^τ+tc), which shifts every fitted value by t and so every residual by −t. For t>0 small, an observation with ri>0 contributes τ(ri−t), one with ri≤0 contributes (τ−1)(ri−t); hence ϕ′(0+)=−τn++(1−τ)(n−+n0). Similarly ϕ′(0−)=−τ(n++n0)+(1−τ)n−. Minimality forces ϕ′(0+)≥0 and ϕ′(0−)≤0. The first gives τn+≤(1−τ)(n−+n0); adding τ(n−+n0) to both sides and using n++n−+n0=n yields τn≤n−+n0. The second gives τ(n++n0)≥(1−τ)n−, and adding τn− to both sides yields τn≥n−.

Part (b) says the fitted surface has about τn observations below it, which is what the name promises; part (a) says it is pinned to p of the data points, as the median of an odd number of points is one of the points. Together they make the fit a combinatorial object: finitely many candidate hyperplanes, one per choice of p rows, and the optimum is one of them. Hence the simplex method of Koenker and d’Orey (1987) rather than differentiation, or the interior-point route of Portnoy and Koenker (1997), which makes quantile regression competitive in cost with least squares for large n.

Example (Seven quantile lines for Engel’s budgets)

Fitting Eq. 45.2.4 at seven levels gives slopes rising steadily with τ:

τ 0.05 0.10 0.25 0.50 0.75 0.90 0.95
slope 0.3434 0.4018 0.4741 0.5602 0.6440 0.6863 0.7091
intercept 124.9 110.1 95.5 81.5 62.4 67.4 64.1

The least squares slope, 0.4852, sits between the quartiles. A household at the tenth percentile of its income group spends about 0.4018 of each extra franc on food, one at the ninetieth 0.6863; at an income of 1000 francs the fitted median budget share is 0.642 and the tenth-percentile share 0.512. Every fit interpolates 2 observations and satisfies Eq. 45.2.5, as the listings verify.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy.optimize import linprog
data = sm.datasets.engel.load_pandas().data
TAUS = (0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 0.90, 0.95)

income = data["income"].to_numpy()
food = data["foodexp"].to_numpy()
n = len(food)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), income])

def check_loss(u, tau):
    """The check function rho_tau applied elementwise."""
    return u * (tau - (u < 0))

def qreg(X, y, tau):
    """Quantile regression by linear programming.

    Variables are (b+, b-, u, v), all nonnegative, with beta = b+ - b- and
    u - v = y - X beta the positive and negative parts of the residual.
    """
    n, p = X.shape
    cost = np.concatenate([np.zeros(2 * p), tau * np.ones(n), (1 - tau) * np.ones(n)])
    A = np.hstack([X, -X, np.eye(n), -np.eye(n)])
    out = linprog(cost, A_eq=A, b_eq=y, bounds=(0, None), method="highs")
    return out.x[:p] - out.x[p:2 * p]

beta = {tau: qreg(X, food, tau) for tau in TAUS}
for tau in (0.10, 0.50, 0.90):
    print(f"tau = {tau:.2f}:  intercept {beta[tau][0]:8.2f}   slope {beta[tau][1]:.4f}")
for tau in TAUS:
    r = food - X @ beta[tau]
    n_zero = np.sum(np.abs(r) < 1e-8)
    n_neg = np.sum(r < -1e-8)
    assert n_zero == X.shape[1]                      # p residuals vanish exactly
    assert n_neg <= tau * n <= n_neg + n_zero        # the counting identity
print("at every tau: 2 residuals are zero and #negative <= tau*n <= #negative + 2")

Koenker and Machado (1999) proposed the analogue of R2: with V^(τ) the minimized check loss and V~(τ) its value in the intercept-only model, R1(τ)=1−V^(τ)/V~(τ). For Engel’s data it rises from 0.474 at τ=0.05 to 0.805 at τ=0.95: income explains far more about the upper tail than about the lower. Like R2 it says nothing about whether the linear specification is right.

45.2.4. Reading a coefficient

The coefficient βτj is the derivative of the τ-quantile of the conditional distribution with respect to xj. It is not the effect of xj on the household that happens to sit at rank τ.

Remark (Quantiles of distributions, not of people). Suppose incomes rise by one franc for every household. The 0.9-quantile of the new conditional distribution exceeds the old by β0.9; but the household at rank 0.9 before need not be there after, and nothing in the data says what happened to it. Reading βτ as an individual effect assumes the change preserves ranks — the kind of untestable structure Chapter 25 insists on making explicit (Definition 25.2.1). Without it, quantile regression describes how a distribution moves, which is usually the question anyway.

Two cautions carry over: extrapolation is as unsafe as in Section 12.3, and Section 45.3 shows it is the first place quantile curves misbehave; and linearity of QY(τ∣x) is an assumption, testable in the spirit of Theorem 14.6.2.

45.2.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

A quantile regression with an intercept and one regressor is fitted at τ=0.25 to n=40 observations. How many residuals can be negative, and how many are exactly zero? Use Proposition 45.2.2.

Solution. Generic data give n0=p=2, and Eq. 45.2.5 reads n−≤10≤n−+2, so n−∈{8,9,10}.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Take X=1, so that the problem is to fit one number. Show that the minimizers of ∑iρτ(yi−b) are exactly the τ-quantiles of the empirical distribution of y1,…,yn, and that for τn not an integer the minimizer is the unique order statistic y(⌈τn⌉).

Solution. Apply Theorem 45.2.1(a) to the empirical distribution Fn, which is legitimate because Fn is a distribution function with finite mean. The minimizers are {b:Fn(b−)≤τ≤Fn(b)}. If τn∉Z, no order statistic has Fn equal to τ, and the condition picks out the single point y(⌈τn⌉); if τn is an integer, the whole interval [y(τn),y(τn+1)] minimizes.

Exercise (B2)

Write down the dual of the linear program Eq. 45.2.4 and show that it is maxdy⊤dsubject toX⊤d=0,  d∈[τ−1,τ]n, or, after the shift a=d+(1−τ)1, max{y⊤a:X⊤a=(1−τ)X⊤1, a∈[0,1]n}. Show that at an optimum ai=1 for a positive residual and ai=0 for a negative one, so that a records the “signs” of the residuals, and explain why these are the raw material of the rank tests of Section 45.3.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that the set of minimizers of Rτ is a convex polyhedron, and that if it contains two distinct points then Rτ is constant on the segment joining them. Deduce that a unique solution is the generic case: non-uniqueness requires a coincidence in the data, such as p+1 observations lying on one hyperplane.

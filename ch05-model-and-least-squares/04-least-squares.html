5.4  Least squares and the normal equations

The straight-line calculation of Section 5.2 extends to any model matrix. The algebra is shorter in matrix form than it was for two coefficients, because the derivatives of linear and quadratic forms were worked out once and for all in Chapter 1. In this section y is a fixed data vector. Nothing here uses probability. The statistical properties come in Section 5.5.

5.4.1. The least squares estimate

Definition 5.4.1 (Least squares estimate)

For an n×p matrix X and y∈Rn, the least squares criterion is S(b)=‖y−Xb‖2=∑i=1n(yi−x(i)⊤b)2,b∈Rp. A least squares estimate of 𝛃β is any 𝛃β^ that minimizes S. The corresponding fitted values are 𝛃Y^=Xβ^, the residuals are 𝛆𝛃ε^=y−Xβ^, and the residual sum of squares is 𝛃𝛆SSE=S(β^)=‖ε^‖2.

Following the book’s notation, we write Y^ and 𝛆ε^ in bold for the vectors, and y^i and ε^i for their entries, whether the data are regarded as fixed numbers or as random.

Theorem 5.4.1 (Least squares for a full-rank model)

Let X be an n×p matrix of rank p. Then:

  • (a) X⊤X is positive definite, and in particular nonsingular;

  • (b) the normal equations

    X⊤Xb=X⊤y
    (5.4.1)

    have exactly one solution,

    𝛃β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y;
    (5.4.2)

  • (c) for every b∈Rp,

    𝛃𝛃𝛃‖y−Xb‖2=‖y−Xβ^‖2+(b−β^)⊤X⊤X(b−β^),
    (5.4.3)

    so 𝛃β^ is the unique least squares estimate.

If instead rank(X)<p, least squares estimates exist but are never unique.

Proof. (a) X⊤X is symmetric, and b⊤X⊤Xb=‖Xb‖2≥0, with equality iff Xb=0. Since X has rank p, rank–nullity (Theorem 1.2.2) gives N(X)={0}, so the quadratic form is positive for b≠0. A positive definite matrix is nonsingular (Proposition 1.7.5(e)).

(b) This is the computation of Example (The least squares criterion): by Theorem 1.11.1 the gradient of S is −2X⊤(y−Xb) and its Hessian is 2X⊤X. Every minimizer of the differentiable function S is a stationary point, and the stationary points are the solutions of Eq. 5.4.1. By (a) there is exactly one, Eq. 5.4.2.

(c) Put 𝛃d=b−β^. Then 𝛆y−Xb=ε^−Xd, and 𝛆𝛆‖y−Xb‖2=‖ε^‖2−2d⊤X⊤ε^+d⊤X⊤Xd. The middle term vanishes because 𝛆𝛃X⊤ε^=X⊤y−X⊤Xβ^=0 by the normal equations. The last term is positive for d≠0 by (a). So 𝛃S(b)>S(β^) whenever 𝛃b≠β^.

For the last statement, suppose rank(X)<p. The vector X⊤y lies in C(X⊤)=C(X⊤X) (Proposition 1.2.3(c)), so by Proposition 1.11.2, applied with A=X⊤X and S(b)=y⊤y+(b⊤X⊤Xb−2b⊤X⊤y), the minimizers of S are exactly the solutions of the normal equations, and they exist. But there is a v≠0 with Xv=0, and then S(b+tv)=S(b) for every t, so no minimizer is unique.

The calculus proof finds the stationary point and then needs a separate argument, Eq. 5.4.3, to show it is the global minimum. The Hessian 2X⊤X tells the same story: it is positive definite, so S is a strictly convex quadratic bowl with a single lowest point. The name “normal equations” has nothing to do with the normal distribution. It refers to the orthogonality they express: 𝛃X⊤(y−Xβ^)=0 says the residual vector is normal, that is, perpendicular, to every column of X. Chapter 6 makes that geometry the starting point, derives the normal equations again without calculus (Theorem 6.4.2), and treats the rank-deficient case in full.

Remark (The rank-deficient case). When rank(X)<p, the proof shows that the least squares estimates form an affine set 𝛃β^+N(X) of dimension p−rank(X). Different programs report different members of this set, according to which columns they drop or which constraints they impose. The fitted values 𝛃Xβ^ are the same for all of them, since 𝛃𝛃X(β^+v)=Xβ^ for v∈N(X). Which linear combinations of the coefficients share this invariance is the question of estimability, answered in Chapter 8 (Definition 8.2.1 and Theorem 8.3.1).

Least squares under known linear restrictions Hb=h, such as shares that add to one, has a closed form found by Lagrange multipliers in Exercise (B1); the increase in the residual sum of squares it computes drives the test of the restrictions in Chapter 11 (Theorem 11.3.2).

Example (The straight line again)

For X=[1,x], X⊤X=(nnx¯nx¯∑ixi2),X⊤y=(ny¯∑ixiyi). The determinant is n∑ixi2−n2x¯2=nSxx, positive iff the xi are not all equal, which is exactly when rank(X)=2. Inverting,

(X⊤X)−1=1nSxx(∑ixi2−nx¯−nx¯n)=(1n+x¯2Sxx−x¯Sxx−x¯Sxx1Sxx),
(5.4.4)

using ∑ixi2=Sxx+nx¯2. The second entry of (X⊤X)−1X⊤y is (∑ixiyi−nx¯y¯)/Sxx=Sxy/Sxx, and the first is y¯−x¯Sxy/Sxx. This recovers Theorem 5.2.1, and Eq. 5.4.3 reduces to Eq. 5.2.5.

5.4.2. Fitted values, residuals and the hat matrix

Substituting Eq. 5.4.2, the fitted values are

𝛃Y^=Xβ^=Hy,H=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤,
(5.4.5)

and the residuals are 𝛆ε^=(I−H)y. The n×n matrix H is the hat matrix, so called because it puts the hat on y. It depends only on the regressors, never on the response.

Proposition 5.4.2 (Algebra of the fit)

Let X have rank p, and let H, Y^ and 𝛆ε^ be as above.

  • (a) H is symmetric and idempotent, HX=X and (I−H)X=0, and trH=p, tr(I−H)=n−p.

  • (b) 𝛆X⊤ε^=0 and 𝛆Y^⊤ε^=0.

  • (c) 𝛆‖y‖2=‖Y^‖2+‖ε^‖2, and 𝛃SSE=y⊤(I−H)y=y⊤y−β^⊤X⊤y.

  • (d) If 1=Xc for some c (for example, if X has an intercept column), then ∑iε^i=0, the mean of the fitted values is y¯, and ∑i(yi−y¯)2=∑i(y^i−y¯)2+∑iε^i2, that is, SST=SSR+SSE.

  • (e) If 𝛃𝛆y=Xβ+ε for some 𝛃β and 𝛆ε, then 𝛃𝛃𝛆β^=β+(X⊤X)−1X⊤ε and 𝛆𝛆ε^=(I−H)ε.

Proof. (a) H⊤=X((X⊤X)−1)⊤X⊤=H, since the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric. HX=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤X=X, and then H2=HX(X⊤X)−1X⊤=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤=H. By the cyclic property (Theorem 1.6.1), trH=tr((X⊤X)−1X⊤X)=trIp=p. (b) 𝛆X⊤ε^=X⊤(I−H)y=((I−H)X)⊤y=0, and 𝛆𝛃𝛆Y^⊤ε^=β^⊤X⊤ε^=0. (c) 𝛆y=Y^+ε^ with 𝛆Y^⊤ε^=0 gives the first identity. For the second, (I−H) is symmetric and idempotent, so ‖(I−H)y‖2=y⊤(I−H)y; and 𝛃𝛃y⊤Hy=y⊤Xβ^=β^⊤X⊤y. (d) 𝛆𝛆1⊤ε^=c⊤X⊤ε^=0 by (b). Then ∑iy^i=∑iyi. Write yi−y¯=(y^i−y¯)+ε^i. The cross term 𝛆𝛆∑i(y^i−y¯)ε^i=Y^⊤ε^−y¯1⊤ε^=0. (e) Substitute 𝛃𝛆y=Xβ+ε into Eq. 5.4.2 and into (I−H)y, using (a).

Part (e) will carry most of the statistical theory. The estimation error 𝛃𝛃β^−β is a fixed linear function of the unobserved errors, and the residual vector does not depend on 𝛃β at all. Part (b) says that the residuals satisfy p independent linear constraints. That is the origin of the divisor n−p in Section 5.6.

For a model with an intercept, part (d) motivates the coefficient of determination R2=SSRSST=1−SSESST, the proportion of the variation of the response about its mean that the fitted values reproduce. It generalizes Proposition 5.2.2(d), and lies in [0,1]. It can only increase when a column is added to X (Exercise (B1)), which is why Chapter 9 introduces an adjusted version (Proposition 9.5.3). Section 6.7 interprets R as the cosine of an angle.

5.4.3. Centring the regressors

For a model with an intercept, write X=[1,X1], where X1 is n×k with column means x¯=X1⊤1/n. The centred regressors are the columns of Xc=X1−1x¯⊤=(I−1n11⊤)X1, each of which sums to zero. The next result is the matrix version of the formulas β^1=Sxy/Sxx and β^0=y¯−β^1x¯.

Proposition 5.4.3 (Centred form)

Let X=[1,X1] have rank p=k+1, and partition 𝛃𝛃β^=(β^0,β^1⊤)⊤. Then Xc has rank k, and 𝛃𝛃β^1=(Xc⊤Xc)−1Xc⊤y=(Xc⊤Xc)−1Xc⊤(y−y¯1),β^0=y¯−x¯⊤β^1.

Proof. For b=(b0,b1⊤)⊤, Xb=b01+X1b1=(b0+x¯⊤b1)1+Xcb1=[1,Xc]Tb,T=(1x¯⊤0Ik). T is nonsingular (it is upper triangular with unit diagonal), so [1,Xc]=XT−1 has rank p (Proposition 1.2.3(b)), and hence Xc has rank k. The criterion satisfies ‖y−Xb‖2=‖y−[1,Xc]a‖2 with a=Tb, and b↦Tb is a bijection of Rp. So 𝛃β^ minimizes the left side iff 𝛃a^=Tβ^ minimizes the right side. Because 1⊤Xc=0⊤, the normal equations for [1,Xc] are block diagonal, (n0⊤0Xc⊤Xc)a^=(ny¯Xc⊤y), giving a^0=y¯ and a^1=(Xc⊤Xc)−1Xc⊤y. Undoing T: 𝛃β^1=a^1 and β^0=a^0−x¯⊤a^1. Finally Xc⊤1=0, so Xc⊤y=Xc⊤(y−y¯1).

In words: to find the slopes, centre everything and fit without an intercept; the intercept then makes the fitted surface pass through the point of means (x¯,y¯). The matrix Xc⊤Xc/(n−1) is the sample covariance matrix of the regressors, and Xc⊤y/(n−1) their sample covariances with the response. So the slopes are “covariance divided by variance”, as in the straight-line case, with a matrix inverse in place of the division. This is the simplest case of the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem of Section 6.6, with the intercept playing the part of the regressors that are “partialled out”.

5.4.4. Indicator variables

The least squares estimates in a model with only indicator columns are simple averages.

Proposition 5.4.4 (Coefficients of indicator variables)

Suppose each case belongs to one of c≥2 groups, group k has nk≥1 cases with mean response y¯k, and zk is the indicator column of group k. Let X=[1,z1,…,zc−1], so that group c is the reference. Then X has full rank, and β^0=y¯c,β^k=y¯k−y¯c(k=1,…,c−1). The fitted value of every case is the mean of its group.

Proof. If Xb=0, the entries for a case in group c give b0=0, and those for a case in group k<c give b0+bk=0, so b=0: the rank is p=c. Let b be the proposed vector. Its fitted value for a case in group k<c is y¯c+(y¯k−y¯c)=y¯k, and for a case in group c it is y¯c. The residuals yi−y¯k(i) therefore sum to zero within each group, so zk⊤(y−Xb)=0 for every k=1,…,c. Every column of X is a sum of group indicators, since 1=z1+⋯+zc, so X⊤(y−Xb)=0: the normal equations hold. By Theorem 5.4.1, 𝛃b=β^.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
rand = sm.datasets.randhie.load_pandas().data
y = rand["mdvis"].to_numpy(dtype=float)
D = rand[["hlthg", "hlthf", "hlthp"]].to_numpy(dtype=float)   # good, fair, poor
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), D])                      # excellent = reference

beta_hat = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
level = D @ np.array([1, 2, 3])                                # 0 = excellent, ..., 3 = poor
means = np.array([y[level == k].mean() for k in range(4)])
print("coefficients:            ", np.round(beta_hat, 4))
print("group means:             ", np.round(means, 4))
print("means minus reference:   ", np.round(means - means[0], 4))

For the health data of Example (Self-rated health) the group sizes are 11,019 (excellent), 7,309 (good), 1,560 (fair) and 302 (poor), and the group means are 2.634, 2.902, 3.692 and 5.795 visits. The listing confirms that the coefficients are these means’ differences from the reference.

5.4.5. A worked example

Example (Money demand in Denmark)

Economic theory relates the amount of money people choose to hold to their income, which drives the volume of transactions, and to interest rates, which measure the return given up by holding money rather than bonds. A classic data set records, for n=55 quarters from 1974 to 1987 in Denmark, the logarithm of real money holdings (y), the logarithm of real income, the interest rate on bonds and the interest rate on deposits (rates as fractions per year). The model has p=4 columns: an intercept and the three regressors. Solving the normal equations gives 𝛃β^=(4.3945, 1.2958, −2.6163, 0.6186)⊤, with residual sum of squares SSE=0.09253 and R2=0.9262. The listing checks that 𝛆X⊤ε^ is zero up to rounding error, that the two formulas for SSE in Proposition 5.4.2(c) agree, and that the slopes computed from centred data (Proposition 5.4.3) are the same. Section 5.6 attaches standard errors to these numbers, and Section 5.7 interprets them.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
dk = sm.datasets.danish_data.load_pandas().data
y = dk["lrm"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), dk["lry"], dk["ibo"], dk["ide"]])
n, p = X.shape
print("n =", n, " p =", p)
XtX = X.T @ X
Xty = X.T @ y
beta_hat = np.linalg.solve(XtX, Xty)       # solve X^T X b = X^T y
fitted = X @ beta_hat
resid = y - fitted
SSE = resid @ resid

print("beta_hat =", np.round(beta_hat, 4))
print("X^T e    =", X.T @ resid)           # zero up to rounding
print("SSE two ways:", SSE, y @ y - beta_hat @ Xty)
Z = X[:, 1:] - X[:, 1:].mean(axis=0)       # centred regressors, no intercept column
slopes = np.linalg.solve(Z.T @ Z, Z.T @ (y - y.mean()))
intercept = y.mean() - X[:, 1:].mean(axis=0) @ slopes
print("slopes from centred data:", np.round(slopes, 4))
print("intercept recovered:     ", round(intercept, 4))

Warning (Formulas are not algorithms). The formula 𝛃β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y is for reasoning, not for computing. Forming X⊤X squares the condition number of the problem, and inverting it wastes work and accuracy. The listings solve the normal equations with a linear solver, which is acceptable for small, well-conditioned problems like these. Good software computes 𝛃β^ from an orthogonal factorization of X itself. Section 6.10 shows the loss of accuracy in an example, and Chapter 10 develops the algorithms (Theorem 10.2.1, Theorem 10.3.2).

5.4.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Suppose the columns of X are nonzero and mutually orthogonal. Show that β^j=xj⊤y/xj⊤xj for each j, so that each coefficient is the same as in the regression of y on xj alone. Show also that deleting a column does not change the other estimates.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let X=[X1,x] have full rank, and let SSE1 and SSE be the residual sums of squares from regressing y on X1 and on X. Show that SSE≤SSE1, directly from Definition 5.4.1. Deduce that R2 cannot decrease when a column is added to a model with an intercept.

Solution. Let 𝛃β^1 be the least squares estimate for X1. The vector 𝛃(β^1⊤,0)⊤ is one candidate in the minimization over b∈Rp, and it gives the value 𝛃‖y−X1β^1‖2=SSE1. The minimum over all candidates is at most this. With an intercept in X1, R2=1−SSE/SST and SST does not depend on the model, so R2 cannot decrease. Equality holds iff the extra coefficient in the larger fit is zero, because by Eq. 5.4.3 the candidate 𝛃(β^1⊤,0)⊤ is optimal only if it equals 𝛃β^.

Exercise (B2)

Let W=diag(w1,…,wn) with all wi>0, and X of full rank. Show that ∑iwi(yi−x(i)⊤b)2 has the unique minimizer (X⊤WX)−1X⊤Wy, by applying Theorem 5.4.1 to the rescaled data W1/2y and W1/2X. Which cases get most influence when the weights are the reciprocals of the error variances?

Solution. ∑iwi(yi−x(i)⊤b)2=‖W1/2y−W1/2Xb‖2. The matrix W1/2X has rank p, because W1/2 is nonsingular (Proposition 1.2.3(b)). By Theorem 5.4.1 the unique minimizer is ((W1/2X)⊤W1/2X)−1(W1/2X)⊤W1/2y=(X⊤WX)−1X⊤Wy. With wi=1/Var(Yi), the least variable cases count most. This is weighted least squares, the subject of Chapter 21 and, with a general covariance matrix, of Section 6.9.

Exercise (B3)

Show that 𝛃β^=Ay with A=(X⊤X)−1X⊤, that AX=Ip, and that AA⊤=(X⊤X)−1. Give an example of a different p×n matrix B with BX=Ip when n>p. (Chapter 7 compares all such estimators By.)

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let X have full rank and let a new case (x0,y0) be appended. Using Theorem 1.6.5, show that the new estimate is 𝛃𝛃𝛃β^new=β^+(X⊤X)−1x0(y0−x0⊤β^)1+x0⊤(X⊤X)−1x0. Interpret: the estimate moves in proportion to the new case’s prediction error. This is the basis of recursive least squares; Chapter 10 treats the numerically stable version.

5.5  Properties of the estimator under second-moment assumptions

Least squares was defined for a fixed data vector. Now let the data be random, generated by the linear model of Definition 5.3.1: 𝛃𝛆𝛆𝛆Y=Xβ+ε,E(ε)=0,Cov(ε)=σ2In, with X of full rank p. The estimator 𝛃β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤Y is then a random vector, and so are Y^ and 𝛆ε^. This section computes their means and covariances. Only the second-moment assumptions are used, so the results hold for skewed, heavy-tailed or discrete errors alike, as long as they have finite variance.

5.5.1. Mean and covariance of the estimator

The key observation is that 𝛃β^ is a linear function of Y: 𝛃β^=AY with the constant matrix A=(X⊤X)−1X⊤. The moments of a linear transformation were worked out in Chapter 2.

Theorem 5.5.1 (Moments of the least squares estimator)

Let X have full rank p and 𝛃β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤Y.

  • (a) If 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ, then 𝛃𝛃E(β^)=β: the least squares estimator is unbiased.

  • (b) If moreover Cov(Y)=σ2In, then

    𝛃Cov(β^)=σ2(X⊤X)−1.
    (5.5.1)

Proof. Let A=(X⊤X)−1X⊤. By Theorem 2.2.1(a), 𝛃𝛃𝛃E(β^)=AE(Y)=(X⊤X)−1X⊤Xβ=β. By Theorem 2.2.1(b), 𝛃Cov(β^)=A(σ2In)A⊤=σ2(X⊤X)−1X⊤X(X⊤X)−1=σ2(X⊤X)−1, using the symmetry of (X⊤X)−1.

The proof is two lines, but the division of labour between the assumptions is worth stating. Unbiasedness needs only (L1): the errors may have unequal variances or be correlated, and 𝛃β^ still centres on 𝛃β. The covariance formula needs (L2) and (L3) as well. Neither needs normality. Under normality 𝛃β^ is itself normal, since it is a linear function of a normal vector (Theorem 3.3.1), so 𝛃𝛃β^∼Np(β,σ2(X⊤X)−1). Chapter 7 builds exact inference on this (Theorem 7.5.1).

The matrix (X⊤X)−1 carries all the information about how the design affects precision. Its diagonal entries give the variances of the individual coefficients, and its off-diagonal entries their covariances. When two columns are strongly positively correlated, their coefficients are typically estimated with a strong negative correlation: the data can tell how large the two effects are together, but not how to split the total between them.

Corollary 5.5.2 (Linear functions of the coefficients)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 5.5.1(b), let a∈Rp and let L be a constant q×p matrix. Then 𝛃𝛃𝛃E(a⊤β^)=a⊤β,Var(a⊤β^)=σ2a⊤(X⊤X)−1a, and 𝛃Cov(Lβ^)=σ2L(X⊤X)−1L⊤. In particular, the estimated mean response at a design point x0, 𝛃μ^0=x0⊤β^, is unbiased for 𝛃μ0=x0⊤β with Var(μ^0)=σ2x0⊤(X⊤X)−1x0, and Var(β^j)=σ2[(X⊤X)−1]jj.

Proof. Apply Theorem 2.2.1 to 𝛃Lβ^, with covariance 𝛃LCov(β^)L⊤, and take L=a⊤, L=x0⊤ and L=ej⊤.

Linear functions of 𝛃β are what applications usually need: a single coefficient, the difference between two coefficients, the mean response at a new design point, or the change in mean response between two profiles. Chapter 7 shows that for every such function, 𝛃a⊤β^ has the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators (the Gauss–Markov theorem, Theorem 7.2.1).

5.5.2. Fitted values and residuals

Proposition 5.5.3 (Moments of fitted values and residuals)

Under the linear model with X of full rank and H=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤:

  • (a) 𝛃E(Y^)=Xβ and Cov(Y^)=σ2H;

  • (b) 𝛆E(ε^)=0 and 𝛆Cov(ε^)=σ2(I−H); in particular Var(ε^i)=σ2(1−hii), where hii is the ith diagonal entry of H;

  • (c) 𝛆Cov(Y^,ε^)=0 and 𝛃𝛆Cov(β^,ε^)=0;

  • (d) 0≤hii≤1 for every i, and ∑ihii=p.

Proof. Y^=HY and 𝛆ε^=(I−H)Y, with H symmetric and idempotent and HX=X (Proposition 5.4.2(a)). By Theorem 2.2.1, 𝛃𝛃E(Y^)=HXβ=Xβ and Cov(Y^)=σ2HH⊤=σ2H; similarly 𝛆𝛃E(ε^)=(I−H)Xβ=0 and 𝛆Cov(ε^)=σ2(I−H)2=σ2(I−H). The cross-covariances are Cov(HY,(I−H)Y)=σ2H(I−H)=0 and Cov(AY,(I−H)Y)=σ2A(I−H)=σ2(A−AH)=0, since AH=(X⊤X)−1X⊤X(X⊤X)−1X⊤=A. For (d), σ2hii and σ2(1−hii) are variances, so both are nonnegative, and ∑ihii=trH=p.

Two consequences deserve emphasis. First, residuals are not uncorrelated and not of equal variance, even when the errors are both. The residual of a case with hii near 1 has variance near zero: the fit is pulled so close to such a case that its residual says little about its error. The number hii is the leverage of case i (Section 6.8, Chapter 20). Second, the fitted values and the residuals are uncorrelated. Under normality they are then independent (Theorem 3.4.1), which is the key to the exact tests of Chapter 11.

5.5.3. The straight line

Specializing to X=[1,x], the inverse Eq. 5.4.4 gives the classical formulas at once.

Corollary 5.5.4 (Moments for a straight line)

In the model Eq. 5.2.1 with Sxx>0, Var(β^1)=σ2Sxx,Var(β^0)=σ2(1n+x¯2Sxx),Cov(β^0,β^1)=−σ2x¯Sxx, and the estimated mean response at x0 has

Var(β^0+β^1x0)=σ2(1n+(x0−x¯)2Sxx).
(5.5.2)

Both estimates are unbiased, and Y¯ is uncorrelated with β^1.

Proof. The first three are the entries of σ2(X⊤X)−1 in Eq. 5.4.4. For the mean response, Corollary 5.5.2 with x0=(1,x0)⊤ gives σ2(1n+x¯2Sxx−2x0x¯Sxx+x02Sxx), which rearranges to Eq. 5.5.2. Finally Y¯=β^0+β^1x¯ (the line passes through the point of means), and Cov(β^0+β^1x¯,β^1)=−σ2x¯/Sxx+x¯σ2/Sxx=0.

Three design lessons can be read off. The slope is estimated precisely when the xi are spread out, since its variance is inversely proportional to Sxx. The intercept is estimated precisely only near the data: its variance grows with x¯2, and the estimated intercept and slope are strongly negatively correlated when x¯ is large and positive, the tilting of the ellipses noted after Theorem 5.2.1. And the mean response is estimated best at x0=x¯, with a variance that grows quadratically as x0 moves away. Extrapolation is doubly risky: the model may fail outside the range of the data, and even if it holds, the estimate is imprecise there.

Example (Where to put the design points)

Suppose n=20 observations can be taken anywhere in [0,10] to estimate a slope. Three designs are compared in Figure 5.5.1. Equally spaced points give Sxx=184.21 and a slope standard deviation of 0.0737σ. Putting half the points at each end gives the largest possible Sxx=500.00 (Exercise (B1)) and 0.0447σ. Bunching the points in the middle, over [4,6], gives Sxx=7.37 and 0.3684σ, more than eight times worse than the two-ends design. The simulated sampling distributions in panel (b), each from 20,000 samples, match these standard deviations. The two-ends design has a price the variance formula does not show: with only two distinct values of x, the data cannot reveal curvature. The equally spaced design gives up some precision for the ability to check the model.

import numpy as np
n = 20
designs = {
    "equally spaced": np.linspace(0, 10, n),
    "two ends": np.repeat([0.0, 10.0], n // 2),
    "middle only": np.linspace(4, 6, n),
}
for name, xd in designs.items():
    Sxx = np.sum((xd - xd.mean()) ** 2)
    print(f"{name:15s} S_xx = {Sxx:7.2f}   sd(slope) = sigma * {1 / np.sqrt(Sxx):.4f}")

5.5.4. Only two moments are needed

Example (Skewed errors)

To see that normality plays no role in Theorem 5.5.1, fit the quadratic E(Y)=β0+β1x+β2x2 at x=1,…,12, with 𝛃β=(2,0.5,−0.03)⊤ and errors that are centred exponential variables scaled to σ=1.5. These errors are strongly skewed. Over 100,000 simulated data sets, the averages of the three estimates are 1.9974, 0.5005 and −0.0300, and the simulated standard deviations are 1.5421, 0.5457 and 0.0409, against 1.5503, 0.5483 and 0.0411 from Eq. 5.5.1. Every entry of the simulated covariance matrix is within 0.011 of the formula, measured as a correlation. The formula also predicts that β^1 and β^2 have correlation −0.973, because the columns x and x2 are nearly collinear on 1,…,12. A slightly larger linear coefficient can be almost exactly compensated by a slightly more negative quadratic one.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
x = np.arange(1.0, 13.0)                        # twelve design points
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x, x**2])
beta = np.array([2.0, 0.5, -0.03])
sigma = 1.5
reps = 100_000

A = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T)               # beta_hat = A y
E = sigma * (rng.exponential(size=(reps, len(x))) - 1.0)   # skewed, mean 0, sd sigma
B = (X @ beta + E) @ A.T                        # one estimate per row

print("mean of estimates:", np.round(B.mean(axis=0), 4), " true:", beta)
print("Monte Carlo covariance:\n", np.round(np.cov(B.T), 5))
print("sigma^2 (X^T X)^{-1}:\n", np.round(sigma**2 * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X), 5))

5.5.5. Random regressors

When the regressors are random, the assumptions are read conditionally on X (Section 5.1). The conditional results then transfer.

Corollary 5.5.5 (Random regressors)

Let (X,Y) be random, with rank(X)=p with probability one, and suppose 𝛃E(Y∣X)=Xβ and Cov(Y∣X)=σ2In. If E[(X⊤X)−1] is finite, then 𝛃𝛃𝛃E(β^)=β,Cov(β^)=σ2E[(X⊤X)−1].

Proof. Conditionally on X, Theorem 5.5.1 gives 𝛃𝛃E(β^∣X)=β and 𝛃Cov(β^∣X)=σ2(X⊤X)−1. By the laws of total expectation and covariance (Proposition 2.5.4), 𝛃𝛃𝛃E(β^)=E(β)=β and 𝛃𝛃Cov(β^)=E[σ2(X⊤X)−1]+Cov(β)=σ2E[(X⊤X)−1], since 𝛃β is constant. The finiteness assumption guarantees 𝛃β^ has finite second moments, which Proposition 2.5.4 needs.

This is what Example (One slope, two sampling schemes) showed by simulation: under the random design, the variance of the slope was σ2E(1/Sxx), not σ2/Sxx for any one design. For inference, the conditional covariance σ2(X⊤X)−1 is the relevant one, because it describes the design actually observed.

5.5.6. Consistency

As the sample grows, a good estimator should approach the truth. For a fixed p and a sequence of model matrices Xn, the mean squared error of 𝛃β^ is 𝛃𝛃𝛃E‖β^−β‖2=trCov(β^)=σ2tr(Xn⊤Xn)−1, by Theorem 5.5.1 and the fact that the trace of a covariance matrix is the sum of the variances.

Proposition 5.5.6 (Consistency in mean square)

Under the linear model with Xn of full rank p for every n, if tr(Xn⊤Xn)−1→0, equivalently if the smallest eigenvalue of Xn⊤Xn tends to infinity, then 𝛃𝛃E‖β^−β‖2→0, and hence 𝛃𝛃Pr(‖β^−β‖>δ)→0 for every δ>0.

Proof. The first claim is the display above. The eigenvalues of (Xn⊤Xn)−1 are the reciprocals 1/λ1,…,1/λp of those of Xn⊤Xn, all positive, so the trace is ∑j1/λj. It tends to zero iff every λj→∞, iff the smallest does. The last claim is Markov’s inequality applied to 𝛃𝛃‖β^−β‖2: 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃Pr(‖β^−β‖>δ)≤E‖β^−β‖2/δ2.

For a straight line the condition is Sxx→∞ together with x¯2/Sxx→0, which holds for any design that keeps spreading, but fails, for instance, for xi=1/i, where Sxx converges to a finite limit. Then even infinitely many observations do not pin down the slope (Exercise (C1)).

5.5.7. What if the assumptions fail?

The proof of Theorem 5.5.1 shows exactly what each assumption contributes, so it also shows what goes wrong without it. Two cases recur throughout the book.

Proposition 5.5.7 (Moments under departures)

Let X have full rank, A=(X⊤X)−1X⊤ and 𝛃β^=AY.

  • (a) If 𝛃𝛅E(Y)=Xβ+δ for some 𝛅δ∈Rn, then 𝛃𝛃𝛅E(β^)=β+(X⊤X)−1X⊤δ.

  • (b) If 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ and Cov(Y)=Σ for some covariance matrix Σ, then 𝛃𝛃E(β^)=β and

    𝛃Cov(β^)=(X⊤X)−1X⊤ΣX(X⊤X)−1.
    (5.5.3)

Proof. Both are Theorem 2.2.1 applied to AY: 𝛃𝛅E(AY)=AXβ+Aδ and Cov(AY)=AΣA⊤.

Part (a) says that when the mean is misspecified, least squares estimates not 𝛃β but 𝛃β plus the coefficients from regressing the missing part 𝛅δ on X. The most important case is an omitted regressor, which Section 5.7 examines. Part (b) says that with the wrong covariance, the estimator stays unbiased but the formula σ2(X⊤X)−1 is wrong. The “sandwich” Eq. 5.5.3 is the correct covariance, and Chapter 21 shows how to estimate it when Σ is diagonal but unknown. Chapter 19 studies both departures systematically.

5.5.8. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In the model of Proposition 5.4.4 with c=2 groups, show that Var(β^1)=σ2(1/n1+1/n2), both from Eq. 5.5.1 and directly from β^1=Y¯1−Y¯2. For fixed n=n1+n2, which split minimizes it?

Exercise (A2)

Show that for a straight line hii=1/n+(xi−x¯)2/Sxx. Which case in Example (Forecasting December sea temperature) has the largest leverage? Check that the leverages sum to 2.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Suppose n is even and the xi may be chosen anywhere in [a,b]. Show that Sxx≤n(b−a)2/4, with equality iff half the points are at a and half at b. Hint: |xi−c|≤(b−a)/2 with c=(a+b)/2, and Sxx=∑i(xi−c)2−n(x¯−c)2.

Solution. With c the midpoint, Sxx=∑i(xi−c)2−n(x¯−c)2≤∑i(xi−c)2≤n(b−a)2/4. Equality in the second step needs every xi∈{a,b}, and in the first step needs x¯=c, that is, equally many points at each end. For [0,10] and n=20 this gives Sxx=20⋅25=500, the value in Example (Where to put the design points).

Exercise (B2)

In Example (Money demand in Denmark), write the variances of β^2−β^3 and β^2+β^3, the difference and the sum of the two interest-rate coefficients, in terms of the entries of G=(X⊤X)−1. Show that if β^2 and β^3 are negatively correlated, the difference is estimated less precisely, and the sum more precisely, than they would be if the two estimates were uncorrelated. The two rate columns have sample correlation 0.80. Which combination would you expect to be better determined?

Solution. With a=(0,0,1,∓1)⊤ and indices counting the intercept as the first coefficient, Corollary 5.5.2 gives Var(β^2∓β^3)=σ2(g33+g44∓2g34), and Cov(β^2,β^3)=σ2g34. If g34<0, the variance of the difference exceeds σ2(g33+g44)=Var(β^2)+Var(β^3), and that of the sum falls below it. Positively correlated columns tend to produce negatively correlated estimates (as in Example (Skewed errors)), because the data can tell how large the combined effect of the two rates is more easily than how to divide it between them. So the sum should be the better determined combination.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let xi=1/i for i=1,2,… in the straight-line model. Show that Sxx converges to a finite limit as n→∞, so that Var(β^1) does not tend to zero. Then construct a sequence of designs, in which the points may change with n (for example, designs centred farther and farther from 0), with Sxx→∞ but x¯2/Sxx↛0, and decide whether the slope, the intercept, or both fail to be consistent.

Solution. With xi=1/i, Sxx=∑i≤n1/i2−nx¯2. The sum increases to π2/6, and nx¯2=Hn2/n→0 because the harmonic number Hn=∑i≤n1/i grows like log⁡n. So Sxx→π2/6 and Var(β^1)=σ2/Sxx stays above 6σ2/π2.

For even n take xi=n+(−1)i, i=1,…,n: half the points at n−1, half at n+1. Then x¯=n and Sxx=n, so x¯2/Sxx=n→∞. By Corollary 5.5.4, Var(β^1)=σ2/n→0, so the slope is consistent (Chebyshev’s inequality), while Var(β^0)=σ2(1/n+n)→∞. The intercept, the mean response at x=0, ever farther from the data, is not consistent: with normal errors, β^0−β0 is normal with unbounded variance.

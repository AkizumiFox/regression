# Exponential dispersion families

The normal linear model makes two assumptions usually stated together but logically
independent: that \( \E(Y_i) \) is linear in the regressors, and that \( Y_i \) is normal with
a variance not depending on that mean. This section replaces the second by the least it can,
a one-parameter family whose log-density is linear in the data with a single extra scale.
[Section 7.4](../ch07-optimality/04-minimum-variance.html) already wrote the normal linear
model in that form (@eq-opt-expfam); what follows names the ingredients, because in a
generalized linear model each does a separate job.

## The family

::: {#def-glm-edf}
[Exponential dispersion family]

Let \( \nu \) be a \( \sigma \)-finite measure on \( \Real \). A random variable \( Y \) belongs to the
**exponential dispersion family** generated by \( \nu \) if, for a dispersion \( \phi>0 \) and a
known **prior weight** \( w>0 \), its density with respect to \( \nu \) is
\[
f(y;\theta,\phi)=\exp\Bigl\{\frac{w\,\{y\theta-b(\theta)\}}{\phi}+c(y,\phi,w)\Bigr\},
\]{#eq-glm-edf}

where \( \theta \) lies in the **natural parameter space**
\[
\Theta=\Bigl\{\theta\in\Real:\ \kappa(\theta)=\int e^{w y\theta/\phi}\,e^{c(y,\phi,w)}\,d\nu(y)<\infty\Bigr\}
\]
and \( b(\theta)=(\phi/w)\log\kappa(\theta) \) is the **cumulant function**, required to be the
*same* function of \( \theta \) for every admissible pair \( (\phi,w) \). The parameter
\( \theta \) is the **natural** (or canonical) parameter.
:::

Four ingredients, four jobs. The measure \( \nu \) fixes what kind of thing the response is:
Lebesgue measure on \( \Real \) for the normal, on \( (0,\infty) \) for the gamma, counting
measure on \( \{0,1,\dots\} \) for the Poisson. The cumulant function \( b \) determines every
moment, as this section shows. The dispersion \( \phi \) is a nuisance scale, unknown for the
normal and the gamma, fixed at \( 1 \) for the Poisson and the binomial. The prior weight
\( w \) is known and records how much data each observation summarizes: \( 1 \) for a single
observation, \( m \) when \( y \) is an average of \( m \) of them. Only \( \phi/w \) appears, so
\( w \) never needs estimating; and that one formalism covers \( w=1 \) and \( w=m \) at once is
what the common-\( b \) requirement buys, since it forces \( c \) to absorb the whole dependence
on \( (\phi,w) \). That is what makes the family a *dispersion* family (Jørgensen 1987).

The function \( c \) does no statistical work: it absorbs whatever is left once the term
linear in \( y\theta \) has been extracted, and never depends on \( \theta \). That is the
point, because in the log-likelihood
\[
\log f(y;\theta,\phi)=\frac{w\{y\theta-b(\theta)\}}{\phi}+c(y,\phi,w),
\]{#eq-glm-loglik-one}

the data and the parameter of interest meet only in the product \( y\theta \).
By @lem-opt-factorization, \( y \) itself is sufficient for \( \theta \).

The normalization \( b=(\phi/w)\log\kappa \) is forced, because a density must integrate to
one. The next lemma records the two properties of \( b \) that everything else rests on.

::: {#lem-glm-convex}
[The natural parameter space and the cumulant function]

\( \Theta \) is a convex subset of \( \Real \), and \( b \) is convex on \( \Theta \). If \( Y \) is not
degenerate under some \( \theta_0\in\Theta \), then \( b \) is strictly convex on \( \Theta \).
:::

::: {.proof}
Write \( h(y)=e^{c(y,\phi,w)} \) and \( s=w/\phi>0 \), so that \( \kappa(\theta)=\int e^{sy\theta}h(y)\,d\nu(y) \).
Let \( \theta_1,\theta_2\in\Theta \) and \( \alpha\in(0,1) \). Hölder's inequality with exponents
\( 1/\alpha \) and \( 1/(1-\alpha) \) applied to the factors \( (e^{sy\theta_1}h)^{\alpha} \) and
\( (e^{sy\theta_2}h)^{1-\alpha} \) gives
\[
\kappa\bigl(\alpha\theta_1+(1-\alpha)\theta_2\bigr)
\le\kappa(\theta_1)^{\alpha}\kappa(\theta_2)^{1-\alpha}<\infty .
\]
So \( \alpha\theta_1+(1-\alpha)\theta_2\in\Theta \), which makes \( \Theta \) convex, and taking
logarithms shows \( \log\kappa \), hence \( b \), is convex. Equality in Hölder's inequality
requires \( e^{sy\theta_1}h \) and \( e^{sy\theta_2}h \) to be proportional \( \nu \)-almost
everywhere, that is, \( e^{sy(\theta_1-\theta_2)} \) constant on the set where \( h>0 \). For
\( \theta_1\ne\theta_2 \) this forces that set to be a single point, so \( Y \) is degenerate.
:::

Throughout the chapter \( \Theta \) is assumed to have nonempty interior and \( \theta \) to lie
in it — the same regularity @lem-opt-exponential-family needs, and what makes the
differentiations below legal.

## Moments from the cumulant function

::: {#prp-glm-moments}
[Mean, variance and cumulants]

Let \( Y \) have density @eq-glm-edf with \( \theta \) in the interior of \( \Theta \). Then \( Y \) has
moments of all orders, and

::: {.enumerate options="label=(\alph*)"}
1. \( \mu:=\E(Y)=b'(\theta) \) and \( \Var(Y)=\dfrac{\phi}{w}\,b''(\theta) \);

2. the map \( \theta\mapsto b'(\theta) \) is strictly increasing, hence a bijection from the
   interior of \( \Theta \) onto an open interval \( \mathcal M \), the **mean space**;

3. the cumulant generating function of \( Y \) is
   \( K(t)=\log\E e^{tY}=\dfrac{w}{\phi}\bigl\{b(\theta+t\phi/w)-b(\theta)\bigr\} \) for small
   \( \lvert t\rvert \), so the \( r \)th cumulant of \( Y \) is \( (\phi/w)^{r-1}b^{(r)}(\theta) \).
:::

:::

::: {.proof}
With \( h \) and \( s=w/\phi \) as in the proof of @lem-glm-convex, the density is
\( \kappa(\theta)^{-1}e^{sy\theta}h(y) \). The function \( \kappa \) is a two-sided Laplace
transform of the measure \( h\,d\nu \) evaluated at \( -s\theta \); such a transform is analytic
in the interior of its domain of convergence, and may be differentiated under the integral
sign there, together with the finiteness of all moments (Widder 1941, chapter II;
Brown 1986, chapter 2). Hence
\[
\kappa'(\theta)=s\int y\,e^{sy\theta}h\,d\nu=s\,\E(Y)\,\kappa(\theta),\qquad
\kappa''(\theta)=s^2\,\E(Y^2)\,\kappa(\theta).
\]
Since \( b=s^{-1}\log\kappa \), we have \( b'=\kappa'/(s\kappa)=\E(Y) \), which is (a) for the
mean. Differentiating \( \kappa'=s\,b'\kappa \) once more gives
\( \kappa''=s\,b''\kappa+s^2(b')^2\kappa \), so \( \E(Y^2)=b''/s+(b')^2 \) and
\( \Var(Y)=b''(\theta)/s=\phi\,b''(\theta)/w \).

(b) By @lem-glm-convex, \( b \) is strictly convex, so \( b'=\E(Y) \) is strictly increasing;
it is continuous, so its range on an open interval is an open interval.

(c) For \( t \) small enough that \( \theta+t/s\in\Theta \),
\( \E e^{tY}=\kappa(\theta)^{-1}\int e^{(s\theta+t)y}h\,d\nu=\kappa(\theta+t/s)/\kappa(\theta) \);
taking logarithms gives \( K(t)=s^{-1}\{b(\theta+t/s)-b(\theta)\} \) with \( s^{-1}=\phi/w \), and
the coefficient of \( t^r/r! \) in its expansion is \( s^{-(r-1)}b^{(r)}(\theta) \).
:::

Part (a) is the engine of the chapter: the variance is not free but determined by the mean,
up to the single scalar \( \phi/w \). Part (b) lets us reparameterize by the mean, which is
the parameter a modeller wants.

::: {#def-glm-variance}
[Variance function]

For \( \mu\in\mathcal M \) let \( \theta(\mu) \) be the inverse of \( b' \) from @prp-glm-moments(b).
The **variance function** of the family is \( V(\mu)=b''\bigl(\theta(\mu)\bigr) \), so that
\[
\Var(Y)=\frac{\phi}{w}\,V(\mu),\qquad \frac{d\theta}{d\mu}=\frac{1}{V(\mu)} .
\]
:::

The last identity is the derivative of an inverse function, \( d\mu/d\theta=b''=V \), and is
used constantly below. The pair \( (\mathcal M,V) \) determines the family: integrating \( V \)
gives \( \theta(\mu) \), hence \( b \), hence the density.

::: {.idea}
A generalized linear model is specified by three separate choices: the **variance function**
\( V \), which fixes the family; the **link**; and the **linear predictor** \( \X\bbeta \).
[Chapter 38](../ch38-quasi-likelihood/index.html) keeps the first two and throws the density away, which is possible because the
estimating equations of [Section 34.3](03-likelihood-equations.html) involve nothing else.
:::

## The standard families

The five classical families, plus the negative binomial with a known shape \( k \), come from
choosing \( \nu \) and reading off \( b \). The gamma row is written for shape \( a \), so that
\( \phi=1/a \). The measure \( \nu \) is Lebesgue on \( \Real \) for the normal and on
\( (0,\infty) \) for the gamma and inverse Gaussian, counting measure on
\( \{0,1,2,\dots\} \) for the Poisson and negative binomial, and counting measure on
\( \{0,1/m,\dots,1\} \) for the binomial, whose response is a proportion out of \( m \).

| Family | \( \theta(\mu) \) | \( b(\theta) \) | \( \phi \) | \( V(\mu) \) | \( \mathcal M \) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| normal | \( \mu \) | \( \theta^2/2 \) | \( \sigma^2 \) | \( 1 \) | \( \Real \) |
| binomial | \( \log\dfrac{\mu}{1-\mu} \) | \( \log(1+e^{\theta}) \) | \( 1 \) | \( \mu(1-\mu) \) | \( (0,1) \) |
| Poisson | \( \log\mu \) | \( e^{\theta} \) | \( 1 \) | \( \mu \) | \( (0,\infty) \) |
| gamma | \( -1/\mu \) | \( -\log(-\theta) \) | \( 1/a \) | \( \mu^2 \) | \( (0,\infty) \) |
| inverse Gaussian | \( -1/(2\mu^2) \) | \( -\sqrt{-2\theta} \) | \( \sigma^2 \) | \( \mu^3 \) | \( (0,\infty) \) |
| neg. binomial | \( \log\dfrac{\mu}{\mu+k} \) | \( -k\log(1-e^{\theta}) \) | \( 1 \) | \( \mu(1+\mu/k) \) | \( (0,\infty) \) |

Two entries deserve a full derivation, because they show the two ways the prior weight and
the dispersion enter.

::: {#exm-glm-binomial}
[The binomial as a family of proportions]

Let \( S\sim\text{Binomial}(m,\mu) \) and let \( Y=S/m \) be the observed proportion. Its
probability mass function at \( y=s/m \) is
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\binom{m}{my}\mu^{my}(1-\mu)^{m(1-y)}\\
&\qquad=\exp\Bigl\{m\Bigl(y\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}+\log(1-\mu)\Bigr)+\log\binom{m}{my}\Bigr\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
This is @eq-glm-edf with \( w=m \), \( \phi=1 \), \( \theta=\log\{\mu/(1-\mu)\} \) and
\( b(\theta)=-\log(1-\mu)=\log(1+e^{\theta}) \). Then \( b'(\theta)=e^{\theta}/(1+e^{\theta})=\mu \)
and \( b''(\theta)=\mu(1-\mu) \), so @prp-glm-moments gives \( \E(Y)=\mu \) and
\( \Var(Y)=\mu(1-\mu)/m \): the number of trials acts entirely through the prior weight. The
dispersion is fixed at \( 1 \), so a proportion more variable than \( \mu(1-\mu)/m \) needs the
different treatment of [Chapter 38](../ch38-quasi-likelihood/index.html).
:::

::: {#exm-glm-gamma}
[The gamma and its constant coefficient of variation]

Let \( Y \) have the gamma density with shape \( a>0 \) and mean \( \mu \),
\[
f(y)=\frac{(a/\mu)^{a}}{\Gamma(a)}\,y^{a-1}e^{-ay/\mu}
\quad\text{on }(0,\infty).
\]
Its logarithm is
\[
a\Bigl\{y\Bigl(-\frac1\mu\Bigr)-\log\mu\Bigr\}
+(a-1)\log y+a\log a-\log\Gamma(a),
\]
which is @eq-glm-edf with \( w=1 \), \( \phi=1/a \), \( \theta=-1/\mu \) and
\( b(\theta)=\log\mu=-\log(-\theta) \). Then \( b'(\theta)=-1/\theta=\mu \) and
\( b''(\theta)=1/\theta^2=\mu^2 \), so \( \Var(Y)=\phi\mu^2 \) and the coefficient of variation
\( \sqrt{\Var(Y)}/\mu=\sqrt{\phi} \) is free of the mean: the spread is *proportional* to the
level, the defining feature of the gamma family as a regression model. With
\( \mu=4 \) and \( \phi=0.4 \) the formula gives \( \Var(Y)=6.400 \), and
by @prp-glm-moments(c) the third cumulant is \( \phi^2b'''(\theta)=2\phi^2\mu^3=20.480 \);
both agree with the exact gamma moments in the listing below.
:::

```{.python .run #cell-families-table}
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Each family: theta(mu), b(theta) and its first three derivatives, and the exact
# distribution of Y, so that the exponential-family formulas can be checked.
FAMILIES = {
    "normal": dict(
        mu=2.0, phi=1.5, w=1.0,
        theta=lambda mu: mu,
        b=lambda t: t ** 2 / 2, b1=lambda t: t, b2=lambda t: 1.0 + 0 * t, b3=lambda t: 0 * t,
        dist=lambda mu, phi, w: stats.norm(mu, np.sqrt(phi / w)),
    ),
    "binomial": dict(                                  # Y = S/m, the sample proportion
        mu=0.3, phi=1.0, w=10.0,
        theta=lambda mu: np.log(mu / (1 - mu)),
        b=lambda t: np.log1p(np.exp(t)),
        b1=lambda t: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-t)),
        b2=lambda t: np.exp(-t) / (1 + np.exp(-t)) ** 2,
        b3=lambda t: np.exp(-t) * (np.exp(-t) - 1) / (1 + np.exp(-t)) ** 3,
        dist=None,                                     # handled separately: Y is m^-1 Binomial(m, mu)
    ),
    "Poisson": dict(
        mu=3.5, phi=1.0, w=1.0,
        theta=lambda mu: np.log(mu),
        b=lambda t: np.exp(t), b1=np.exp, b2=np.exp, b3=np.exp,
        dist=lambda mu, phi, w: stats.poisson(mu),
    ),
    "gamma": dict(                                     # shape a = 1/phi
        mu=4.0, phi=0.4, w=1.0,
        theta=lambda mu: -1 / mu,
        b=lambda t: -np.log(-t), b1=lambda t: -1 / t, b2=lambda t: 1 / t ** 2,
        b3=lambda t: -2 / t ** 3,
        dist=lambda mu, phi, w: stats.gamma(a=w / phi, scale=mu * phi / w),
    ),
    "inverse Gaussian": dict(
        mu=2.0, phi=0.4, w=1.0,
        theta=lambda mu: -1 / (2 * mu ** 2),
        b=lambda t: -np.sqrt(-2 * t), b1=lambda t: (-2 * t) ** -0.5,
        b2=lambda t: (-2 * t) ** -1.5, b3=lambda t: 3 * (-2 * t) ** -2.5,
        dist=lambda mu, phi, w: stats.invgauss(mu=mu * phi / w, scale=w / phi),
    ),
}

def check(name, spec, k=None):
    """Compare b'(theta), phi b''(theta)/w and (phi/w)^2 b'''(theta) with the true moments."""
    mu, phi, w = spec["mu"], spec["phi"], spec["w"]
    t = spec["theta"](mu)
    mean_ef = spec["b1"](t)
    var_ef = phi * spec["b2"](t) / w
    cum3_ef = (phi / w) ** 2 * spec["b3"](t)
    if name == "binomial":                             # Y = S/m with S ~ Binomial(m, mu)
        m = int(w)
        s = np.arange(m + 1)
        p = stats.binom(m, mu).pmf(s)
        y = s / m
        mean, var = y @ p, (y - mu) ** 2 @ p
        cum3 = (y - mu) ** 3 @ p
    else:
        d = spec["dist"](mu, phi, w)
        mean, var = d.mean(), d.var()
        cum3 = d.stats("s") * var ** 1.5               # third cumulant = skewness * sd^3
    return mean_ef, mean, var_ef, var, cum3_ef, float(cum3)

for name, spec in FAMILIES.items():
    m_ef, m, v_ef, v, c_ef, c = check(name, spec)
    print(f"{name:17s} mean {m_ef:8.4f} {m:8.4f}   var {v_ef:8.4f} {v:8.4f}   cum3 {c_ef:8.4f} {c:8.4f}")
```

The variance functions are drawn in
[Figure 34.1.1](01-exponential-dispersion-families.html#fig-glm-variance-functions). The
normal, Poisson, gamma and inverse Gaussian are powers, \( V(\mu)=\mu^{\,\xi} \) with
\( \xi=0,1,2,3 \); interpolating between them gives a continuum of families
(Jørgensen 1987) that includes a useful model for a response with a point mass at zero and a
continuous positive part. The remaining two are not powers: the binomial variance vanishes
at both ends of \( (0,1) \), because a proportion pinned near \( 0 \) or \( 1 \) cannot vary, and
the negative binomial variance exceeds the Poisson variance by an amount set by \( k \).

::: {#exm-glm-negbin}
[The negative binomial with known shape]

Let \( \Pr(Y=y)=\frac{\Gamma(y+k)}{\Gamma(k)\,y!}\,\pi^{y}(1-\pi)^{k} \), \( y=0,1,2,\dots \), for a
fixed \( k>0 \). Writing \( \theta=\log\pi \) puts this in the form @eq-glm-edf with \( w=1 \),
\( \phi=1 \) and \( b(\theta)=-k\log(1-e^{\theta}) \), so that
\[
\mu=b'(\theta)=\frac{k\pi}{1-\pi},\qquad
b''(\theta)=\frac{k\pi}{(1-\pi)^2}=\mu\Bigl(1+\frac{\mu}{k}\Bigr)
\]
after substituting \( \pi=\mu/(\mu+k) \). With \( k=2 \) and \( \mu=3 \) this gives
\( \Var(Y)=7.500 \), against \( 3 \) for a Poisson variable with the same mean.

The caveat is essential: this is an exponential dispersion family **only for fixed \( k \)**.
The shape enters the variance function, not the dispersion, so it cannot be divided out, and
letting \( k \) vary leaves the form @eq-glm-edf. Estimating \( k \) is outside this chapter's
machinery; [Chapter 37](../ch37-counts/index.html) takes it up.
:::

## Sufficiency, and what the natural parameter buys

Suppose \( Y_1,\dots,Y_n \) are independent, each with density @eq-glm-edf, with a common
\( b \) and \( \phi \), known weights \( w_i \), and natural parameters \( \theta_i \). The joint
log-likelihood is
\[
\ell=\frac1\phi\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\{y_i\theta_i-b(\theta_i)\}+\sum_{i=1}^n c(y_i,\phi,w_i).
\]{#eq-glm-joint-loglik}

Nothing has been assumed yet about how the \( \theta_i \) depend on regressors — that is the
next section's business — but @eq-glm-joint-loglik already shows what to expect. If the
\( \theta_i \) are constrained to a \( p \)-dimensional linear family
\( \theta_i=\x_{(i)}\T\bbeta \), then \( \sum_iw_iy_i\theta_i=\bbeta\T\X\T\diag(\bw)\y \) and
the data enter the likelihood only through the \( p \) numbers \( \X\T\diag(\bw)\y \). With
\( \phi \) known the remaining term \( \sum_ic(y_i,\phi,w_i) \) is a function of the data
alone, so @lem-opt-factorization makes this vector sufficient for \( \bbeta \), and if the
attainable \( \bbeta \) contain an open subset of \( \Real^p \),
@lem-opt-exponential-family makes it complete. With \( \phi \) unknown that term depends on a
parameter, both arguments fail, and a second statistic is needed — as in the normal linear
model, the case \( b(\theta)=\theta^2/2 \), \( w_i=1 \), where \( \X\T\y \) is accompanied by
\( \y\T\y \) for \( \sigma^2 \) (@prp-opt-complete-sufficient).

That is a genuine reduction, and it is why the natural parameter deserves its name. What it
does not give is any analogue of the Gauss–Markov theorem: the maximum likelihood estimate
is a nonlinear function of \( \y \), generally biased, with an exact distribution unknown
outside the normal case. Everything finite-sample that Parts II and III delivered is gone,
and [Section 34.5](05-deviance-and-inference.html) replaces it with asymptotics.

::: {.warning}
The reduction needs \( \theta_i \) — not \( \mu_i \), and not some other function of it — to be
linear in \( \bbeta \). That is the *canonical link*, one modelling choice among several. With
any other link the reduction fails and the data enter the likelihood through the whole
vector \( \y \); [Section 34.2](02-link-functions.html) explains why one might accept that.
:::

## Exercises

### A. Check your understanding

::: {#exr-glm-poisson-edf}
[A1]

Write the Poisson probability mass function in the form @eq-glm-edf, identify
\( \theta \), \( b \), \( \phi \), \( w \) and \( c \), and verify \( \E(Y)=\Var(Y)=\mu \)
using @prp-glm-moments. What is \( \Theta \)?
:::

::: {.solution}
\( \Pr(Y=y)=e^{-\mu}\mu^y/y!=\exp\{y\log\mu-\mu-\log y!\} \), so \( \theta=\log\mu \),
\( b(\theta)=e^{\theta} \), \( \phi=w=1 \) and \( c(y)=-\log y! \). Then \( b'=b''=e^{\theta}=\mu \),
and @prp-glm-moments gives \( \E(Y)=\Var(Y)=\mu \). The sum \( \sum_ye^{y\theta}/y!=e^{e^{\theta}} \)
converges for every real \( \theta \), so \( \Theta=\Real \) and \( \mathcal M=(0,\infty) \).
:::

### B. Practice

::: {#exr-glm-invgauss}
[B1]

Verify the inverse Gaussian row of the table: starting from
\[
f(y)=(2\pi\sigma^2y^3)^{-1/2}\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2\mu^2y}\Bigr\}
\quad\text{on }(0,\infty),
\]
put it in the form @eq-glm-edf and confirm \( V(\mu)=\mu^3 \). What is \( \Theta \), and what
happens at \( \theta=0 \)?
:::

::: {.solution}
Expanding the square, \( -(y-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2\mu^2y)=-y/(2\sigma^2\mu^2)+1/(\sigma^2\mu)-1/(2\sigma^2y) \).
So the exponent is \( \sigma^{-2}\{y\theta-b(\theta)\} \) with \( \theta=-1/(2\mu^2) \) and
\( b(\theta)=-1/\mu=-\sqrt{-2\theta} \), while the remaining terms
\( -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2y^3)-1/(2\sigma^2y) \) make up \( c(y,\phi,w) \) with \( \phi=\sigma^2 \),
\( w=1 \). Then \( b'(\theta)=(-2\theta)^{-1/2}=\mu \) and \( b''(\theta)=(-2\theta)^{-3/2}=\mu^3 \).
Here \( \Theta=(-\infty,0] \) and \( \theta=0 \) is a boundary point: the integral still converges
there, but \( b' \) blows up, so \( \mathcal M=(0,\infty) \) is the image of the *interior* only.
:::

::: {#exr-glm-variance-determines}
[B2]

Show that the variance function determines the family: if \( V \) is given on an interval
\( \mathcal M \), then \( \theta(\mu)=\int^{\mu}dt/V(t) \) and
\( b(\theta(\mu))=\int^{\mu}t\,dt/V(t) \), each up to constants that only relocate the density.
Carry this out for \( V(\mu)=\mu(1-\mu) \) on \( (0,1) \) and recover the binomial row.
:::

::: {.solution}
By @def-glm-variance, \( d\theta/d\mu=1/V(\mu) \), which integrates to the stated
\( \theta(\mu) \). Also \( db/d\mu=(db/d\theta)(d\theta/d\mu)=\mu/V(\mu) \), giving the second
integral. For \( V(\mu)=\mu(1-\mu) \), partial fractions give
\( \int d\mu/\{\mu(1-\mu)\}=\log\{\mu/(1-\mu)\}=\theta \), and
\( \int\mu\,d\mu/\{\mu(1-\mu)\}=-\log(1-\mu)=\log(1+e^{\theta})=b(\theta) \), which is the
binomial row.
:::

::: {#exr-glm-skewness}
[B3]

Use @prp-glm-moments(c) to show that the skewness of \( Y \) is
\( \gamma_1=\sqrt{\phi/w}\;V'(\mu)/\sqrt{V(\mu)} \). Evaluate it for the Poisson, the gamma and
the binomial, and say in each case how the skewness behaves as the mean moves.
:::

::: {.solution}
The third cumulant is \( (\phi/w)^2b'''(\theta) \), and
\( b'''(\theta)=dV/d\theta=V'(\mu)\,d\mu/d\theta=V'(\mu)V(\mu) \). Dividing by
\( \Var(Y)^{3/2}=\{(\phi/w)V\}^{3/2} \) gives \( \gamma_1=\sqrt{\phi/w}\,V'(\mu)/\sqrt{V(\mu)} \).
For the Poisson (\( \phi=w=1 \), \( V=\mu \)), \( \gamma_1=\mu^{-1/2} \): small counts are strongly
skewed and large ones nearly symmetric. For the gamma, \( V=\mu^2 \) gives
\( \gamma_1=2\sqrt{\phi} \), free of the mean. For the binomial with \( w=m \),
\( \gamma_1=(1-2\mu)/\sqrt{m\mu(1-\mu)} \), which changes sign at \( \mu=\tfrac12 \).
:::

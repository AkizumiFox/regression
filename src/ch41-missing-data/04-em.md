# The EM algorithm

Maximizing the observed-data likelihood @eq-mis-observed-likelihood directly means
differentiating an integral. EM avoids that: it alternates between filling in the
missing pieces of the *complete-data log-likelihood* and maximizing the filled-in
version, and it never decreases the observed-data likelihood. The iteration of
@thm-dsn-missing(c) — fill each lost plot with its fitted value, refit, repeat — is
the oldest instance in this book.

## The two steps

Let \( \ell(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\log f(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}) \)
and \( \ell_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\log f(\mathbf{D}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}) \)
be the observed-data and complete-data log-likelihoods, the second a function of data
we do not have. The mechanism is ignorable throughout, so @thm-mis-ignorable licenses
working with \( \ell \).

::: {#def-mis-em}
[EM]

Given a current value \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)} \), define
\[
Q(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})
=\E\bigl\{\ell_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\ \big|\
\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}\bigr\},
\]{#eq-mis-expected-complete}

the expectation being over \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}} \) under the conditional
density \( f(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}) \).
The **E step** computes \( Q(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}) \); the **M step**
sets \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)} \) to a maximizer of it. If the M step only
*increases* \( Q \), the scheme is called **generalized EM**.
:::

The E step is not "fill in the missing values"; it fills in whatever functions of them
the complete-data log-likelihood contains. When \( \ell_{\mathrm{c}} \) is linear in a
set of sufficient statistics, as for every exponential family (@def-glm-edf), the E
step reduces to filling in those statistics (@exr-mis-em-exponential-family) — which
is the same as filling in the missing values only when the statistics are linear in
them. A normal variance, whose statistic contains \( \sum_iy_i^2 \), is the standing
counterexample, and only the second reading is EM.

## Monotonicity

::: {#thm-mis-em}
[The EM sequence never decreases the likelihood]

Let \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)} \) satisfy
\( Q(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})
\ge Q(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}) \). Then

::: {.enumerate options="label=(\alph*)"}
1. \( \ell(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)})\ge\ell(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}) \),
   with equality only if
   \( Q(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})
   =Q(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}) \) and the two
   conditional densities of \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}} \) agree almost everywhere;

2. if \( \ell \) is bounded above, \( \ell(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}) \) converges;

3. if \( Q(\cdot\mid\cdot) \) is differentiable in its first argument and
   \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)} \) is an interior maximizer, then at any fixed
   point \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*} \) of the iteration the observed-data score
   vanishes:
   \( \partial\ell(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})/\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
   \big|_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*}}=\bzero \).
:::

:::

::: {.proof}
Write \( g(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
=f(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}) \)
for the conditional density of the missing part. Since
\( f(\mathbf{D}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
=f(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\,
g(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}) \), taking logarithms and
rearranging gives, for every \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}} \),
\[
\ell(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\ell_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
-\log g(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}).
\]
The left side does not depend on \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}} \), so taking the
expectation of both sides under \( g(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}) \) leaves
it unchanged and yields the decomposition
\[
\begin{gathered}
\ell(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=Q(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})
-H(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}),\quad\text{where}\\
H(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})
=\E\bigl\{\log g(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\ \big|\
\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}\bigr\}.
\end{gathered}
\]{#eq-mis-em-decomposition}

(a) Subtract @eq-mis-em-decomposition at \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)} \) from the
same at \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)} \):
\[
\begin{aligned}
\ell(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)})-\ell(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})
&=\bigl\{Q(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})
-Q(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})\bigr\}\\
&\qquad-\bigl\{H(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})
-H(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})\bigr\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The first brace is nonnegative by hypothesis. For the second, write
\( g_s=g(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}) \) and
\( g_{s+1}=g(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)}) \); then
\[
\begin{aligned}
H(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s+1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})
-H(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)})
&=\int g_s\log\frac{g_{s+1}}{g_s}\\
&\le\log\int g_s\frac{g_{s+1}}{g_s}=\log 1=0 ,
\end{aligned}
\]
by Jensen's inequality applied to the concave logarithm. So the difference of
log-likelihoods is a nonnegative number minus a nonpositive one. Equality throughout
forces equality in Jensen's inequality, which for the strictly concave logarithm
requires \( g_{s+1}/g_s \) to be constant almost everywhere under \( g_s \), hence
\( g_{s+1}=g_s \), and forces the first brace to vanish.

(b) A nondecreasing sequence bounded above converges.

(c) At a fixed point, \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*} \) maximizes
\( Q(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*}) \), so
\( \partial Q(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})/\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta} \)
vanishes at \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}=\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*} \). The function
\( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}\mapsto H(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*}) \)
is maximized at \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}=\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*} \) by the Jensen
argument just given, so its derivative vanishes there too. Differentiating
@eq-mis-em-decomposition at \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*} \) gives
\( \partial\ell/\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}=\bzero-\bzero \).
:::

::: {.warning}
Part (c) says fixed points are stationary points, not maxima: EM can converge to a
saddle point or a local maximum, and with several modes the limit depends on the
start. Wu (1983) gives conditions under which the limit points are stationary and
under which the sequence converges; none rules out a local maximum. Start from
several values.
:::

## Missing responses in a linear model

The oldest case is the one where every step is explicit, and it settles a question
left open in
[Section 18.6](../ch18-covariance-and-design/06-missing-observations.html).

::: {#prp-mis-em-responses}
[EM for a normal linear model with missing responses]

Let \( \y=\X\bbeta+\be \) with \( \be\sim\Normal_n(\bzero,\sigma^2\I) \) and
\( \rank(\X)=p \); let the responses of a set of \( m \) rows be unrecorded, the
covariates of those rows being known, with an ignorable mechanism, and suppose the
observed rows \( \X_o \) have rank \( p \).
Write \( \mathrm{SSE}_o \) for the residual sum of squares of the least squares fit
on the observed rows. Then:

::: {.enumerate options="label=(\alph*)"}
1. the E step replaces each missing \( y_i \) by
   \( \x_{(i)}\T\bbeta^{(s)} \) in the cross-product terms of
   \( \ell_{\mathrm{c}} \), and each \( y_i^2 \) by
   \( (\x_{(i)}\T\bbeta^{(s)})^2+\sigma^{2(s)} \);

2. the M step is
   \( \bbeta^{(s+1)}=(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T\y^{(s)} \) with \( \y^{(s)} \) the completed
   vector, and
   \( \sigma^{2(s+1)}=n^{-1}\{\norm{\y^{(s)}-\X\bbeta^{(s+1)}}^{2}+m\sigma^{2(s)}\} \);

3. the unique fixed point is
   \( \bbeta^{*}=\hbeta_o \), the least squares estimate from the observed rows, and
   \[
   \sigma^{2*}=\frac{\mathrm{SSE}_o}{n-m},
   \]{#eq-mis-em-sigma}

   which is the observed-data maximum likelihood estimate, based on \( n-m \)
   observations.
:::

:::

::: {.proof}
(a) The complete-data log-likelihood
\( -\tfrac12 n\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\tfrac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-\x_{(i)}\T\bbeta)^2 \)
is quadratic in the \( y_i \), so its conditional expectation needs their first two
conditional moments. Given the observed data an unrecorded \( y_i \) is a draw from
its own model, so those are \( \x_{(i)}\T\bbeta^{(s)} \) and
\( (\x_{(i)}\T\bbeta^{(s)})^2+\sigma^{2(s)} \).

(b) With those substitutions,
\[
Q=-\tfrac12 n\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac1{2\sigma^2}
\Bigl\{\norm{\y^{(s)}-\X\bbeta}^2+m\sigma^{2(s)}\Bigr\},
\]
because each missing row contributes
\( \E(y_i^2)-2\x_{(i)}\T\bbeta\,\E(y_i)+(\x_{(i)}\T\bbeta)^2
=(\x_{(i)}\T\bbeta^{(s)}-\x_{(i)}\T\bbeta)^2+\sigma^{2(s)} \). Maximizing in
\( \bbeta \) is ordinary least squares on \( \y^{(s)} \); maximizing in
\( \sigma^2 \) gives the stated average.

(c) At a fixed point the completed vector has
\( y_i=\x_{(i)}\T\bbeta^{*} \) at the missing rows, so those rows have zero residual
and contribute nothing to the least squares criterion; hence \( \bbeta^{*} \)
minimizes \( \norm{\y_o-\X_o\bb}^2 \) and equals \( \hbeta_o \), which is
@thm-dsn-missing(a). Its residual sum of squares is \( \mathrm{SSE}_o \), so the
variance equation reads \( n\sigma^{2*}=\mathrm{SSE}_o+m\sigma^{2*} \),
giving @eq-mis-em-sigma. Uniqueness of \( \bbeta^{*} \) follows from @thm-dsn-missing(b),
and @eq-mis-em-sigma then determines \( \sigma^{2*} \).
:::

The term \( m\sigma^{2(s)} \) is the whole point. Without it — fill the gaps with
fitted values and hand the completed data to a regression routine — the variance comes
out as \( \mathrm{SSE}_o/n \). That is the error @thm-dsn-missing(e) warns against,
now derived rather than asserted: *the filled-in values are not data, and the
likelihood knows it.*

::: {#exm-mis-co2-em}
[The Mauna Loa gaps]

Fit the trend-plus-harmonics model of
[Section 5.3](../ch05-model-and-least-squares/03-multiple-regression-model.html) to all
\( 2284 \) weeks, with the \( 59 \) missing
ones handled by EM. The coefficients agree with the complete-case fit to machine
precision, as @prp-mis-em-responses(c) requires, and the error standard deviation is
\( 0.7992 \) ppm; filling in the gaps and treating them as data
gives \( 0.7889 \) ppm, too small by the factor
\( \sqrt{n/(n-m)}=1.0132 \). The discrepancy is small here only
because the gaps are few.
:::

```{.python .run #cell-em-responses}
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

co2 = sm.datasets.co2.load_pandas().data["co2"]
t = (co2.index.year + (co2.index.dayofyear - 0.5) / 365.25).to_numpy()
tc = t - 1980.0
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(t)), tc, tc**2,
                     np.cos(2 * np.pi * t), np.sin(2 * np.pi * t),
                     np.cos(4 * np.pi * t), np.sin(4 * np.pi * t)])
y = co2.to_numpy()
obs = ~np.isnan(y)
n, m, p = len(y), int((~obs).sum()), X.shape[1]

beta, sigma2 = np.zeros(p), 1.0                       # any starting value will do
for sweep in range(60):
    filled = np.where(obs, y, X @ beta)               # E step: gaps get their fitted values
    beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, filled, rcond=None)  # M step for the coefficients
    sse = float(np.sum((filled - X @ beta) ** 2))
    sigma2 = (sse + m * sigma2) / n                   # M step for the variance: add m*sigma^2
print(f"EM after {sweep + 1} sweeps: sigma-hat = {np.sqrt(sigma2):.4f} ppm")

beta_cc, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X[obs], y[obs], rcond=None)
sse_o = float(np.sum((y[obs] - X[obs] @ beta_cc) ** 2))
print(f"observed-data MLE:          {np.sqrt(sse_o / (n - m)):.4f} ppm")
print(f"treating the fills as data: {np.sqrt(sse_o / n):.4f} ppm")
```

## A missing covariate

When a covariate is missing, the conditional distribution the E step needs runs
*backwards*: what does a recorded response say about an unrecorded covariate? A model
for the covariate is now unavoidable.

::: {#prp-mis-em-covariate}
[EM with a missing covariate]

Let \( x_i\sim\Normal(\mu,\tau^2) \) independently, and
\( y_i\mid x_i\sim\Normal(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i,\sigma^2) \), with
\( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}=(\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2,\mu,\tau^2) \). Suppose
\( y_i \) is always recorded and \( x_i \) is missing for a subset, with an
ignorable mechanism. Then for a record with \( x_i \) missing the E step uses
\[
\E(x_i\mid y_i)=\mu+\frac{\beta_1\tau^2}{\beta_1^2\tau^2+\sigma^2}
 \bigl(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1\mu\bigr),\qquad
\Var(x_i\mid y_i)=\tau^2-\frac{\beta_1^2\tau^4}{\beta_1^2\tau^2+\sigma^2},
\]{#eq-mis-em-estep}

both evaluated at \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)} \), and the M step is the
complete-data maximum likelihood fit with the sufficient statistics
\( \sum_ix_i \), \( \sum_ix_i^2 \) and \( \sum_ix_iy_i \) replaced by
\( \sum_i\E(x_i\mid y_i) \), \( \sum_i\E(x_i^2\mid y_i) \) and
\( \sum_iy_i\E(x_i\mid y_i) \).
:::

::: {.proof}
The pair \( (x_i,y_i) \) is bivariate normal, with \( \E y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1\mu \),
\( \Var y_i=\beta_1^2\tau^2+\sigma^2 \) and
\( \Cov(x_i,y_i)=\beta_1\tau^2 \). The conditional moments @eq-mis-em-estep are the
standard normal conditional mean and variance (@thm-mvn-conditional). The
complete-data log-likelihood is
\[
-\tfrac{n}2\log\sigma^2-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}
-\tfrac{n}2\log\tau^2-\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\tau^2}+\text{const},
\]
which is linear in the three listed statistics together with \( \sum_iy_i \) and
\( \sum_iy_i^2 \); taking conditional expectations therefore replaces exactly those
statistics, and the maximizer is the complete-data one evaluated at them.
:::

Note what the E step does *not* do: it does not impute \( x_i \) by \( \mu \), nor
from other covariates alone. It uses the response. Imputing a covariate without the
response is among the commonest errors in practice, and it attenuates
\( \hat\beta_1 \) towards zero, for the reason
[Chapter 24](../ch24-errors-in-variables/index.html) gives.

::: {#exm-mis-em-covariate}
[An EM fit with a missing covariate]

Simulate \( N=400 \) records with \( \beta_1=0.8 \), and let the covariate be missing
more often when the response is large; this loses \( 169 \)
covariate values. The mechanism is MAR — it uses only the recorded \( y \) — so
likelihood is valid and the complete-case fit is not. EM gives
\( \hat\beta_1=0.825 \); the complete cases give
\( 0.620 \), attenuated by a quarter.

[Figure 41.4.1](04-em.html#fig-mis-em) shows the two faces of the algorithm. The
observed-data log-likelihood climbs monotonically, as @thm-mis-em(a) requires, and the
distance to the fixed point falls by the constant factor
\( 0.760 \) per sweep, matching to three decimals the largest
eigenvalue of \( \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{c}}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{m}} \) below.
Reaching eight decimal places takes \( 68 \) sweeps — slow by
the standards of Newton's method, which is the price of never differentiating an
integral.
:::

```{.python .run #cell-em-covariate}
rng = np.random.default_rng(4104)
N = 400
theta_true = dict(b0=1.0, b1=0.8, s2=1.0, mu=0.5, t2=1.0)
x = theta_true["mu"] + np.sqrt(theta_true["t2"]) * rng.normal(size=N)
y2 = theta_true["b0"] + theta_true["b1"] * x + np.sqrt(theta_true["s2"]) * rng.normal(size=N)
seen = rng.uniform(size=N) > 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(-0.4 + 2.0 * (y2 - y2.mean()))))
print(f"{N - seen.sum()} of {N} covariates missing, more often when y is large")


def estep(th, y, seen):
    """Conditional mean and second moment of the covariate given the response."""
    b0, b1, s2, mu, t2 = th
    vy = b1**2 * t2 + s2                              # marginal variance of y
    cond_mean = mu + (b1 * t2 / vy) * (y - b0 - b1 * mu)
    cond_var = t2 - (b1 * t2) ** 2 / vy
    ex = np.where(seen, x, cond_mean)
    ex2 = np.where(seen, x**2, cond_mean**2 + cond_var)
    return ex, ex2


def mstep(ex, ex2, y):
    mu, n_ = ex.mean(), len(y)
    t2 = ex2.mean() - mu**2
    sxx = ex2.sum() - n_ * mu**2
    sxy = (ex * y).sum() - n_ * mu * y.mean()
    b1 = sxy / sxx
    b0 = y.mean() - b1 * mu
    s2 = ((y**2).sum() - 2 * b0 * y.sum() - 2 * b1 * (ex * y).sum()
          + n_ * b0**2 + 2 * b0 * b1 * ex.sum() + b1**2 * ex2.sum()) / n_
    return np.array([b0, b1, s2, mu, t2])


def loglik(th, y, seen):
    """Observed-data log-likelihood: joint for complete rows, marginal for the rest."""
    b0, b1, s2, mu, t2 = th
    ll = -0.5 * np.sum(np.log(2 * np.pi * s2) + (y[seen] - b0 - b1 * x[seen]) ** 2 / s2)
    ll += -0.5 * np.sum(np.log(2 * np.pi * t2) + (x[seen] - mu) ** 2 / t2)
    vy = b1**2 * t2 + s2
    ll += -0.5 * np.sum(np.log(2 * np.pi * vy) + (y[~seen] - b0 - b1 * mu) ** 2 / vy)
    return ll


th = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0, 1.0])
path, iterates = [loglik(th, y2, seen)], [th]
for sweep in range(200):
    ex, ex2 = estep(th, y2, seen)
    th = mstep(ex, ex2, y2)
    path.append(loglik(th, y2, seen))
    iterates.append(th)
print("EM   b0, b1 =", np.round(th[:2], 4))

cc = np.polyfit(x[seen], y2[seen], 1)[::-1]           # complete cases only
print("complete cases:", np.round(cc, 4))
```

## Rate of convergence, and the standard errors EM does not give

Both the speed of EM and its principal defect come from the same matrix. Define
the **complete-data information**
\( \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{c}}=-\partial^2Q(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})/
\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\,\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\T \) at
\( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}=\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*} \), the **observed information**
\( \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{o}}=-\partial^2\ell/\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\,\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\T \)
there, and their difference
\( \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{m}}=\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{c}}-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{o}} \),
the **missing information**.

::: {#prp-mis-louis}
[The missing-information principle]

At any \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta} \), with \( \bS_{\mathrm{c}} \) the complete-data
score,
\[
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
=\E\bigl(\bS_{\mathrm{c}}\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\bigr),
\qquad
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{o}}
=\E\bigl(-\partial\bS_{\mathrm{c}}/\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\T
 \mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\bigr)
-\Var\bigl(\bS_{\mathrm{c}}\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\bigr).
\]{#eq-mis-louis}

Consequently \( \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{m}}
=\Var(\bS_{\mathrm{c}}\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}) \) is
nonnegative definite: the observed information is never larger than the expected
complete-data information.
:::

::: {.proof}
Differentiate the identity
\( \ell(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\ell_{\mathrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
-\log g(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}) \) from the proof of
@thm-mis-em and take the conditional expectation under
\( g(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\uptheta}) \). The conditional score of \( g \) has mean
zero, being the score of a density in its own parameter, so the first identity
follows. Differentiating again,
\[
-\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\T}
=-\frac{\partial^2\ell_{\mathrm{c}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\T}
+\frac{\partial^2\log g}{\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\T},
\]
and taking the conditional expectation turns the last term into minus the
information of \( g \), which by the usual information identity equals minus the
variance of its score. Since the score of \( g \) is
\( \bS_{\mathrm{c}}-\partial\ell/\partial\boldsymbol{\uptheta} \) and the second
term is a constant given the observed data, that variance is
\( \Var(\bS_{\mathrm{c}}\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}) \).
:::

Identity @eq-mis-louis is Louis's formula (Louis 1982); "observed = complete minus
missing" is the missing-information principle of Orchard and Woodbury (1972). Two
consequences:

**The rate.** Near a fixed point the EM map is approximately linear with Jacobian
\( \I-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{c}}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{o}}
=\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{c}}^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{m}} \), so the iterates converge
linearly at its largest eigenvalue — the largest **fraction of missing information**
(Dempster, Laird and Rubin, 1977). In @exm-mis-em-covariate that eigenvalue is
\( 0.760 \) and the measured rate is \( 0.760 \);
the log-likelihood gap, quadratic near the maximum, falls at its square,
\( 0.577 \) per sweep. In @thm-dsn-missing(c) the same
matrix is \( \M_{mm} \), the submatrix of the hat matrix belonging to the lost
plots.

**The standard errors.** The matrix EM manipulates is \( \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{c}} \),
which is *too large*, so inverting it understates the variance by the fraction of
missing information. Three honest routes: @eq-mis-louis; numerical differentiation of
\( \ell \), as this section's script does; or the bootstrap, resampling records and
rerunning EM (@def-bs-bootstrap(b)). In
@exm-mis-em-covariate these give \( 0.0611 \) and
\( 0.0605 \) (bootstrap, \( 300 \)
resamples), against \( 0.0512 \) from the complete-data
information — an understatement of \( 16\% \).

::: {.warning}
The variance of an EM estimate is never the inverse of the matrix the M step inverts.
If a routine reports standard errors after EM without saying how it got them, assume
it has made this mistake.
:::

[B1]

In @prp-mis-em-responses, suppose one carries out the iteration but *omits* the
term \( m\sigma^{2(s)} \) from the M step for the variance. Show that the

::: {.solution}
The coefficient update does not involve \( \sigma^2 \) at all, so
\( \bbeta^{(s)} \) follows the same sequence and converges to \( \hbeta_o \); at
that point the completed residual sum of squares is \( \mathrm{SSE}_o \). The
truncated variance update is \( \sigma^{2(s+1)}=\mathrm{SSE}_o/n \) once the
coefficients have settled. That is the complete-data formula applied to \( n \)
"observations", \( m \) of which have been manufactured to fit exactly; reducing
the divisor by \( m \) is the degrees-of-freedom correction of @thm-dsn-missing(e).
:::

::: {#exr-mis-em-exponential-family}
[B2]

Let the complete-data model be an exponential dispersion family (@def-glm-edf) with
sufficient statistic \( \mathbf{t}(\mathbf{D}) \). Show that the E step reduces to
computing \( \E\{\mathbf{t}(\mathbf{D})\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{(s)}\} \)
and the M step to solving the complete-data likelihood equations with
\( \mathbf{t} \) replaced by that expectation. Why does the normal variance in
@prp-mis-em-responses still require the extra term \( m\sigma^{2(s)} \)?
:::

::: {.solution}
The complete-data log-likelihood is
\( \boldsymbol{\uptheta}\T\mathbf{t}(\mathbf{D})-A(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}) \) plus a
term free of \( \boldsymbol{\uptheta} \), so it is affine in \( \mathbf{t} \) and
its conditional expectation replaces \( \mathbf{t} \) by
\( \E(\mathbf{t}\mid\cdot) \). Setting the derivative to zero gives
\( \E_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}\mathbf{t}=\E(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}) \).
The normal case is no exception: the sufficient statistics include
\( \sum_iy_i^2 \), and its conditional expectation over a missing row is
\( (\x_{(i)}\T\bbeta^{(s)})^2+\sigma^{2(s)} \), which is where the extra term comes
from. The trap is to substitute the conditional *mean of the data* rather than the
conditional mean of the *statistic*.
:::

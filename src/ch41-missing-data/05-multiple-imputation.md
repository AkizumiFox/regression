# Multiple imputation

Filling a hole with a plausible value is not wrong; filling it with *one* plausible
value and then forgetting that it was a guess is. Multiple imputation keeps the
convenience of a complete rectangular data set and restores the uncertainty a single
filling destroys, by filling several times and letting the variation between fillings
speak.

## The idea

Rubin's proposal (1978, 1987) has three parts.

1. **Impute.** Draw \( M \) independent completions
   \( \mathbf{D}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{D}^{(M)} \), each replacing
   \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}} \) by a draw from its posterior predictive
   distribution given \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}} \) under an **imputation model**.
2. **Analyse.** Apply the intended complete-data analysis to each completion, getting
   estimates \( \hat Q_1,\dots,\hat Q_M \) and complete-data variance estimates
   \( U_1,\dots,U_M \).
3. **Combine.** Pool them by @thm-mis-rubin.

Step 1 is the only novelty. Drawing from the posterior predictive distribution means
drawing the imputation model's *parameters* as well as the values; an imputation that
conditions on a point estimate treats it as known, which is one of the ways the
procedure fails.

::: {#def-mis-multiple-imputation}
[Multiple imputation]

Let \( \boldsymbol{\upgamma} \) parameterize a model for the conditional
distribution of \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}} \) given
\( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}} \), with prior \( p(\boldsymbol{\upgamma}) \). An
imputation is **proper** in Rubin's sense if the \( M \) completions are
independent draws from
\[
f(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
=\int f(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{mis}}\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}},\boldsymbol{\upgamma})\,
 p(\boldsymbol{\upgamma}\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})\,d\boldsymbol{\upgamma},
\]{#eq-mis-posterior-predictive}

the posterior predictive distribution. Three standard constructions:

::: {.enumerate options="label=(\alph*)"}
1. **Regression imputation with a parameter draw.** For a continuous variable
   modelled as normal given the others, draw
   \( \sigma^{2*}=\mathrm{SSE}/\chi^2_{k-q} \) from its posterior under a flat
   prior, then \( \boldsymbol{\upgamma}^{*}\sim\Normal(\hat{\boldsymbol{\upgamma}},
   \sigma^{2*}(\Z_o\T\Z_o)^{-1}) \), then impute
   \( \mathbf{z}\T\boldsymbol{\upgamma}^{*}+\sigma^{*}\varepsilon \) with
   \( \varepsilon \) standard normal. This is @thm-opt-bayes-conjugate with the
   noninformative limit @cor-opt-flat-prior.

2. **Predictive mean matching.** Compute the predicted value for the record with the
   gap, find the \( d \) recorded records with the closest predicted values, and
   impute one of their *observed* values at random. Every imputed value then actually
   occurred, so the marginal distribution is respected even when the normal model is
   wrong, at the cost of a less transparent theory.

3. **Chained equations.** With gaps in several variables, cycle through them,
   imputing each in turn from a regression on the current versions of all the
   others, for several sweeps, and keep the completion at the end of a sweep.
:::

:::

Construction (c), also called fully conditional specification or MICE
(van Buuren, 2018), is what makes multiple imputation usable on real files, where
dozens of variables have gaps and no one will write a joint density for all of them.
It is a Gibbs-like sweep (compare @exm-mix-gibbs) carrying a risk the Gibbs sampler
does not: the specified conditionals need not correspond to *any* joint distribution,
and then the sweep has no stationary distribution and the result depends on the order
of the variables. The dependence is usually slight, and the flexibility is worth the
untidiness, but it should be known.

## Rubin's rules

::: {#thm-mis-rubin}
[Combining M analyses]

Let \( Q \) be the estimand and suppose the complete-data analysis is calibrated in
the sense that, given the complete data \( \mathbf{D} \), it reports
\( \hat Q(\mathbf{D}) \) and \( U(\mathbf{D}) \) with
\[
\E(Q\mid\mathbf{D})=\hat Q(\mathbf{D}),\qquad \Var(Q\mid\mathbf{D})=U(\mathbf{D}).
\]{#eq-mis-complete-calibration}

Let \( \mathbf{D}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{D}^{(M)} \) be independent draws from
@eq-mis-posterior-predictive, generated by the analyst and therefore conditionally
independent of \( Q \) given \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}} \); put
\( \hat Q_\ell=\hat Q(\mathbf{D}^{(\ell)}) \) and
\( U_\ell=U(\mathbf{D}^{(\ell)}) \), and define
\[
\bar Q=\frac1M\sum_{\ell}\hat Q_\ell,\qquad
\bar U=\frac1M\sum_{\ell}U_\ell,\qquad
B=\frac1{M-1}\sum_{\ell}(\hat Q_\ell-\bar Q)^2 .
\]{#eq-mis-rubin-pieces}

Then, with all expectations conditional on \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}} \):

::: {.enumerate options="label=(\alph*)"}
1. *(Decomposition.)*
   \( \Var(Q\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})=\E(U\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
   +\Var(\hat Q\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}) \), and \( \bar U \) and \( B \) are
   unbiased for the two terms;

2. *(Total variance.)* \( \bar Q \) is unbiased for
   \( \E(Q\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}) \), and
   \[
   T=\bar U+\Bigl(1+\frac1M\Bigr)B
   \]{#eq-mis-rubin-total}

   is unbiased for the variance of \( Q-\bar Q \) given
   \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}} \);

3. *(Degrees of freedom.)* treating \( \bar U \) as fixed and
   \( (M-1)B/\Var(\hat Q\mid\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}) \) as
   \( \chi^2_{M-1} \), the variance of \( T \) matches that of a scaled
   \( \chi^2_{\nu} \) variable with
   \[
   \nu=(M-1)\Bigl\{\frac{T}{(1+M^{-1})B}\Bigr\}^{2}=\frac{M-1}{\gamma^{2}},
   \qquad \gamma=\frac{(1+M^{-1})B}{T},
   \]{#eq-mis-rubin-df}

   and the interval \( \bar Q\pm t_{\nu,1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{T} \) is the standard
   multiple-imputation interval; \( \gamma \) is the estimated **fraction of
   missing information**;

4. *(Efficiency.)* write \( T_\infty=\E(U)+\Var(\hat Q) \) for the total variance
   at \( M=\infty \) and \( \gamma_\infty=\Var(\hat Q)/T_\infty \) for the limiting
   fraction of missing information. Then the target of \( T \) exceeds
   \( T_\infty \) by the factor \( 1+\gamma_\infty/M \), so using \( M \)
   imputations rather than infinitely many inflates the standard error by
   \( (1+\gamma_\infty/M)^{1/2} \).
:::

:::

::: {.proof}
Throughout, condition on \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}} \) and write \( \E \) and
\( \Var \) for the conditional operators.

(a) The conditional variance decomposition applied to \( Q \) with the
intermediate conditioning variable \( \mathbf{D} \) gives
\( \Var(Q)=\E\{\Var(Q\mid\mathbf{D})\}+\Var\{\E(Q\mid\mathbf{D})\} \), which is
\( \E(U)+\Var(\hat Q) \) by @eq-mis-complete-calibration. The completions are
draws of \( \mathbf{D} \) from its conditional law, so \( \E\bar U=\E(U) \), and
the sample variance of independent draws is unbiased for their variance, so
\( \E B=\Var(\hat Q) \).

(b) \( \E\bar Q=\E\hat Q=\E\{\E(Q\mid\mathbf{D})\}=\E Q \), so \( \bar Q \) is
unbiased for the posterior mean. Because the completions are conditionally
independent of \( Q \) given \( \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{obs}} \), so is \( \bar Q \),
and therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Var(Q-\bar Q)&=\Var(Q)+\Var(\bar Q)
=\bigl\{\E(U)+\Var(\hat Q)\bigr\}+\frac{\Var(\hat Q)}{M}\\
&=\E(U)+\Bigl(1+\frac1M\Bigr)\Var(\hat Q),
\end{aligned}
\]
using (a) for the first bracket and independence of the \( M \) draws for the
second term. By (a) again, \( \bar U \) and \( (1+M^{-1})B \) are unbiased for the
two pieces, so \( T \) is unbiased for the whole.

(c) Under the stated approximation \( T=\bar U+(1+M^{-1})B \) has variance
\( (1+M^{-1})^2\Var(B) \), which is
\( 2(1+M^{-1})^2\{\Var(\hat Q)\}^2/(M-1) \). A scaled
\( \chi^2_\nu \) variable with mean \( T \) has variance \( 2T^2/\nu \). Equating
and replacing \( \Var(\hat Q) \) by its estimate \( B \),
\[
\frac{2T^{2}}{\nu}=\frac{2(1+M^{-1})^{2}B^{2}}{M-1}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\nu=(M-1)\frac{T^{2}}{(1+M^{-1})^{2}B^{2}},
\]
which is @eq-mis-rubin-df.

(d) By (b) the target of \( T \) is
\[
\E(U)+(1+M^{-1})\Var(\hat Q)=T_\infty+\frac{\Var(\hat Q)}{M}
=T_\infty\Bigl(1+\frac{\gamma_\infty}{M}\Bigr).
\]

Taking square roots gives the inflation factor.
:::

::: {.remark}
[What the proof assumes]

The calibration @eq-mis-complete-calibration is a Bayesian statement: the
complete-data analysis behaves as a posterior mean and variance would. Rubin's rules
are therefore exactly right when imputation and analysis come from the *same* joint
model, and approximately right otherwise. The degrees of freedom in part (c) can
exceed the complete-data degrees of freedom, absurdly, when \( M \) is large and the
sample small; Barnard and Rubin (1999) give the corrected formula.
:::

::: {.idea}
The between-imputation variance \( B \) is the price of not knowing the missing
values, and \( \bar U \) is the price of having a finite sample. Adding them is the
whole of multiple imputation. The factor \( 1+1/M \) is the price of using finitely
many imputations to measure \( B \).
:::

## How many imputations

Part (d) is why the classical advice was "three to five imputations". With
\( \gamma_\infty=0.3 \) and \( M=5 \) the standard error is inflated by
\( 1.0296 \), three per cent; with
\( \gamma_\infty=0.5 \) the inflation is \( 1.0488 \)
at \( M=5 \) and \( 1.0124 \) at \( M=20 \).

The advice has shifted upward since, for two reasons the theorem does not address.
The degrees of freedom @eq-mis-rubin-df grow like \( M-1 \), so a small \( M \)
gives a fat-tailed reference distribution and wide intervals even when the point
estimate is fine; and \( \bar Q \) is itself a Monte Carlo average, so a second
analyst with a different seed gets a different number — uncomfortable when that
number is a \( p \)-value near a threshold. Imputations are cheap, and the curve in
[Figure 41.5.1](05-multiple-imputation.html#fig-mis-mi)(b) is flat past \( M=20 \)
for every plausible \( \gamma \).

## Coverage in practice

[Five analyses of one problem]

Take \( n=200 \) records with two correlated covariates and
\( y=1+0.5x_1+0.6x_2+\varepsilon \), and lose \( x_2 \) with a probability depending
on \( x_1 \) and \( y \) — both recorded, so MAR — leaving about
\( 106 \) complete records. Over
\( 2000 \) replicates, for the coefficient of \( x_2 \):

| analysis | bias | mean se | sd | coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| complete cases | \( -0.151 \) | \( 0.101 \) | \( 0.102 \) | \( 67.2\% \) |
| mean imputation | \( -0.319 \) | \( 0.125 \) | \( 0.108 \) | \( 25.6\% \) |
| single imputation | \( -0.010 \) | \( 0.082 \) | \( 0.138 \) | \( 75.9\% \) |
| multiple imputation, \( M=5 \) | \( -0.013 \) | \( 0.118 \) | \( 0.119 \) | \( 95.0\% \) |
| multiple imputation, \( M=20 \) | \( -0.013 \) | \( 0.115 \) | \( 0.114 \) | \( 94.5\% \) |

Each row fails in its own way. The complete cases are biased, the missingness
depending on the response. Mean imputation is far worse, and its reported standard
error is *larger* than its actual spread. A single proper imputation removes the bias
but
reports a standard error three fifths of the truth, and covers
\( 75.9\% \) of the time. Multiple imputation with five draws
gets both right, with an average estimated fraction of missing information
\( 0.463 \) and median degrees of freedom
\( 18.3 \).
:::

```{.python .run #cell-imputation-impute}
import numpy as np

n = 200
rho = 0.5
beta_true = np.array([1.0, 0.5, 0.6])                 # intercept, x1, x2


def make_data(rng):
    x1 = rng.normal(size=n)
    x2 = rho * x1 + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * rng.normal(size=n)
    y = beta_true[0] + beta_true[1] * x1 + beta_true[2] * x2 + rng.normal(size=n)
    eta = -0.3 + 0.8 * x1 + 2.0 * (y - y.mean())      # depends on observed values only: MAR
    seen = rng.uniform(size=n) > 1 / (1 + np.exp(-eta))
    return x1, x2, y, seen


def fit(X, y):
    """Least squares with the coefficient of x2 and its squared standard error."""
    b, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
    dof = len(y) - X.shape[1]
    s2 = np.sum((y - X @ b) ** 2) / dof
    v = s2 * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)[2, 2]
    return b[2], v, dof


def impute_once(x1, x2, y, seen, rng):
    """One draw from the posterior predictive distribution of the missing x2."""
    Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1, y])          # imputation model: x2 on x1 and y
    Zo, k = Z[seen], int(seen.sum())
    g, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Zo, x2[seen], rcond=None)
    sse = float(np.sum((x2[seen] - Zo @ g) ** 2))
    s2_star = sse / rng.chisquare(k - Z.shape[1])     # draw the variance ...
    cov = s2_star * np.linalg.inv(Zo.T @ Zo)
    g_star = rng.multivariate_normal(g, cov)          # ... then the coefficients
    draw = Z @ g_star + np.sqrt(s2_star) * rng.normal(size=n)
    return np.where(seen, x2, draw)                   # observed values are kept


def rubin(estimates, variances):
    """Combine M analyses: the average, the total variance and the degrees of freedom."""
    M = len(estimates)
    q_bar, u_bar = np.mean(estimates), np.mean(variances)
    b = np.var(estimates, ddof=1)
    total = u_bar + (1 + 1 / M) * b
    gamma = (1 + 1 / M) * b / total                   # fraction of missing information
    nu = (M - 1) / gamma**2
    return q_bar, total, nu


demo_rng = np.random.default_rng(4105)
x1d, x2d, yd, seend = make_data(demo_rng)             # one data set, x2 missing at random
qs, us = [], []
for _ in range(5):                                    # M = 5 completions of it
    xi = impute_once(x1d, x2d, yd, seend, demo_rng)
    qi, ui, _ = fit(np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1d, xi]), yd)
    qs.append(qi)
    us.append(ui)
q_bar, total, nu = rubin(qs, us)
print(f"M = 5: estimate {q_bar:.3f}, total variance {total:.4f}, "
      f"degrees of freedom {nu:.1f}")
```

Chained equations handle gaps in more than one variable: ten sweeps of the two
conditional regressions below recover the coefficient of \( x_2 \) when both
covariates are incomplete.

```{.python .run #cell-imputation-chained}
rho = 0.5
beta_true = np.array([1.0, 0.5, 0.6])                 # intercept, x1, x2


def make_data(rng):
    x1 = rng.normal(size=n)
    x2 = rho * x1 + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * rng.normal(size=n)
    y = beta_true[0] + beta_true[1] * x1 + beta_true[2] * x2 + rng.normal(size=n)
    eta = -0.3 + 0.8 * x1 + 2.0 * (y - y.mean())      # depends on observed values only: MAR
    seen = rng.uniform(size=n) > 1 / (1 + np.exp(-eta))
    return x1, x2, y, seen


rng2 = np.random.default_rng(4107)
x1, x2, y, seen2 = make_data(rng2)
seen1 = rng2.uniform(size=n) > 0.25                   # x1 now has gaps of its own
x1_work = np.where(seen1, x1, x1[seen1].mean())       # a crude start
x2_work = np.where(seen2, x2, x2[seen2].mean())
for sweep in range(10):                               # one sweep = one pass over the variables
    Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x2_work, y])     # x1 given the others
    g, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Z[seen1], x1[seen1], rcond=None)
    s = np.std(x1[seen1] - Z[seen1] @ g, ddof=3)
    x1_work = np.where(seen1, x1, Z @ g + s * rng2.normal(size=n))
    Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1_work, y])     # x2 given the others
    g, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Z[seen2], x2[seen2], rcond=None)
    s = np.std(x2[seen2] - Z[seen2] @ g, ddof=3)
    x2_work = np.where(seen2, x2, Z @ g + s * rng2.normal(size=n))
b_chained, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1_work, x2_work]), y, rcond=None)
print("after chained equations:", np.round(b_chained, 3))
```

## Congeniality

Multiple imputation splits the work between two models: the imputer's and the
analyst's. Nothing forces them to agree, and Meng (1994) showed that the
consequences depend on which way they disagree.

**The imputation model omits something the analysis uses.** If the analyst fits an
interaction and the imputer imputed one of its covariates from a model without it,
the imputed values carry no interaction and the analyst's interaction is biased
towards zero. This is the serious failure, and easy to commit: the imputer usually
works first, for a whole file, without knowing which analyses will be run.

**The imputation model includes something the analysis does not.** Then the
imputations are richer than the analysis needs; estimates are usually fine and
Rubin's \( T \) tends to be *too large*, so the procedure is conservative rather
than wrong.

Meng calls a pair of models **congenial** when both are implied by a single Bayesian
model, and shows that Rubin's variance estimator is consistent under congeniality and
can be biased either way without it. Two rules follow: put into the imputation model
every variable the analysis will use, including the response and every transformation
and interaction to be fitted; and when imputer and analyst are different people, err
towards a rich imputation model.

::: {.warning}
Omitting the response from the imputation model is the single commonest mistake.
It looks cautious — "I do not want to build the answer into the data" — and it is
exactly backwards: it imputes covariates as though they were unrelated to the
response, which biases every coefficient towards zero. The response belongs in the
imputation model.
:::

## Exercises

### A. Check your understanding

::: {#exr-mis-mi-single}
[A1]

Set \( M=1 \) in @thm-mis-rubin and explain why the formulas break down. Which of
the three quantities in @eq-mis-rubin-pieces cannot be computed, and what does that
say about single imputation?
:::

::: {.solution}
With \( M=1 \) the between-imputation sum of squares has no degrees of freedom:
\( B \) is \( 0/0 \). So the part of the uncertainty that comes from not knowing the
missing values cannot be estimated at all from one imputation, which is exactly why
a single imputation reports \( \bar U \) alone and understates the variance.
:::

::: {#exr-mis-mi-gamma}
[A2]

From @eq-mis-rubin-df, compute \( \nu \) for \( M=5 \) and
\( \gamma\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5\} \). At which value of \( \gamma \) does the
multiple-imputation reference distribution become so heavy-tailed that the interval
is more than \( 20\% \) wider than a normal-based one?
:::

::: {.solution}
Equation @eq-mis-rubin-df gives \( \nu=(M-1)/\gamma^2 \), so at \( M=5 \) the three
values are \( 4/0.01=400 \), \( 4/0.09=44.4 \) and \( 4/0.25=16 \). The interval is
wider than the normal-based one by the ratio of the \( t \) quantile to \( 1.96 \),
and a table shows that ratio passing \( 1.2 \) at about seven degrees of freedom,
that is at \( \gamma=\sqrt{4/7.2}\approx0.75 \). Only a problem in which three
quarters of the information is missing costs that much at \( M=5 \) — and raising
\( M \) is the cheap repair.
:::

# Why model more than the mean

A fitted regression is usually reported as a curve with a band around it. The curve
estimates \( \E(Y\mid\x) \); the band says how far individual responses fall from
it. In every model this book has built, the curve has been the object of study and
the band an afterthought, computed from one variance parameter, one variance function
or one sandwich correction. That division of labour is a modelling decision, and often
a bad one.

## What the mean model promises

Take the model of [Chapter 5](../ch05-model-and-least-squares/index.html) in its
strongest form: \( Y = \x\T\bbeta + \varepsilon \) with \( \varepsilon \) independent
of \( \x \). Write
\[
Q_Y(\tau\mid\x)=\inf\{q:\;P(Y\le q\mid\x)\ge\tau\}
\]
for the conditional quantile function. A homoscedastic model makes it a rigid object:
all its level curves are one curve, shifted.

::: {#lem-qnt-monotone}
[Quantiles follow monotone maps]

Let \( Y \) be a random variable and \( h \) a strictly increasing, left-continuous
function on the range of \( Y \). Then \( Q_{h(Y)}(\tau)=h\bigl(Q_Y(\tau)\bigr) \) for
every \( \tau\in(0,1) \).
:::

::: {.proof}
Write \( F \) for the distribution function of \( Y \) and \( q_0=Q_Y(\tau) \).
Strict monotonicity gives \( \{y:h(y)\le h(q_0)\}=\{y:y\le q_0\} \), so
\( P\{h(Y)\le h(q_0)\}=F(q_0)\ge\tau \) and \( h(q_0) \) is admissible in the
infimum defining \( Q_{h(Y)}(\tau) \). For the reverse inequality the infimum runs
over all real \( c \), not only over values of \( h \), and that is where
left-continuity is needed. Take \( c<h(q_0) \) and put
\( b=\sup\{y:h(y)\le c\} \); if that set is empty then \( P\{h(Y)\le c\}=0<\tau \)
at once. Otherwise \( h(b)=\lim_{y\uparrow b}h(y)\le c \) by left-continuity, so the
set is \( (-\infty,b] \) and \( P\{h(Y)\le c\}=F(b) \); and \( h(b)\le c<h(q_0) \)
forces \( b<q_0 \), whence \( F(b)<\tau \) by the definition of \( q_0 \). No
\( c<h(q_0) \) is admissible, so the two infima agree.
:::

::: {#prp-qnt-beyond-mean}
[What a mean model does and does not say]

Let \( \x \) be a covariate vector and \( \varepsilon \) a random variable with quantile
function \( q_{\varepsilon} \), independent of \( \x \).

::: {.enumerate options="label=(\alph*)"}
1. **Location model.** If \( Y=\x\T\bbeta+\varepsilon \), then
   \( Q_Y(\tau\mid\x)=\x\T\bbeta+q_{\varepsilon}(\tau) \). Every conditional quantile
   curve is the regression curve shifted by a constant, so the effect of a covariate
   on the \( \tau \)-quantile is \( \bbeta \), the same for every \( \tau \), and the
   conditional distribution has the same shape and the same spread everywhere.

2. **Location-scale model.** If \( Y=\x\T\bbeta+\exp(\x\T\boldsymbol{\upalpha})\varepsilon \),
   then
   \[
   Q_Y(\tau\mid\x)=\x\T\bbeta+\exp(\x\T\boldsymbol{\upalpha})\,q_{\varepsilon}(\tau),
   \]{#eq-qnt-locscale}

   so \( \partial Q_Y(\tau\mid\x)/\partial\x
   =\bbeta+q_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\exp(\x\T\boldsymbol{\upalpha})\boldsymbol{\upalpha} \) does depend on
   \( \tau \). The quantile curves are no longer parallel, but they are still
   generated by one curve \( q_{\varepsilon} \) stretched by one positive function.

3. **Beyond scale.** In (b) the standardized response
   \( \{Y-\x\T\bbeta\}\exp(-\x\T\boldsymbol{\upalpha}) \) has a distribution free of \( \x \).
   Any feature of the conditional law that is invariant under location and scale
   changes — skewness, kurtosis, the ratio of any two central moments of the same
   degree — is therefore constant in \( \x \). A model of the mean and the variance
   cannot represent a conditional distribution whose *shape* moves with \( \x \).
:::

:::

::: {.proof}
Parts (a) and (b) are @lem-qnt-monotone applied conditionally on \( \x \) to the
strictly increasing map \( h(e)=\x\T\bbeta+ce \) with \( c=1 \) and
\( c=\exp(\x\T\boldsymbol{\upalpha})>0 \); the derivative in (b) is immediate
from @eq-qnt-locscale. For (c), write \( Z=\{Y-\x\T\bbeta\}\exp(-\x\T\boldsymbol{\upalpha}) \).
By construction \( Z=\varepsilon \), whose law does not involve \( \x \), and any
location-scale invariant functional of the conditional law of \( Y \) equals the same
functional of the law of \( Z \).
:::

In plain words: quoting one residual standard deviation asserts that the poorest
household's food budget is as predictable as the richest one's, and that a covariate
moving the median moves the tenth and ninetieth percentiles by exactly the same
amount. Those are claims about the world, rarely examined and often false.

## Heteroscedasticity is a finding

[Chapter 21](../ch21-nonnormality-heteroscedasticity-serial/index.html) treated
non-constant variance as a problem to be managed: test for
it (@thm-het-breusch-pagan), fix the standard errors (@def-het-hc), or
reweight (@thm-het-wls). All three treat \( \Var(Y\mid\x) \) as a nuisance, yet the
variance function is often the more interesting half of the model. If the spread of
insurance claims grows faster than their mean, that is the fact the insurer is
pricing; if the variability of a child's height at a given age defines the reference
band, the band *is* the model.

::: {#exm-qnt-engel-bands}
[Engel's budgets, one more time]

Engel's 1857 survey of \( 235 \) Belgian working-class households records annual
income and annual food expenditure; the same data supported the diagnostics of
[Chapter 20](../ch20-residuals-leverage-influence/index.html) and the tests of
[Section 21.2](../ch21-nonnormality-heteroscedasticity-serial/02-detecting-heteroscedasticity.html).
Least squares gives a slope of \( 0.4852 \) and a single residual standard deviation
of \( 114.1 \) francs. Split the households into thirds by income: the residual
standard deviations within the thirds are \( 48.6 \), \( 74.8 \) and \( 171.6 \)
francs, a ratio of \( 3.53 \) from the poorest third to the richest.

The consequence for a prediction interval is immediate. The nominal \( 95\% \)
interval from the homoscedastic normal model contains \( 1.000 \) of the poorest
third, \( 1.000 \) of the middle third and \( 0.911 \) of the richest — far too wide
where the data are tight and too narrow where they are loose — while the overall
figure of \( 0.970 \) looks respectable. Averaging over the design hides the error
that matters to each household.

Shape moves too. The quartile coefficient of skewness,
\( (q_{0.75}+q_{0.25}-2q_{0.5})/(q_{0.75}-q_{0.25}) \), is \( -0.23 \) for the
residuals of the poorest fifth of households and \( 0.40 \) for the richest fifth,
and the interquartile range grows by a factor of \( 3.75 \) between them
([Figure 45.1.1](01-beyond-the-mean.html#fig-qnt-mean-not-enough)).
:::

```{.python .run #cell-beyond-mean-bands}
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats
data = sm.datasets.engel.load_pandas().data

income = data["income"].to_numpy()
food = data["foodexp"].to_numpy()
n = len(income)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), income])

beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, food, rcond=None)
resid = food - X @ beta
sigma = np.sqrt(resid @ resid / (n - 2))          # one number for every household

# a 95% prediction interval from the homoscedastic normal model
t = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 2)
lev = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", X, np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X), X)
half = t * sigma * np.sqrt(1 + lev)
inside = (food >= X @ beta - half) & (food <= X @ beta + half)

# split the households into income thirds and look at the spread in each
edges = np.quantile(income, [1 / 3, 2 / 3])
third = np.digitize(income, edges)
spread = [resid[third == k].std(ddof=1) for k in range(3)]
covered = [inside[third == k].mean() for k in range(3)]

print(f"residual sd by income third: {spread[0]:.1f}, {spread[1]:.1f}, {spread[2]:.1f}")
print(f"inside the 95% band:         {covered[0]:.3f}, {covered[1]:.3f}, {covered[2]:.3f}")
```

## Questions that need a distribution

Some questions cannot even be stated in terms of a conditional mean. *What share of
a poor household's income goes on food?* asks for a low quantile, because policy is
written about the vulnerable. *What is the tenth percentile of birth weight at this
gestational age?* asks for a paediatric reference chart, a grid of conditional
quantiles. *How much capital must be held against this portfolio?* asks for a value
at risk, a conditional quantile and not a multiple of a conditional standard
deviation unless the shape is known. *Has inequality in wages changed, or only the
average wage?* compares quantile curves at different levels, which is where quantile
regression entered economics.

## Three routes

The three sections that follow take three strategies, in increasing order of what
they assume: estimate each quantile separately by an asymmetrically weighted absolute
deviation; estimate each expectile separately by an asymmetrically weighted *squared*
deviation, buying a differentiable loss at the price of a fitted quantity that is
harder to describe; or model the whole conditional law by giving every parameter of a
parametric family its own structured additive predictor. A fourth route was taken
long ago — transform the response until a homoscedastic model fits, as
in @def-tr-box-cox — but one power must serve the whole design, and
[Section 22.5](../ch22-transformations/05-interpreting.html) recorded what that costs
when the answer is wanted on the original scale. Because the routes answer different
questions, they are compared in [Section 45.6](06-comparing.html) by which produces
the better predictive distribution.

## Exercises

### A. Check your understanding

::: {#exr-qnt-parallel}
[A1]

In a homoscedastic linear model with normal errors, by how much does the \( 0.9 \)
conditional quantile exceed the \( 0.5 \) conditional quantile? Show that the answer
does not depend on \( \x \), and say which part of @prp-qnt-beyond-mean this
illustrates.
:::

::: {.solution}
The difference is \( \sigma\{\Phi^{-1}(0.9)-\Phi^{-1}(0.5)\}=\sigma\Phi^{-1}(0.9) \),
with \( \Phi^{-1}(0.9)\approx1.2816 \). By @prp-qnt-beyond-mean(a) the two quantile
curves are the regression curve shifted by constants, so their difference is a
constant free of \( \x \).
:::

### B. Practice

::: {#exr-qnt-lognormal-shape}
[B1]

Let \( \log Y\mid x\sim\Normal(\alpha+\beta x,\ \sigma^2) \) with \( \sigma \) constant.
Show that the conditional quantiles of \( Y \) are *not* parallel, that the
conditional coefficient of variation is constant, and that the conditional skewness
is constant. Which parts of @prp-qnt-beyond-mean apply to \( Y \), and which to
\( \log Y \)?
:::

::: {.solution}
By @lem-qnt-monotone with \( h=\exp \),
\( Q_Y(\tau\mid x)=\exp\{\alpha+\beta x+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(\tau)\} \), so the ratio of two
quantile curves is constant while their difference is not: the curves fan out.
\( \E(Y\mid x)=\exp(\alpha+\beta x+\sigma^2/2) \) and
\( \Var(Y\mid x)=\E(Y\mid x)^2(e^{\sigma^2}-1) \), so the coefficient of variation is
\( (e^{\sigma^2}-1)^{1/2} \), free of \( x \); the skewness of a lognormal depends only
on \( \sigma \). Thus \( \log Y \) obeys part (a) and \( Y \) obeys part (b) with
\( \exp(\x\T\boldsymbol{\upalpha}) \) proportional to \( \exp(\beta x) \): a covariate-dependent
scale but a frozen shape.
:::

::: {#exr-qnt-band-arithmetic}
[B2]

In @exm-qnt-engel-bands the homoscedastic \( 95\% \) prediction interval has
half-width close to \( 2\times114.1 \) francs for every household. Using the
within-third standard deviations, say roughly what nominal level that half-width
actually attains in the poorest third and in the richest third, if the conditional

:::

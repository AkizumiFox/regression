# Properties of the estimator under second-moment assumptions

Least squares was defined for a fixed data vector. Now let the data be random,
generated by the linear model of @def-lm-linear-model:
\[
\Y=\X\bbeta+\be,\qquad \E(\be)=\bzero,\qquad \Cov(\be)=\sigma^2\I_n,
\]
with \( \X \) of full rank \( p \). The estimator \( \hbeta=(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T\Y \) is then a
random vector, and so are \( \hY \) and \( \he \). This section computes their means and
covariances. Only the second-moment assumptions are used, so the results hold for skewed,
heavy-tailed or discrete errors alike, as long as they have finite variance.

## Mean and covariance of the estimator

The key observation is that \( \hbeta \) is a *linear* function of \( \Y \):
\( \hbeta=\A\Y \) with the constant matrix \( \A=(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T \). The moments of a
linear transformation were worked out in [Chapter 2](../ch02-random-vectors/index.html).

::: {#thm-lm-moments}
[Moments of the least squares estimator]

Let \( \X \) have full rank \( p \) and \( \hbeta=(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T\Y \).

::: {.enumerate options="label=(\alph*)"}
1. If \( \E(\Y)=\X\bbeta \), then \( \E(\hbeta)=\bbeta \): the least squares estimator is
   unbiased.

2. If moreover \( \Cov(\Y)=\sigma^2\I_n \), then
   \[
   \Cov(\hbeta)=\sigma^2(\X\T\X)^{-1}.
   \]{#eq-lm-cov-beta}

:::

:::

::: {.proof}
Let \( \A=(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T \). By @thm-rv-linear(a),
\( \E(\hbeta)=\A\,\E(\Y)=(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T\X\bbeta=\bbeta \). By @thm-rv-linear(b),
\[
\Cov(\hbeta)=\A(\sigma^2\I_n)\A\T=\sigma^2(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T\X(\X\T\X)^{-1}=\sigma^2(\X\T\X)^{-1},
\]
using the symmetry of \( (\X\T\X)^{-1} \).
:::

The proof is two lines, but the division of labour between the assumptions is worth
stating. Unbiasedness needs only (L1): the errors may have unequal variances or be
correlated, and \( \hbeta \) still centres on \( \bbeta \). The covariance formula needs (L2)
and (L3) as well. Neither needs normality. Under normality \( \hbeta \) is itself normal,
since it is a linear function of a normal vector (@thm-mvn-linear), so
\( \hbeta\sim\Normal_p\bigl(\bbeta,\sigma^2(\X\T\X)^{-1}\bigr) \). [Chapter 7](../ch07-optimality/index.html) builds exact
inference on this (@thm-opt-sampling).

The matrix \( (\X\T\X)^{-1} \) carries all the information about how the design affects
precision. Its diagonal entries give the variances of the individual coefficients, and its
off-diagonal entries their covariances. When two columns are strongly positively
correlated, their coefficients are typically estimated with a strong *negative*
correlation: the data can tell how large the two effects are together, but not how to
split the total between them.

::: {#cor-lm-linear-functions}
[Linear functions of the coefficients]

Under the assumptions of @thm-lm-moments(b), let \( \bm a\in\Real^p \) and let \( \bm L \) be a
constant \( q\times p \) matrix. Then
\[
\E(\bm a\T\hbeta)=\bm a\T\bbeta,\qquad \Var(\bm a\T\hbeta)=\sigma^2\bm a\T(\X\T\X)^{-1}\bm a,
\]
and \( \Cov(\bm L\hbeta)=\sigma^2\bm L(\X\T\X)^{-1}\bm L\T \).
In particular, the estimated mean response at a design point \( \x_0 \),
\( \hat{\mu}_0=\x_0\T\hbeta \), is unbiased for \( \mu_0=\x_0\T\bbeta \) with
\( \Var(\hat{\mu}_0)=\sigma^2\x_0\T(\X\T\X)^{-1}\x_0 \), and
\( \Var(\hat{\beta}_j)=\sigma^2\bigl[(\X\T\X)^{-1}\bigr]_{jj} \).
:::

::: {.proof}
Apply @thm-rv-linear to \( \bm L\hbeta \), with covariance
\( \bm L\,\Cov(\hbeta)\bm L\T \), and take \( \bm L=\bm a\T \), \( \bm L=\x_0\T \) and \( \bm L=\bm e_j\T \).
:::

Linear functions of \( \bbeta \) are what applications usually need: a single coefficient,
the difference between two coefficients, the mean response at a new design point, or
the change in mean response between two profiles. [Chapter 7](../ch07-optimality/index.html) shows that for every such
function, \( \bm a\T\hbeta \) has the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators
(the Gauss–Markov theorem, @thm-opt-gauss-markov).

## Fitted values and residuals

::: {#prp-lm-fit-moments}
[Moments of fitted values and residuals]

Under the linear model with \( \X \) of full rank and \( \bH=\X(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T \):

::: {.enumerate options="label=(\alph*)"}
1. \( \E(\hY)=\X\bbeta \) and \( \Cov(\hY)=\sigma^2\bH \);

2. \( \E(\he)=\bzero \) and \( \Cov(\he)=\sigma^2(\I-\bH) \); in particular
   \( \Var(\hat{\varepsilon}_i)=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii}) \), where \( h_{ii} \) is the \( i \)th diagonal entry
   of \( \bH \);

3. \( \Cov(\hY,\he)=\bzero \) and \( \Cov(\hbeta,\he)=\bzero \);

4. \( 0\le h_{ii}\le1 \) for every \( i \), and \( \sum_ih_{ii}=p \).
:::

:::

::: {.proof}
\( \hY=\bH\Y \) and \( \he=(\I-\bH)\Y \), with \( \bH \) symmetric and idempotent and \( \bH\X=\X \)
(@prp-lm-fit-algebra(a)). By @thm-rv-linear, \( \E(\hY)=\bH\X\bbeta=\X\bbeta \) and
\( \Cov(\hY)=\sigma^2\bH\bH\T=\sigma^2\bH \); similarly \( \E(\he)=(\I-\bH)\X\bbeta=\bzero \) and
\( \Cov(\he)=\sigma^2(\I-\bH)^2=\sigma^2(\I-\bH) \). The cross-covariances are
\( \Cov(\bH\Y,(\I-\bH)\Y)=\sigma^2\bH(\I-\bH)=\bzero \) and
\( \Cov(\A\Y,(\I-\bH)\Y)=\sigma^2\A(\I-\bH)=\sigma^2(\A-\A\bH)=\bzero \), since
\( \A\bH=(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T\X(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T=\A \). For (d), \( \sigma^2h_{ii} \) and
\( \sigma^2(1-h_{ii}) \) are variances, so both are nonnegative, and \( \sum_ih_{ii}=\tr\bH=p \).
:::

Two consequences deserve emphasis. First, residuals are *not* uncorrelated and
*not* of equal variance, even when the errors are both. The residual of a case with
\( h_{ii} \) near \( 1 \) has variance near zero: the fit is pulled so close to such a case
that its residual says little about its error. The number \( h_{ii} \) is the **leverage** of
case \( i \) ([Section 6.8](../ch06-projections/08-leverage.html), Chapter 20).
Second, the fitted values and the residuals are uncorrelated. Under normality they are
then independent (@thm-mvn-independence), which is the key to the exact tests of
[Chapter 11](../ch11-general-linear-hypothesis/index.html).

## The straight line

Specializing to \( \X=[\bone,\x] \), the inverse @eq-lm-simple-inverse gives the classical
formulas at once.

::: {#cor-lm-simple-moments}
[Moments for a straight line]

In the model @eq-lm-simple-model with \( S_{xx}>0 \),
\[
\Var(\hat{\beta}_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}},\qquad
\Var(\hat{\beta}_0)=\sigma^2\Bigl(\frac1n+\frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}}\Bigr),\qquad
\Cov(\hat{\beta}_0,\hat{\beta}_1)=-\frac{\sigma^2\bar{x}}{S_{xx}},
\]
and the estimated mean response at \( x_0 \) has
\[
\Var(\hat{\beta}_0+\hat{\beta}_1x_0)=\sigma^2\Bigl(\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}}\Bigr).
\]{#eq-lm-var-mean-response}

Both estimates are unbiased, and \( \bar{Y} \) is uncorrelated with \( \hat{\beta}_1 \).
:::

::: {.proof}
The first three are the entries of \( \sigma^2(\X\T\X)^{-1} \) in @eq-lm-simple-inverse. For
the mean response, @cor-lm-linear-functions with \( \x_0=(1,x_0)\T \) gives
\( \sigma^2\bigl(\tfrac1n+\tfrac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}}-\tfrac{2x_0\bar{x}}{S_{xx}}+\tfrac{x_0^2}{S_{xx}}\bigr) \),
which rearranges to @eq-lm-var-mean-response. Finally
\( \bar{Y}=\hat{\beta}_0+\hat{\beta}_1\bar{x} \) (the line passes through the point of means), and
\( \Cov(\hat{\beta}_0+\hat{\beta}_1\bar{x},\hat{\beta}_1)=-\sigma^2\bar{x}/S_{xx}+\bar{x}\sigma^2/S_{xx}=0 \).
:::

Three design lessons can be read off. The slope is estimated precisely when the
\( x_i \) are spread out, since its variance is inversely proportional to \( S_{xx} \). The
intercept is estimated precisely only near the data: its variance grows with \( \bar{x}^2 \), and
the estimated intercept and slope are strongly negatively correlated when \( \bar{x} \) is
large and positive, the tilting of the ellipses noted after @thm-lm-simple-ls. And the mean
response is estimated best at \( x_0=\bar{x} \), with a variance that grows quadratically as
\( x_0 \) moves away. Extrapolation is doubly risky: the model may fail outside the range of
the data, and even if it holds, the estimate is imprecise there.

::: {#exm-lm-designs}
[Where to put the design points]

Suppose \( n=20 \) observations can be taken anywhere in \( [0,10] \) to estimate a slope.
Three designs are compared in [Figure 5.5.1](#fig-lm-designs). Equally spaced points give
\( S_{xx}=184.21 \) and a slope standard deviation of \( 0.0737\,\sigma \).
Putting half the points at each end gives the largest possible
\( S_{xx}=500.00 \) (@exr-lm-optimal-design) and
\( 0.0447\,\sigma \). Bunching the points in the middle, over \( [4,6] \),
gives \( S_{xx}=7.37 \) and \( 0.3684\,\sigma \), more than eight times
worse than the two-ends design. The simulated sampling distributions in panel (b), each
from 20,000 samples, match these standard deviations. The two-ends design has
a price the variance formula does not show: with only two distinct values of \( x \), the data
cannot reveal curvature. The equally spaced design gives up some precision for the
ability to check the model.
:::

```{.python .run #cell-moments-mc-designs}
import numpy as np
n = 20
designs = {
    "equally spaced": np.linspace(0, 10, n),
    "two ends": np.repeat([0.0, 10.0], n // 2),
    "middle only": np.linspace(4, 6, n),

for name, xd in designs.items():
    Sxx = np.sum((xd - xd.mean()) ** 2)
    print(f"{name:15s} S_xx = {Sxx:7.2f}   sd(slope) = sigma * {1 / np.sqrt(Sxx):.4f}")
```

## Only two moments are needed

::: {#exm-lm-skewed-errors}
[Skewed errors]

To see that normality plays no role in @thm-lm-moments, fit the quadratic
\( \E(Y)=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2x^2 \) at \( x=1,\dots,12 \), with
\( \bbeta=(2,0.5,-0.03)\T \) and errors that are centred exponential variables scaled to
\( \sigma=1.5 \). These errors are strongly skewed. Over 100,000 simulated data sets,
the averages of the three estimates are \( 1.9974 \), \( 0.5005 \) and
\( -0.0300 \), and the simulated standard deviations are
\( 1.5421 \), \( 0.5457 \) and \( 0.0409 \), against
\( 1.5503 \), \( 0.5483 \) and \( 0.0411 \) from @eq-lm-cov-beta.
Every entry of the simulated covariance matrix is within
\( 0.011 \) of the formula, measured as a correlation. The formula also
predicts that \( \hat{\beta}_1 \) and \( \hat{\beta}_2 \) have correlation
\( -0.973 \), because the columns \( x \) and \( x^2 \) are nearly collinear on
\( 1,\dots,12 \). A slightly larger linear coefficient can be almost exactly compensated by a
slightly more negative quadratic one.
:::

```{.python .run #cell-moments-mc-quadratic}
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
x = np.arange(1.0, 13.0)                        # twelve design points
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x, x**2])
beta = np.array([2.0, 0.5, -0.03])
sigma = 1.5
reps = 100_000

A = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T)               # beta_hat = A y
E = sigma * (rng.exponential(size=(reps, len(x))) - 1.0)   # skewed, mean 0, sd sigma
B = (X @ beta + E) @ A.T                        # one estimate per row

print("mean of estimates:", np.round(B.mean(axis=0), 4), " true:", beta)
print("Monte Carlo covariance:\n", np.round(np.cov(B.T), 5))
print("sigma^2 (X^T X)^{-1}:\n", np.round(sigma**2 * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X), 5))
```

## Random regressors

When the regressors are random, the assumptions are read conditionally on \( \X \)
([Section 5.1](01-what-a-model-claims.html)). The conditional results then transfer.

::: {#cor-lm-random-regressors}
[Random regressors]

Let \( (\X,\Y) \) be random, with \( \rank(\X)=p \) with probability one, and suppose
\( \E(\Y\mid\X)=\X\bbeta \) and \( \Cov(\Y\mid\X)=\sigma^2\I_n \). If
\( \E\bigl[(\X\T\X)^{-1}\bigr] \) is finite, then
\[
\E(\hbeta)=\bbeta,\qquad \Cov(\hbeta)=\sigma^2\,\E\bigl[(\X\T\X)^{-1}\bigr].
\]
:::

::: {.proof}
Conditionally on \( \X \), @thm-lm-moments gives \( \E(\hbeta\mid\X)=\bbeta \) and
\( \Cov(\hbeta\mid\X)=\sigma^2(\X\T\X)^{-1} \). By the laws of total expectation and covariance
(@prp-rv-total-covariance), \( \E(\hbeta)=\E(\bbeta)=\bbeta \) and
\( \Cov(\hbeta)=\E\bigl[\sigma^2(\X\T\X)^{-1}\bigr]+\Cov(\bbeta)=\sigma^2\E\bigl[(\X\T\X)^{-1}\bigr] \),
since \( \bbeta \) is constant. The finiteness assumption guarantees \( \hbeta \) has finite
second moments, which @prp-rv-total-covariance needs.
:::

This is what @exm-lm-fixed-random showed by simulation: under the random design, the
variance of the slope was \( \sigma^2\E(1/S_{xx}) \), not \( \sigma^2/S_{xx} \) for any one design.
For inference, the conditional covariance \( \sigma^2(\X\T\X)^{-1} \) is the relevant one,
because it describes the design actually observed.

## Consistency

As the sample grows, a good estimator should approach the truth. For a fixed \( p \) and
a sequence of model matrices \( \X_n \), the mean squared error of \( \hbeta \) is
\[
\E\norm{\hbeta-\bbeta}^2=\tr\Cov(\hbeta)=\sigma^2\tr(\X_n\T\X_n)^{-1},
\]
by @thm-lm-moments and the fact that the trace of a covariance matrix is the sum of the
variances.

::: {#prp-lm-consistency}
[Consistency in mean square]

Under the linear model with \( \X_n \) of full rank \( p \) for every \( n \), if
\( \tr(\X_n\T\X_n)^{-1}\to0 \), equivalently if the smallest eigenvalue of \( \X_n\T\X_n \) tends
to infinity, then \( \E\norm{\hbeta-\bbeta}^2\to0 \), and hence
\( \Pr(\norm{\hbeta-\bbeta}>\delta)\to0 \) for every \( \delta>0 \).
:::

::: {.proof}
The first claim is the display above. The eigenvalues of \( (\X_n\T\X_n)^{-1} \) are the
reciprocals \( 1/\lambda_1,\dots,1/\lambda_p \) of those of \( \X_n\T\X_n \), all positive, so the
trace is \( \sum_j1/\lambda_j \). It tends to zero iff every \( \lambda_j\to\infty \), iff the smallest
does. The last claim is Markov's inequality applied to \( \norm{\hbeta-\bbeta}^2 \):
\( \Pr(\norm{\hbeta-\bbeta}>\delta)\le\E\norm{\hbeta-\bbeta}^2/\delta^2 \).
:::

For a straight line the condition is \( S_{xx}\to\infty \) together with
\( \bar{x}^2/S_{xx}\to0 \), which holds for any design that keeps spreading, but fails, for
instance, for \( x_i=1/i \), where \( S_{xx} \) converges to a finite limit. Then even infinitely
many observations do not pin down the slope (@exr-lm-inconsistent).

## What if the assumptions fail?

The proof of @thm-lm-moments shows exactly what each assumption contributes, so it also
shows what goes wrong without it. Two cases recur throughout the book.

::: {#prp-lm-misspecified}
[Moments under departures]

Let \( \X \) have full rank, \( \A=(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T \) and \( \hbeta=\A\Y \).

::: {.enumerate options="label=(\alph*)"}
1. If \( \E(\Y)=\X\bbeta+\bm\delta \) for some \( \bm\delta\in\Real^n \), then
   \( \E(\hbeta)=\bbeta+(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T\bm\delta \).

2. If \( \E(\Y)=\X\bbeta \) and \( \Cov(\Y)=\bSigma \) for some covariance matrix \( \bSigma \), then
   \( \E(\hbeta)=\bbeta \) and
   \[
   \Cov(\hbeta)=(\X\T\X)^{-1}\X\T\bSigma\X(\X\T\X)^{-1}.
   \]{#eq-lm-sandwich}

:::

:::

::: {.proof}
Both are @thm-rv-linear applied to \( \A\Y \): \( \E(\A\Y)=\A\X\bbeta+\A\bm\delta \) and
\( \Cov(\A\Y)=\A\bSigma\A\T \).
:::

Part (a) says that when the mean is misspecified, least squares estimates not
\( \bbeta \) but \( \bbeta \) plus the coefficients from regressing the missing part \( \bm\delta \) on
\( \X \). The most important case is an omitted regressor, which
[Section 5.7](07-interpreting-coefficients.html) examines. Part (b) says that with the wrong
covariance, the estimator stays unbiased but the formula \( \sigma^2(\X\T\X)^{-1} \) is wrong.
The "sandwich" @eq-lm-sandwich is the correct covariance, and Chapter 21 shows how to
estimate it when \( \bSigma \) is diagonal but unknown. Chapter 19 studies both
departures systematically.

### B. Practice

::: {#exr-lm-optimal-design}
[B1]

Suppose \( n \) is even and the \( x_i \) may be chosen anywhere in \( [a,b] \). Show that
\( S_{xx}\le n(b-a)^2/4 \), with equality iff half the points are at \( a \) and half at \( b \).
*Hint:* \( \lvert x_i-c\rvert\le(b-a)/2 \) with \( c=(a+b)/2 \), and
\( S_{xx}=\sum_i(x_i-c)^2-n(\bar{x}-c)^2 \).
:::

::: {.solution}
With \( c \) the midpoint, \( S_{xx}=\sum_i(x_i-c)^2-n(\bar{x}-c)^2\le\sum_i(x_i-c)^2\le n(b-a)^2/4 \).
Equality in the second step needs every \( x_i\in\{a,b\} \), and in the first step needs
\( \bar{x}=c \), that is, equally many points at each end. For \( [0,10] \) and \( n=20 \) this gives
\( S_{xx}=20\cdot25=500 \), the value in @exm-lm-designs.
:::

::: {#exr-lm-var-difference}
[B2]

In @exm-lm-money, write the variances of \( \hat{\beta}_2-\hat{\beta}_3 \) and \( \hat{\beta}_2+\hat{\beta}_3 \),
the difference and the sum of the two interest-rate coefficients, in terms of the entries of
\( \bm G=(\X\T\X)^{-1} \). Show that if \( \hat{\beta}_2 \) and \( \hat{\beta}_3 \) are negatively correlated, the
difference is estimated *less* precisely, and the sum *more* precisely, than they would be
if the two estimates were uncorrelated. The two rate columns have sample
correlation \( 0.80 \). Which combination would you expect to be better determined?
:::

::: {.solution}
With \( \bm a=(0,0,1,\mp1)\T \) and indices counting the intercept as the first coefficient,
@cor-lm-linear-functions gives \( \Var(\hat{\beta}_2\mp\hat{\beta}_3)=\sigma^2(g_{33}+g_{44}\mp2g_{34}) \),
and \( \Cov(\hat{\beta}_2,\hat{\beta}_3)=\sigma^2g_{34} \). If \( g_{34}<0 \), the variance of the difference
exceeds \( \sigma^2(g_{33}+g_{44})=\Var(\hat{\beta}_2)+\Var(\hat{\beta}_3) \), and that of the sum falls below it.
Positively correlated columns tend to produce negatively correlated estimates (as in
@exm-lm-skewed-errors), because the data can tell how large the combined effect of the two
rates is more easily than how to divide it between them. So the sum should be the better
determined combination.
:::

### C. Going deeper

::: {#exr-lm-inconsistent}
[C1]

Let \( x_i=1/i \) for \( i=1,2,\dots \) in the straight-line model. Show that \( S_{xx} \) converges to
a finite limit as \( n\to\infty \), so that \( \Var(\hat{\beta}_1) \) does not tend to zero. Then construct
a sequence of designs, in which the points may change with \( n \) (for example, designs centred
farther and farther from \( 0 \)), with \( S_{xx}\to\infty \) but \( \bar{x}^2/S_{xx}\not\to0 \), and decide
whether the slope, the intercept, or both fail to be consistent.
:::

::: {.solution}
With \( x_i=1/i \), \( S_{xx}=\sum_{i\le n}1/i^2-n\bar{x}^2 \). The sum increases to \( \pi^2/6 \), and
\( n\bar{x}^2=H_n^2/n\to0 \) because the harmonic number \( H_n=\sum_{i\le n}1/i \) grows like \( \log n \).
So \( S_{xx}\to\pi^2/6 \) and \( \Var(\hat{\beta}_1)=\sigma^2/S_{xx} \) stays above \( 6\sigma^2/\pi^2 \).

For even \( n \) take \( x_i=n+(-1)^i \), \( i=1,\dots,n \): half the points at \( n-1 \), half at \( n+1 \).
Then \( \bar{x}=n \) and \( S_{xx}=n \), so \( \bar{x}^2/S_{xx}=n\to\infty \). By
@cor-lm-simple-moments, \( \Var(\hat{\beta}_1)=\sigma^2/n\to0 \), so the slope is consistent (Chebyshev's
inequality), while \( \Var(\hat{\beta}_0)=\sigma^2(1/n+n)\to\infty \). The intercept, the mean response
at \( x=0 \), ever farther from the data, is not consistent: with normal errors,
\( \hat{\beta}_0-\beta_0 \) is normal with unbounded variance.
:::

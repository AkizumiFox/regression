# Orthogonal polynomials

The space \( \mathcal P_d \), evaluated at the design, is an ordinary
\( (d+1) \)-dimensional subspace of \( \Real^n \). What is bad is the spanning set
\( 1,x,\dots,x^d \). An orthonormal basis makes \( \X\T\X=\I \), the condition number
one, and the coefficients independent of the degree — and, by @thm-proj-reparam,
changes not one fitted value.
[Section 9.4](../ch09-sums-of-squares/04-orthogonal-designs.html) built these
polynomials by Gram–Schmidt; this section supplies what they are for an
arbitrary design, the recurrence that generates them in \( O(nd) \) operations,
and what changes when the basis is swapped.

## The inner product of a design

::: {#def-ply-orthogonal}
[Orthogonal polynomials for a design]

Let \( x_1,\dots,x_n \) be design points, \( m \) of them distinct, with weights
\( w_1,\dots,w_n>0 \). For real functions \( f,g \) on the design write
\[
\inner{f}{g}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_if(x_i)g(x_i),
\qquad \norm{f}^2=\inner{f}{f},
\]
an inner product on the functions restricted to the design. Polynomials
\( p_0,\dots,p_d \) with \( d<m \) are the **orthogonal polynomials of the design**
if \( p_j \) has degree exactly \( j \) with leading coefficient one (it is
**monic**) and \( \inner{p_j}{p_k}=0 \) for \( j\neq k \). The normalized
\( q_j=p_j/\norm{p_j} \) are the **orthonormal polynomials** of the design.
:::

The restriction \( d<m \) is the rank condition of @exr-ply-vandermonde-rank:
with fewer distinct points some nonzero polynomial of degree \( d \) vanishes on
the design, and the form is only nonnegative definite. Two facts make the
construction work, and both are one line.

::: {#lem-ply-shift}
[Multiplication by the argument is self-adjoint]

For polynomials \( f,g \) let \( (xf)(x)=xf(x) \). Then
\( \inner{xf}{g}=\inner{f}{xg} \).
:::

::: {.proof}
Both sides equal \( \sum_iw_ix_if(x_i)g(x_i) \).
:::

The second is that \( p_j \) is orthogonal to *every* polynomial of degree less
than \( j \), not only to \( p_0,\dots,p_{j-1} \): those span \( \mathcal P_{j-1} \),
because their degrees are \( 0,1,\dots,j-1 \).

## The three-term recurrence

::: {#thm-ply-recurrence}
[Existence, uniqueness and the three-term recurrence]

Let \( d<m \). The orthogonal polynomials of the design exist, are unique, and
are generated by
\[
p_{-1}=0,\qquad p_0=1,\qquad
p_{j+1}(x)=(x-a_j)\,p_j(x)-b_j\,p_{j-1}(x),\quad j=0,1,\dots,d-1,
\]{#eq-ply-recurrence}

with
\[
a_j=\frac{\inner{xp_j}{p_j}}{\norm{p_j}^2},\qquad
b_j=\frac{\norm{p_j}^2}{\norm{p_{j-1}}^2}\ (j\ge1),\qquad b_0=0 .
\]
Every \( \norm{p_j}^2 \) with \( j\le d \) is strictly positive.
:::

::: {.proof}
*Existence and the recurrence together.* Argue by induction on \( j \), the claim
being that \( p_0,\dots,p_j \) produced by @eq-ply-recurrence are monic of degrees
\( 0,\dots,j \), mutually orthogonal, and have positive squared norms. For \( j=0 \),
\( p_0=1 \) is monic of degree 0 and \( \norm{p_0}^2=\sum_iw_i>0 \).

Suppose the claim holds up to \( j\le d-1 \). The polynomial \( p_{j+1} \) defined by
@eq-ply-recurrence is monic of degree \( j+1 \), since \( xp_j \) is monic of degree
\( j+1 \) and the subtracted terms have degree at most \( j \). Take inner products.
First,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\inner{p_{j+1}}{p_j}
&=\inner{xp_j}{p_j}-a_j\norm{p_j}^2-b_j\inner{p_{j-1}}{p_j}\\
&=\inner{xp_j}{p_j}-a_j\norm{p_j}^2=0
\end{aligned}
\]
by the choice of \( a_j \) and the induction hypothesis. Next, for \( j\ge1 \), using
@lem-ply-shift and \( \inner{p_j}{p_{j-1}}=0 \),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\inner{p_{j+1}}{p_{j-1}}
&=\inner{p_j}{xp_{j-1}}-a_j\inner{p_j}{p_{j-1}}-b_j\norm{p_{j-1}}^2\\
&=\inner{p_j}{xp_{j-1}}-b_j\norm{p_{j-1}}^2 .
\end{aligned}
\]
Now \( xp_{j-1} \) is monic of degree \( j \), so \( xp_{j-1}-p_j \) has degree at most
\( j-1 \) and is therefore orthogonal to \( p_j \). Hence
\( \inner{p_j}{xp_{j-1}}=\norm{p_j}^2 \), and the choice of \( b_j \) makes the
display vanish. Finally, for \( k\le j-2 \), @lem-ply-shift gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\inner{p_{j+1}}{p_k}
&=\inner{p_j}{xp_k}-a_j\inner{p_j}{p_k}-b_j\inner{p_{j-1}}{p_k}\\
&=\inner{p_j}{xp_k}=0,
\end{aligned}
\]
because \( xp_k \) has degree at most \( j-1<j \). So \( p_{j+1} \) is orthogonal to all
of \( p_0,\dots,p_j \).

It remains to show \( \norm{p_{j+1}}^2>0 \) when \( j+1\le d \). If it were zero then
\( p_{j+1} \), a monic polynomial of degree \( j+1\le d<m \), would vanish at all \( m \)
distinct design points, which is impossible.

*Uniqueness.* Suppose \( \tilde p_0,\dots,\tilde p_d \) also satisfy the
definition. Since the degrees are \( 0,\dots,j \), the set
\( \{p_0,\dots,p_j\} \) is a basis of \( \mathcal P_j \), so
\( \tilde p_j=\sum_{k\le j}c_kp_k \). Orthogonality of \( \tilde p_j \) to
\( \mathcal P_{j-1} \) gives \( 0=\inner{\tilde p_j}{p_k}=c_k\norm{p_k}^2 \) for
\( k<j \), hence \( c_k=0 \); and both being monic forces \( c_j=1 \).
:::

The recurrence needs only the two previous polynomials, so the work is
\( O(nd) \) rather than Gram–Schmidt's \( O(nd^2) \); it never forms \( x^j \) for
\( j>1 \), so nothing overflows or cancels; and \( a_j,b_j \) describe the basis in
\( 2d \) numbers, which is how a fitted polynomial should be stored.

::: {.remark}
[Why only three terms]

Writing \( xp_j=\sum_{k\le j+1}c_{jk}p_k \), the coefficient
\( c_{jk}\norm{p_k}^2=\inner{xp_j}{p_k}=\inner{p_j}{xp_k} \) vanishes whenever
\( xp_k \) has degree below \( j \), that is whenever \( k<j-1 \). The same argument
gives three-term recurrences for the classical families — Legendre, Chebyshev,
Hermite, Laguerre — the orthogonal polynomials of \( \inner{f}{g}=\int fg\,w \),
which [Section 39.5](../ch39-glms-in-practice-bayes/05-priors-as-regularizers.html)
used for numerical integration.
:::

## Sequential fitting

::: {#cor-ply-sequential}
[Fitting in the orthonormal basis]

Let \( \mathbf{q}_j \) have entries \( q_j(x_i) \) and let the weights be one, so the
\( \mathbf{q}_j \) are orthonormal in \( \Real^n \); write
\( \hat\gamma_j=\mathbf{q}_j\T\y \). For every \( d<m \):

::: {.enumerate options="label=(\alph*)"}
1. the least squares fit of degree \( d \) is
   \( \hat f_d(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\hat\gamma_jq_j(t) \), and \( \hat\gamma_j \) does not
   depend on \( d \);

2. \( \E(\hat\gamma_j)=\inner{f}{q_j} \) when \( \E(Y_i)=f(x_i) \) for any function
   \( f \), and \( \Cov(\hat{\bgamma})=\sigma^2\I \);

3. the residual sum of squares equals \( \norm{\y}^2-\sum_{j\le d}\hat\gamma_j^2 \),
   so the sequential sum of squares for degree \( j \) is \( \hat\gamma_j^2 \) on one
   degree of freedom, free of the order of entry;

4. the \( F \) statistic for \( H:\gamma_d=0 \) in the model of degree \( d \) is
   \( \hat\gamma_d^2/\hat\sigma_d^2 \), with \( \hat\sigma_d^2 \) that model's
   residual mean square, on \( (1,n-d-1) \) degrees of freedom.
:::

:::

::: {.proof}
With orthonormal columns \( \X\T\X=\I \), so \( \hbeta=\X\T\y \) componentwise,
which is (a); the columns for degrees below \( d \) do not change when \( d \) grows,
as in @cor-ss-orthogonal-coefficients. Part (b) is
\( \E(\mathbf{q}_j\T\Y)=\mathbf{q}_j\T\bmu=\inner{f}{q_j} \) with
\( \Cov(\X\T\Y)=\sigma^2\X\T\X \); part (c) is Pythagoras,
\( \norm{\y}^2=\sum_{j\le d}\hat\gamma_j^2+\norm{\he}^2 \),
and @thm-ss-orthogonal-design; part (d) is @thm-glh-t-test for a single coefficient
with \( \Var(\hat\gamma_d)=\sigma^2 \), squared.
:::

Part (b) is worth a second look. Whatever the true regression function,
\( \hat\gamma_j \) estimates the \( j \)th coefficient of its expansion in the
design's own orthonormal basis, with variance \( \sigma^2 \) for every \( j \).
Where the sequence sinks into the noise is where the data stop distinguishing
degrees: the practice of choosing the degree from the sequential sums of
squares, and its honest statement.

::: {.warning}
Stopping at the first small \( \hat\gamma_j^2 \) is selection from the same data:
the chosen model's residual mean square is too small, its intervals too narrow,
and the \( F \) statistic that justified the stop is not
\( F \) (@prp-sel-selection-bias). The sequence can also dip and rise again, as it
does below, so one insignificant term is a weak reason to stop.
:::

## The Nile, revisited

::: {#exm-ply-nile-recurrence}
[The recurrence on the Nile series]

The annual flow of the Nile at Aswan, 1871–1970, gives \( n=100 \)
equally spaced design points, rescaled here to \( [-1,1] \).
@exm-ss-nile-polynomials built its orthonormal polynomials by a QR
factorization of the matrix of powers; @eq-ply-recurrence gives the same basis,
the largest discrepancy between corresponding columns being
\( 1.1\times 10^{-14} \) and the largest inner product between
distinct columns \( 6.7\times 10^{-16} \), machine precision.
The design being symmetric about its mean, every \( a_j \)
vanishes (@exr-ply-symmetric-a), and the first four values of \( b_j \) are
\( 0.34007 \), \( 0.27197 \),
\( 0.26213 \), \( 0.25871 \);
[Figure 42.2.1](#fig-ply-recurrence)(a) draws the polynomials themselves.

The sequential sums of squares reproduce the table of
@exm-ss-nile-polynomials — \( 613893 \), \( 309415 \),
\( 1894 \), \( 134142 \) for degrees 1 to 4, and
\( 4225 \) and \( 7034 \) for degrees 5 and 6 — with
\( F \) statistics \( 27.08 \), \( 15.70 \),
\( 0.10 \), \( 7.18 \), \( 0.22 \)
and \( 0.37 \). The cubic term is negligible and the quartic
is not: a reader stopping at the first insignificant term would have missed it.
:::

```{.python .run #cell-orthogonal-recurrence-recurrence}
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

nile = sm.datasets.nile.load_pandas().data
year = nile["year"].to_numpy().astype(float)
y = nile["volume"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)
u = 2 * (year - year.min()) / (year.max() - year.min()) - 1     # design rescaled to [-1, 1]


def orthogonal_polynomials(t, d):
    """Monic polynomials p_0, ..., p_d orthogonal over the design t, by the recurrence."""
    P = np.zeros((len(t), d + 1))
    P[:, 0] = 1.0
    a, b, sq = np.zeros(d), np.zeros(d), [float(len(t))]
    for j in range(d):
        a[j] = (t * P[:, j] @ P[:, j]) / sq[j]
        P[:, j + 1] = (t - a[j]) * P[:, j]
        if j > 0:
            b[j] = sq[j] / sq[j - 1]
            P[:, j + 1] -= b[j] * P[:, j - 1]
        sq.append(P[:, j + 1] @ P[:, j + 1])
    return P, a, b, np.array(sq)


P, a, b, sq = orthogonal_polynomials(u, 8)
Q = P / np.sqrt(sq)                                             # orthonormal columns
print("a_j:", np.round(a[:5], 6))
print("b_j:", np.round(b[1:5], 5))
print("largest inner product between distinct columns:",
      f"{np.max(np.abs(Q.T @ Q - np.eye(9))):.2e}")
```

```{.python .run #cell-orthogonal-recurrence-sequential}
coef = Q.T @ y                                    # gamma_j = <q_j, y>, unchanged by the degree
seq_ss = coef[1:] ** 2                            # sequential sum of squares for each degree
sse = np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2) - np.cumsum(seq_ss)
for j in range(1, 7):
    F = seq_ss[j - 1] / (sse[j - 1] / (n - j - 1))
    print(f"degree {j}: SS = {seq_ss[j - 1]:9.0f}   SSE = {sse[j - 1]:9.0f}   F = {F:7.2f}")
```

::: {.remark}
[A limit that explains the numbers]

For a design filling an interval evenly, \( n^{-1}\sum_if(x_i)g(x_i) \) is a
Riemann sum for \( \tfrac12\int_{-1}^{1}fg \), so the design's orthogonal
polynomials approach the monic Legendre polynomials, with \( a_j=0 \) and
\( b_j=j^2/(4j^2-1) \) (@exr-ply-legendre): that is
\( 0.33333 \), \( 0.26667 \),
\( 0.25714 \), \( 0.25397 \) against the
Nile's \( 0.34007 \), \( 0.27197 \),
\( 0.26213 \), \( 0.25871 \). Since
\( b_j\to1/4 \), the monic polynomials shrink geometrically: badly scaled even
though orthogonal, so normalizing matters as much as orthogonalizing.
:::

## What changes and what does not

For the Nile design the condition numbers of four bases for \( \mathcal P_d \) are

| Degree | raw years | rescaled | monic orthogonal | orthonormal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | \( 1.83\times 10^{10} \) | \( 3.70\times 10^{0} \) | \( 3.29\times 10^{0} \) | \( 1 \) |
| 4 | \( 3.90\times 10^{20} \) | \( 1.85\times 10^{1} \) | \( 1.26\times 10^{1} \) | \( 1 \) |
| 6 | \( 2.83\times 10^{31} \) | \( 9.77\times 10^{1} \) | \( 4.92\times 10^{1} \) | \( 1 \) |
| 8 | \( 1.42\times 10^{40} \) | \( 5.31\times 10^{2} \) | \( 1.93\times 10^{2} \) | \( 1 \) |

Orthogonality by itself buys a factor growing from about one to about three
over this range; the orthonormal basis has \( \X\T\X=\I \), so its condition
number is one at every degree and least squares in it is a matrix–vector
product. The gap between the last two columns is entirely scaling.

What does *not* change is everything else: \( \hY \), \( \he \), every leverage,
every band, every \( F \) statistic about the *space*. The degree-16 polynomial of
[Figure 42.1.1](01-polynomial-regression.html#fig-ply-degree) oscillates identically
in the orthonormal basis, and its extrapolation is still nonsense. Orthogonal
polynomials answer the first complaint of
[Section 42.1](01-polynomial-regression.html) and are silent on the second.

::: {.remark}
[Where these have appeared before]

For a factor with quantitative levels, the same construction with weights
\( w_k=n_k \) gives the polynomial contrasts of
[Section 8.7](../ch08-estimability/07-contrasts.html) (@exm-est-party-trend): the
orthogonal polynomial coding of a factor and the basis that software offers for
a continuous regressor are one object.
:::

:::

::: {#exr-ply-degree-invariance}
[A2]

A quartic is fitted in the raw powers and again in the orthonormal polynomials of the

### B. Practice

::: {#exr-ply-symmetric-a}
[B1]

Show that if \( x\mapsto2c-x \) permutes the design and preserves the weights, then
\( a_j=c \) for every \( j \) and \( p_j(2c-x)=(-1)^jp_j(x) \).
:::

::: {.solution}
Take \( c=0 \) after a shift, and induct. \( p_0 \) is even, and \( p_1=x-a_0 \) with
\( a_0=\inner{x}{1}/\norm{1}^2=0 \) by symmetry, so \( p_1 \) is odd. If \( p_{j-1} \) and
\( p_j \) have parities \( (-1)^{j-1} \) and \( (-1)^j \), then \( xp_j^2 \) is odd, so
\( a_j=0 \), and \( p_{j+1}=xp_j-b_jp_{j-1} \) is a sum of two polynomials of parity
\( (-1)^{j+1} \).
:::

::: {#exr-ply-legendre}
[B2]

The monic Legendre polynomials are orthogonal for \( \inner{f}{g}=\int_{-1}^{1}fg \).
Using @thm-ply-recurrence and the identity \( \int_{-1}^{1}P_j^2=2/(2j+1) \) for the
standard Legendre polynomials, whose leading coefficient is \( \binom{2j}{j}2^{-j} \),
verify that \( b_j=j^2/(4j^2-1) \).
:::

::: {.solution}
Write \( \tilde P_j=P_j/\ell_j \) with \( \ell_j=\binom{2j}{j}2^{-j} \) for the monic
version. Then \( \norm{\tilde P_j}^2=2/\{(2j+1)\ell_j^2\} \) and
\[
b_j=\frac{\norm{\tilde P_j}^2}{\norm{\tilde P_{j-1}}^2}
=\frac{(2j-1)\ell_{j-1}^2}{(2j+1)\ell_j^2}.
\]
Since \( \ell_j/\ell_{j-1}=(2j-1)/j \), the ratio is
\( (2j-1)j^2/\{(2j+1)(2j-1)^2\}=j^2/(4j^2-1) \).
:::

::: {#exr-ply-storage}
[B3]

Given \( a_0,\dots,a_{d-1} \), \( b_1,\dots,b_{d-1} \), \( \norm{p_j}^2 \) and
\( \hat\gamma_0,\dots,\hat\gamma_d \), write down an algorithm that evaluates
\( \hat f_d(t) \) at a new \( t \) in \( O(d) \) operations and storage, without ever
computing a power of \( t \). Why is this preferable to expanding \( \hat f_d \) in the
monomial basis and using Horner's rule?
:::

::: {.solution}
Run @eq-ply-recurrence forwards at the single point \( t \), accumulating
\( \sum_j\hat\gamma_jp_j(t)/\norm{p_j} \) as you go; only \( p_{j-1}(t) \) and \( p_j(t) \)
need be kept. (Running it backwards, accumulating the coefficients instead, is
Clenshaw's algorithm, the standard way of summing a Chebyshev or other
orthogonal-polynomial series.) Horner's rule is equally fast but needs the monomial
coefficients, and converting the fit to those is the ill-conditioned step: the
conversion matrix is the change of basis whose condition number the table above reports.
:::

[C2]

For two regressors the analogue of \( \mathcal P_d \) is the span of the monomials
\( x^rz^s \) with \( r+s\le d \). List an orthogonal basis \( \phi_1,\phi_2,\dots \) of it
in a fixed order of nondecreasing total degree. The one-variable proof works because
\( xp_j \) expands in \( p_{j-1},p_j,p_{j+1} \) alone; exhibit a design in \( \Real^2 \)
and an index \( i \) for which \( x\phi_i \) has a nonzero inner product with some
\( \phi_k \), \( k<i-1 \), so that no scalar three-term recurrence generates the list.

single-variable bookkeeping is what fails.) Relate this to

33.5  Growth curves

A growth-curve model gives each subject a curve of its own — a straight line, a polynomial in time — the population curve being their average. How is the average curve estimated, and how is an individual’s curve recovered from its own few observations? The second answer is the best linear unbiased predictor: a matrix-weighted average of the subject’s own noisy curve and the precise population curve, weighted by signal against signal-plus-noise — the shrinkage of Chapter 27 by a different road.

33.5.1. Two formulations

Let each of N subjects be observed at the same m times, and collect the data in an m×N matrix Y whose column i is subject i’s profile. Let X be the m×p matrix of a chosen basis in time, say [1,t] for a straight line.

The Potthoff–Roy formulation (Potthoff and Roy 1964) writes

E(Y)=XΞA⊤,Cov(column i)=Σ, columns independent,
(33.5.1)

where A is an N×k matrix of between-subject covariates (a column of ones if the subjects form one group, group indicators if not) and Ξ is the p×k matrix of curve coefficients, with Σ unstructured. It is a multivariate regression with a design on both sides: X acts on the times and A on the subjects.

The random-coefficient formulation gives each subject its own coefficient vector,

𝛃𝛆𝛆yi=X(β+ui)+εi,ui∼(0,D) independent of εi∼(0,σ2Im),
(33.5.2)

with u1,…,uN uncorrelated. This is the linear mixed model (Definition 32.2.1) with Zi=X, G=D and R=σ2I, and the implied within-subject covariance is

Σ=XDX⊤+σ2Im,
(33.5.3)

a covariance with p(p+1)/2+1 parameters instead of the m(m+1)/2 of Eq. 33.5.1 — four, for a straight line, whatever m is. The two describe the same mean structure: with one group of subjects, the random-coefficient model is Eq. 33.5.1 with A=1N and Σ restricted to Eq. 33.5.3. That restriction gives C(ΣX)⊆C(X), so the population curve is ordinary least squares and needs no covariance parameter (Proposition 33.5.1(a) below); with Σ unstructured the inclusion fails unless p=m, and the efficient estimate is a genuine generalized least squares fit.

33.5.2. Estimation

Proposition 33.5.1 (The balanced random-coefficient growth model)

Assume Eq. 33.5.2 with a common X of full column rank p≤m, and D positive definite. Write K=X⊤X, C=σ2K−1, S=D+C, and let bi=K−1X⊤yi be subject i’s own least squares coefficient vector.

  • (a) C(ΣX)⊆C(X), so ordinary and generalized least squares coincide, and

    𝛃𝛃β^=K−1X⊤y¯=b¯,Cov(β^)=D+σ2K−1N=SN,
    (33.5.4)

    exactly, for every N. No covariance parameter is needed to estimate the population curve.

  • (b) The best linear unbiased predictor of ui (Theorem 32.3.1, which is Theorem 14.2.8 applied coordinatewise with x0=0) is

    𝛃𝛃u^i=DX⊤Σ−1(yi−Xβ^)=W(bi−β^),W=DS−1,
    (33.5.5)

    with W=D(D+C)−1 and Cov(u^i−ui)=D−(1−1/N)WD. The predicted curve of subject i is 𝛃X(β^+u^i), with

    𝛃𝛃Cov(β^+u^i−β−ui)=(D−1+C−1)−1+(I−W)CN.
    (33.5.6)

  • (c) The restricted maximum likelihood estimates of σ2 and D are

    σ^2=1N(m−p)∑i=1N‖yi−Xbi‖2,D^=Sb−σ^2K−1,
    (33.5.7)

    where Sb=(N−1)−1∑i(bi−b¯)(bi−b¯)⊤, provided D^ is nonnegative definite.

Proof.

  • (a) ΣX=XDK+σ2X=X(DK+σ2I), whose columns lie in C(X). Stacking the subjects, the model matrix is 1N⊗X and the covariance is IN⊗Σ, so (IN⊗Σ)(1N⊗X)=1N⊗ΣX has columns in C(1N⊗X), and Theorem 6.9.3 gives the equality of the two fits (this is Theorem 31.2.2 in the balanced case). The ordinary least squares estimate from the stacked data is (NK)−1∑iX⊤yi=K−1X⊤y¯=b¯. Its covariance is K−1X⊤(Σ/N)XK−1, and K−1X⊤ΣXK−1=K−1(KDK+σ2K)K−1=D+σ2K−1.

  • (b) The first expression is Theorem 32.3.1. For the second, Theorem 1.6.5 gives Σ−1=σ−2{I−X(σ2D−1+K)−1X⊤}, so DX⊤Σ−1=σ−2D{I−K(σ2D−1+K)−1}X⊤. The braced matrix is σ2D−1(σ2D−1+K)−1, so DX⊤Σ−1=(σ2D−1+K)−1X⊤; applying this to 𝛃yi−Xβ^ and inserting KK−1 gives 𝛃u^i=(σ2D−1+K)−1K(bi−β^), and (σ2D−1+K)−1K=(σ2I+DK)−1DK=D(σ2K−1+D)−1=W.

    For the prediction error covariance, Theorem 32.3.1 gives Cov(u^−u)=G−GZ⊤PZG with P=V−1−V−1X∗(X∗⊤V−1X∗)−1X∗⊤V−1 for the stacked model. Here Z=IN⊗X, G=IN⊗D, X∗=1N⊗X and V=IN⊗Σ, so Z⊤PZ=(IN−N−11N1N⊤)⊗(X⊤Σ−1X) and the ith diagonal block of G−GZ⊤PZG is D−(1−1/N)DX⊤Σ−1XD=D−(1−1/N)WD, using DX⊤Σ−1X=W from the first part.

    For Eq. 33.5.6, write 𝛃bi=β+ui+ci with 𝛆ci=K−1X⊤εi∼(0,C) independent of ui, so 𝛃𝛃β^=β+u¯+c¯ and 𝛃u^i=W(bi−β^). Then 𝛃Cov(β^,u^i)={Cov(b¯,bi)−Cov(b¯)}W⊤=0 and 𝛃Cov(β^,ui)=D/N, so 𝛃𝛃Cov(β^+u^i−β−ui)=SN+D−(1−1N)WD−2DN. Now D−WD=D−DS−1D=DS−1(S−D)=WC and I−W=(S−D)S−1=CS−1, so the right-hand side is WC+(S−2D+DS−1D)/N=WC+CS−1C/N=WC+(I−W)C/N. Finally WC=DS−1C={C−1SD−1}−1=(C−1+D−1)−1, which is Eq. 33.5.6.

  • (c) An error contrast is a linear function of the data with mean zero for every 𝛃β (Definition 32.4.1). Let U be m×(m−p) with columns an orthonormal basis of C(X)⊥, and consider the Nm−p functions wi=U⊤yi (i=1,…,N),bi−b¯ (i=1,…,N). They are error contrasts, and a maximal set: the map yi↦(bi,wi) is a bijection, the wi contribute N(m−p) independent functions and the bi−b¯ contribute (N−1)p, totalling Nm−p. Now 𝛆wi=U⊤εi∼Nm−p(0,σ2I) independently, while 𝛃bi∼Np(β,S) independently, and Cov(bi,wi)=σ2K−1X⊤U=0; the two blocks are independent, so the restricted likelihood factors as LR(σ2,D)=L1(σ2)⋅L2(S),S=D+σ2K−1, with L1 the likelihood of N(m−p) independent N(0,σ2) variables and L2 that of S from the contrasts bi−b¯. Since (σ2,D)↦(σ2,S) is a bijection onto {S−σ2K−1⪰0}, the unconstrained maximizers are the restricted maximum likelihood estimates whenever they satisfy the constraint. Maximizing L1 gives σ^2=∑i‖yi−Xbi‖2/{N(m−p)}, and maximizing L2, the restricted likelihood of a multivariate normal mean model with design 1N, gives S^=Sb (Theorem 32.4.2). Subtracting yields D^.

Part (a) is a statement about the restriction Eq. 33.5.3, not about balance alone: the population curve is ordinary least squares on the average profile, which is also the average of the subjects’ own curves, and nothing has to be estimated first. Potthoff and Roy’s own proposal was not to estimate Σ at all: transforming each profile by a positive definite weight matrix reduces Eq. 33.5.1 to an ordinary multivariate analysis of variance, whose tests are exact for weights chosen without looking at the data. The efficient estimate came later, with Rao (1965); estimating Σ from the residual profiles and weighting by it is feasible generalized least squares (Definition 31.4.1).

Idea. W=D(D+C)−1 compares two covariances: D, how much subjects really differ, and C=σ2(X⊤X)−1, how noisily each subject’s own curve is measured. When subjects differ a lot relative to the noise each keeps its own curve, W≈I; when they hardly differ every subject is given the population curve, W≈0. Between those the predictor borrows strength across subjects, coordinate by coordinate: an intercept may be barely shrunk while a slope is shrunk by a quarter.

Part (c) says that restricted maximum likelihood here is the obvious moment estimator: pool the within-subject residual sums of squares for σ2, and from the spread of the subjects’ own coefficient vectors subtract the part that is estimation noise. The subtraction can produce a matrix that is not nonnegative definite — the growth-curve version of the negative variance component of Proposition 32.1.1 — and the estimate then sits on the boundary, where the closed form no longer applies.

33.5.3. Whose curve

Two prediction problems must be kept apart. For a subject in the data the predicted curve is 𝛃X(β^+u^i), with error covariance Eq. 33.5.6: the first term is what remains even with 𝛃β known, the second the price of estimating it. For a subject not in the data nothing is known but its membership of the population, so the best predictor is 𝛃Xβ^ with error covariance D+S/N — far larger, as it should be.

33.5.4. An example

Example (The investment trajectories of eleven firms)

The panel of Example (Investment in eleven firms), seen a third time — after the random firm effect of Chapter 32 and the covariance models of Section 33.4 — now with a straight line in t (decades from the middle of the record) whose intercept and slope vary by firm. The within-firm residual standard deviation is σ^=0.2797, and D^=(2.36350.04550.04550.0361), so firms’ log-investment levels have standard deviation 1.5374 — enormous, the difference between General Motors and Diamond Match — and their slopes 0.1899 per decade, correlated 0.156 with the level. Maximizing the restricted likelihood numerically returns the same three values to four decimals, as Proposition 33.5.1(c) predicts.

The population curve has intercept 3.9109 and slope 0.5577 per decade, with standard errors 0.4639 and 0.0659 from Eq. 33.5.4; the slope is 8.46 standard errors from zero. The shrinkage matrix is W=(0.99830.00160.00480.7496), so intercepts are left alone — with twenty observations per firm a firm’s own level is known almost exactly — while slopes are pulled a quarter of the way towards the population slope. The firms’ own slopes span 0.158 to 0.943, the predicted slopes 0.248 to 0.846, a standard deviation 0.755 times that of the raw slopes; the largest adjustment is IBM’s, from 0.943 to 0.846. Figure 33.5.1 shows the eleven firms with all three lines.

For the last year of the record, t=0.95, the predicted value on a firm’s own curve has standard error 0.1102, against 1.6461 for a firm outside the data — fifteen times larger, and simulation from the fitted model confirms both, at 0.1103 and 1.6603. The interval for a new firm, 1.214 to 7.667 on the log scale, is nearly useless, and rightly so: without knowing a firm’s size, its investment cannot be predicted within three orders of magnitude.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

df = sm.datasets.grunfeld.load_pandas().data
firms = sorted(df["firm"].unique())
years = np.sort(df["year"].unique())
N, m = len(firms), len(years)
t = (years - years.mean()) / 10                      # decades from the middle of the record
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(m), t])                 # the same design for every firm
p = X.shape[1]
Y = np.column_stack([np.log(df[df["firm"] == f].sort_values("year")["invest"].to_numpy())
                     for f in firms])                # m x N: one column per firm
XtXinv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
B = XtXinv @ X.T @ Y                                 # p x N: each firm's own least squares line
beta_hat = B.mean(axis=1)                            # the average of the firm lines
E = Y - X @ B                                        # within-firm residuals
sigma2 = np.sum(E**2) / (N * (m - p))                # pooled within-firm variance
Sb = np.cov(B, ddof=1)                               # spread of the firm lines
D_hat = Sb - sigma2 * XtXinv                         # ... minus what estimation noise adds
print("beta_hat", beta_hat.round(4), " sigma", round(np.sqrt(sigma2), 4))
print("D_hat\n", D_hat.round(4))
print("implied sd of firm intercepts and slopes:", np.sqrt(np.diag(D_hat)).round(4))
Sigma = X @ D_hat @ X.T + sigma2 * np.eye(m)
cov_beta = (D_hat + sigma2 * XtXinv) / N             # exact covariance of beta_hat
W = D_hat @ np.linalg.inv(D_hat + sigma2 * XtXinv)   # the shrinkage matrix
B_blup = beta_hat[:, None] + W @ (B - beta_hat[:, None])
u_direct = D_hat @ X.T @ np.linalg.solve(Sigma, Y - (X @ beta_hat)[:, None])
print("shrinkage matrix\n", W.round(4))
print("largest difference from the direct formula:",
      np.abs(B_blup - beta_hat[:, None] - u_direct).max())
C = sigma2 * XtXinv                                  # noise in a firm's own line
V_known = np.linalg.inv(np.linalg.inv(D_hat) + np.linalg.inv(C)) \
    + (np.eye(p) - W) @ C / N                        # error of the predicted firm curve
V_new = D_hat + cov_beta                             # error for a firm not in the data
x_end = np.array([1.0, t[-1]])
se_curve_known = np.sqrt(x_end @ V_known @ x_end)
se_curve_new = np.sqrt(x_end @ V_new @ x_end)
pred_new = x_end @ beta_hat
print(f"1954 curve: se for a firm in the data {se_curve_known:.4f}, "
      f"for a new firm {se_curve_new:.4f}")

Remark (Beyond the balanced straight line). Three extensions matter in practice and none changes the ideas. Subjects observed at different times, or a different number of times: Eq. 33.5.4 fails, the fit becomes genuine generalized least squares with Σi=XiDXi⊤+σ2I, and Henderson’s equations (Theorem 32.3.2) do the computing. Between-subject covariates: replace 𝛃β by Ξai as in the formulation Eq. 33.5.1. Serial correlation on top of the random coefficients: take R autoregressive, as in Section 33.4. Beyond all of these lies the question whether a polynomial in time is the right shape at all: Chapter 42 and Chapter 43.

33.5.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Take p=1, so X=1m and each subject has a single random level. Show that W reduces to σu2/(σu2+σ2/m) and that the predictor is the shrunken mean 𝛃𝛃β^+W(y¯i−β^). What happens as m→∞, and as σu2→0?

Solution. With X=1m, K=m, C=σ2/m and D=σu2, so W=σu2/(σu2+σ2/m) and bi=y¯i. As m→∞ the noise in y¯i vanishes, W→1 and no shrinkage occurs; as σu2→0, W→0 and every subject is given the common mean, correctly, since the subjects are then identical.

Exercise (A2)

The predicted curves are less spread out than the true ones. Is this a bias? Compute E(u^i) and Cov(u^i) and compare the latter with D.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Suppose the fixed effects use a quadratic in time, X=[1,t,t2], but the random effects only a line, Z=[1,t]. Show that C(ΣX)⊆C(X) still holds, and identify what changes in 𝛃Cov(β^). What if instead the random effects used the quadratic and the fixed effects only the line?

Exercise (B2)

Let subject i be observed at its own times, giving Xi of full column rank p, and let bi=(Xi⊤Xi)−1Xi⊤yi. Show that 𝛃bi∼(β,D+σ2(Xi⊤Xi)−1) independently over i, and deduce that the generalized least squares estimate from the bi is 𝛃β^=(∑iWi)−1∑iWibi with Wi={D+σ2(Xi⊤Xi)−1}−1, a precision-weighted average of the subjects’ own curves. Recover Eq. 33.5.4 as the balanced case.

Solution. 𝛃𝛆bi=β+ui+(Xi⊤Xi)−1Xi⊤εi is a sum of independent terms with covariances D and σ2(Xi⊤Xi)−1, and different subjects are independent. The bi are therefore independent with common mean 𝛃β and covariances Wi−1, so Corollary 7.2.3 applied to the stacked bi with design 1N⊗Ip gives the weighted average. When every Xi=X the weights cancel, leaving 𝛃β^=b¯ with covariance W1−1/N, which is Eq. 33.5.4.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Simulate data for which D^ of Eq. 33.5.7 is not nonnegative definite. Describe what the restricted maximum likelihood estimate does instead, relate it to the boundary problem of Proposition 32.5.1, and explain why the two-stage estimate is nevertheless unbiased for D.

33.3  Repeated measures

A repeated-measures study observes the same subject on several occasions: a clustered design whose cluster is the subject and whose “treatments” are the occasions, laid out like the split-plot design of Section 33.1, and for many years analysed that way.

The analogy has one weak joint. What makes the split-plot covariance compound symmetric is the randomization of subplot treatments within the whole plot, and occasions cannot be randomized: measurements close together in time are more alike than distant ones, so compound symmetry here is usually false. What is remarkable is that the univariate F test survives a much weaker condition, and that the condition can be written down exactly.

33.3.1. The univariate analysis

Let y1,…,yN be independent 𝛍Nm(μ,Σ) vectors, one per subject, with Σ positive definite and unknown, and write yij for occasion j of subject i. The hypothesis of interest is 𝛍H: μ1=⋯=μm,that is,μ∈span{1}.

Laid out with subjects as rows and occasions as columns the data have one observation per cell, and the classical analysis is the additive two-way analysis of variance of Section 16.1, with the subject-by-occasion interaction as error. With y¯i⋅, y¯⋅j and y¯ the subject, occasion and grand means,

SST=N∑j=1m(y¯⋅j−y¯)2,SSE=∑i=1N∑j=1m(yij−y¯i⋅−y¯⋅j+y¯)2,
(33.3.1)

and the statistic is F={SST/(m−1)}/{SSE/((N−1)(m−1))}. It is the subplot stratum of a split-plot design with one whole plot per subject, and under compound symmetry it is exact (Theorem 33.3.2(b) below, with Exercise (B1)). The question is how much of that exactness survives a general Σ.

Everything reduces to the within-subject contrasts. Let U be m×(m−1) with orthonormal columns orthogonal to 1, so that UU⊤=I−J¯m, and set zi=U⊤yi: independent 𝛍Nm−1(U⊤μ,Ψ) with Ψ=U⊤ΣU, with H saying exactly that 𝛍U⊤μ=0. The subject means carry no information about H.

33.3.2. Sphericity

Definition 33.3.1 (Sphericity and Box’s measure)

Let Σ be an m×m positive definite matrix and U as above. Σ satisfies sphericity if U⊤ΣU=λIm−1 for some λ>0. Box’s measure of sphericity is

ϵ(Σ)={trΨ}2(m−1)tr(Ψ2),Ψ=U⊤ΣU.
(33.3.2)

Neither the condition nor ϵ depends on the choice of U: two such matrices satisfy U~=UQ with Q orthogonal, so Ψ~=Q⊤ΨQ has the same trace, the same trace of square, and is a multiple of the identity exactly when Ψ is.

Lemma 33.3.1 (Equivalent forms of sphericity)

For a positive definite m×m matrix Σ the following are equivalent:

  • (i) U⊤ΣU=λIm−1;

  • (ii) Var(Yj−Yk)=2λ for every j≠k;

  • (iii) σjk=aj+ak+λδjk for some a1,…,am, where δjk is one if j=k and zero otherwise;

  • (iv) Σ=λI+1a⊤+a1⊤ for some a∈Rm.

Moreover 1/(m−1)≤ϵ(Σ)≤1, with ϵ=1 iff sphericity holds, and compound symmetry implies sphericity but not conversely.

Proof. Write C=I−J¯m=UU⊤. Since U⊤U=Im−1, condition (i) holds iff CΣC=λC.

  • (i) ⇒ (ii): ej−ek is orthogonal to 1, hence fixed by C, so Var(Yj−Yk) equals (ej−ek)⊤CΣC(ej−ek), which is λ‖ej−ek‖2=2λ.

  • (ii) ⇒ (iii): Var(Yj−Yk)=σjj+σkk−2σjk=2λ gives σjk=(σjj−λ)/2+(σkk−λ)/2 for j≠k. Put aj=(σjj−λ)/2; then σjk=aj+ak off the diagonal and σjj=2aj+λ on it, which is (iii).

  • (iii) ⇔ (iv) is the same statement in matrix form.

  • (iv) ⇒ (i): C1=0, so CΣC=λC.

For the bounds, let ψ1,…,ψm−1>0 be the eigenvalues of Ψ. Then ϵ=(∑lψl)2/{(m−1)∑lψl2}. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives (∑lψl)2≤(m−1)∑lψl2 with equality iff all ψl are equal, so ϵ≤1 with equality iff Ψ=λI; and ∑lψl2≤(∑lψl)2 for nonnegative numbers gives ϵ≥1/(m−1). Compound symmetry is (iv) with all aj equal; taking the aj unequal gives sphericity without compound symmetry, and such a Σ is positive definite as soon as λ is large enough.

Condition (ii) is the form to remember and to check: all pairwise differences of the repeated measurements have the same variance. Compound symmetry says more than the F test needs.

Theorem 33.3.2 (Sphericity is necessary and sufficient for the exact F test)

Let y1,…,yN be independent 𝛍Nm(μ,Σ) with Σ positive definite, m≥2, N≥2, and let F be the statistic built from Eq. 33.3.1. Put Ψ=U⊤ΣU, q=Nz¯ and W=∑i(zi−z¯)(zi−z¯)⊤.

  • (a) SST=‖q‖2 and SSE=tr(W), and under H the vector q∼Nm−1(0,Ψ) is independent of W∼Wm−1(N−1,Ψ). Hence F=N(N−1)‖z¯‖2/tr(W).

  • (b) If Σ satisfies sphericity, then under H F∼F(m−1,(N−1)(m−1)) exactly, for every N≥2.

  • (c) Conversely, if the null distribution of F is F(m−1,(N−1)(m−1)) for every N≥2, then Σ satisfies sphericity.

Proof.

  • (a) Since C=UU⊤ and U has orthonormal columns, ∑j(y¯⋅j−y¯)2=‖Cy¯‖2=‖z¯‖2, so SST=N‖z¯‖2=‖q‖2; likewise subject i’s contribution to SSE is ‖zi−z¯‖2, summing to tr(W). Under H the zi are independent Nm−1(0,Ψ), so Lemma 14.2.3 gives the Wishart law of W and its independence of z¯. Finally F={‖q‖2/(m−1)}{(N−1)(m−1)/trW}.

  • (b) If Ψ=λIm−1, then q/λ is standard normal, so ‖q‖2/λ∼χ2(m−1). And W/λ∼Wm−1(N−1,I), which is the matrix of sums of squares and cross products of (N−1)(m−1) independent standard normal variables, so tr(W)/λ∼χ2((N−1)(m−1)). The two are independent, and λ cancels from the ratio, leaving the ratio of two independent chi-squared variables each divided by its degrees of freedom (Definition 4.1.2).

  • (c) Write FN for the statistic with N subjects. By (a), FN=‖q‖2⋅N−1tr(W), where q∼Nm−1(0,Ψ) has the same distribution for every N and is independent of W. Since W/(N−1) is an average of N−1 independent Wm−1(1,Ψ) matrices, the weak law of large numbers gives tr(W)/(N−1)→trΨ in probability, so by Slutsky’s lemma FN→‖q‖2/trΨ in distribution. On the other hand F(m−1,ν)→χ2(m−1)/(m−1) in distribution as ν→∞, because its denominator chi-squared divided by ν tends to one in probability. If the exactness holds for every N, the two limits agree: ‖q‖2 =d trΨm−1χ2(m−1). Now match variances. By Theorem 4.2.3, Var(‖q‖2)=2tr(Ψ2), while the right-hand side has variance 2{trΨ}2/(m−1). Equality says (m−1)tr(Ψ2)={trΨ}2, that is ϵ(Σ)=1, and Lemma 33.3.1 makes that sphericity.

Idea. The repeated-measures F test does not require the occasions to be exchangeable, only that the m−1 orthonormalized within-subject contrasts be uncorrelated with equal variances — that the contrast space look spherical, which series of measurements in time almost never do.

33.3.3. Two corrections and one alternative

When sphericity fails, ‖q‖2 and tr(W) are still weighted sums of chi-squared variables, and matching their first two moments to scaled chi-squared variables gives a usable approximation (Box 1954).

Proposition 33.3.3 (The moment-matched approximation)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 33.3.2, with H true, write ϵ=ϵ(Σ), d1=m−1 and d2=(N−1)(m−1). Then

  • (a) ESST=trΨ, VarSST=2tr(Ψ2), ESSE=(N−1)trΨ and VarSSE=2(N−1)tr(Ψ2).

  • (b) The scaled chi-squared variable gχ2(h) matching the first two moments of SST has h=ϵd1 and g=tr(Ψ2)/trΨ; the one matching SSE has h′=ϵd2 and the same g. Since SST and SSE are independent by Theorem 33.3.2(a), replacing each by its matched scaled chi-squared gives the approximation F≈F(ϵd1,ϵd2), which is exact when ϵ=1.

Proof.

  • (a) The first two are Theorem 4.2.3 with A=I and mean zero. Writing W=∑l=1N−1wlwl⊤ with wl independent Nm−1(0,Ψ) (Lemma 14.2.3), trW=∑l‖wl‖2 is a sum of N−1 independent copies of ‖q‖2, so its mean and variance are N−1 times theirs.

  • (b) Matching gh=trΨ and 2g2h=2tr(Ψ2) gives g=tr(Ψ2)/trΨ and h={trΨ}2/tr(Ψ2)=ϵd1. For SSE both moments are multiplied by N−1, so g is unchanged and h′=(N−1)h=ϵd2. Since d2/d1=h′/h, the ratio (SST/d1)/(SSE/d2) becomes {χ2(h)/h}/{χ2(h′)/h′}, an F(h,h′) variable, the two chi-squared variables being independent by Theorem 33.3.2(a); at ϵ=1 this is the exact result of Theorem 33.3.2(b).

By Lemma 33.3.1, ϵ≥1/(m−1), so the approximating distribution is never more dispersed than F(d1/(m−1),d2/(m−1))=F(1,N−1), the largest that Proposition 33.3.3(b) can ask for. Referring F to F(1,N−1) is therefore conservative within Box’s approximation, and Geisser and Greenhouse (1958) proved it exactly conservative, for every Σ and N. This is the lower-bound correction, turned into a recommendation by Greenhouse and Geisser (1959).

Part (b) becomes a procedure once ϵ is estimated from the sample covariance matrix S of the yi.

  • The Greenhouse–Geisser correction refers F to F(ϵ^d1,ϵ^d2) with the plug-in ϵ^=ϵ(S), which is biased downward, so the test is conservative.

  • The Huynh–Feldt correction removes the leading bias. For a single group of N subjects it uses

    ϵ~=min{1, N(m−1)ϵ^−2(m−1){(N−1)−(m−1)ϵ^}},
    (33.3.3)

    which exceeds ϵ^ and gives a less conservative test; its own bias is upward when ϵ is small.

  • The lower-bound correction takes ϵ=1/(m−1): safe, and usually too conservative to be useful.

A third option avoids the issue. Since the zi are independent 𝛍Nm−1(U⊤μ,Ψ) with Ψ unrestricted, H says a multivariate normal mean is zero, and the classical test is Hotelling’s.

Proposition 33.3.4 (The multivariate test)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 33.3.2 with N≥m, let Sz=W/(N−1) and T2=Nz¯⊤Sz−1z¯. Then T2 does not depend on the choice of U, nor on replacing U by UK for any nonsingular (m−1)×(m−1) matrix K; and under H, N−m+1(N−1)(m−1)T2∼F(m−1,N−m+1), whatever Σ is. The distributional statement is Hotelling’s and is not proved here; see Anderson (2003, §5.2).

Proof. Only the invariance is proved. Replacing U by UK replaces z¯ by K⊤z¯ and Sz by K⊤SzK, leaving T2 unchanged; two orthonormal bases differ by an orthogonal K. The requirement N≥m makes Sz, a Wishart matrix on N−1≥m−1 degrees of freedom, invertible with probability one.

Exactness under any Σ sounds decisive, but it costs power: the multivariate test estimates a whole covariance matrix and has N−m+1 denominator degrees of freedom rather than (N−1)(m−1). When sphericity nearly holds the univariate test is much more powerful, when it fails badly the two are comparable, and when N<m the multivariate test does not exist.

33.3.4. An example, four ways

Example (Grip strength over six visits)

Twenty-four patients have grip strength measured, in kilograms, at six weekly visits, simulated from 𝛍N6(μ,Σ) with Σ first-order autoregressive, σjk=4×0.6|j−k|, and a mean drifting slowly upward; the observed occasion means are 47.818, 48.099, 49.056, 49.177, 49.561 and 49.263 kg. The true Box measure is ϵ=0.700: adjacent visits correlate 0.6 and visits five weeks apart only 0.65, so adjacent differences are much less variable than distant ones. The four analyses of H give

Analysis statistic degrees of freedom p
univariate, uncorrected 4.770 5, 115 0.0005
Greenhouse–Geisser, ϵ^=0.645 4.770 3.23, 74.2 0.0035
Huynh–Feldt, ϵ~=0.763 4.770 3.81, 87.7 0.0019
multivariate 3.605 5, 19 0.0184

All four reject, but the evidence differs by a factor of nearly forty between first and last. Distrust the uncorrected test: its nominal degrees of freedom are too large by the factor ϵ.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

N, m = 24, 6
mu_true = np.array([48.0, 48.5, 48.9, 49.2, 49.4, 49.5])   # kg, a slow improvement
sigma, rho_true = 2.0, 0.6
lags = np.abs(np.subtract.outer(np.arange(m), np.arange(m)))
Sigma_true = sigma**2 * rho_true**lags               # AR(1) within a patient
rng = np.random.default_rng(3303)
Yobs = np.round(mu_true + rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(m), Sigma_true, N), 2)

def contrast_basis(m):
    """Orthonormal basis U of the space of contrasts among m repeated measures."""
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(np.column_stack([np.ones(m), np.eye(m)[:, 1:]]))
    return Q[:, 1:]                                  # columns orthonormal and orthogonal to 1

def epsilon(Psi):
    """Box's measure of departure from sphericity, 1/(m-1) <= eps <= 1."""
    k = Psi.shape[0]
    return np.trace(Psi)**2 / (k * np.sum(Psi * Psi))

U = contrast_basis(m)
Z = Yobs @ U                                         # N x (m-1) contrasts, one row per patient
zbar = Z.mean(axis=0)
Sz = np.cov(Z, rowvar=False, ddof=1)                 # Wishart, (N-1) degrees of freedom

F_uncorrected = N * (zbar @ zbar) / np.trace(Sz)
eps_hat = epsilon(Sz)
eps_hf = min(1.0, (N * (m - 1) * eps_hat - 2)
             / ((m - 1) * ((N - 1) - (m - 1) * eps_hat)))
d1, d2 = m - 1, (N - 1) * (m - 1)
p_un = stats.f.sf(F_uncorrected, d1, d2)
p_gg = stats.f.sf(F_uncorrected, d1 * eps_hat, d2 * eps_hat)
p_hf = stats.f.sf(F_uncorrected, d1 * eps_hf, d2 * eps_hf)
T2 = N * zbar @ np.linalg.solve(Sz, zbar)            # Hotelling's statistic
F_mv = (N - m + 1) / ((N - 1) * (m - 1)) * T2
p_mv = stats.f.sf(F_mv, m - 1, N - m + 1)
print(f"F = {F_uncorrected:.3f} on ({d1}, {d2}) df,  p = {p_un:.4f}")
print(f"Greenhouse-Geisser eps = {eps_hat:.3f}, p = {p_gg:.4f}")
print(f"Huynh-Feldt eps = {eps_hf:.3f}, p = {p_hf:.4f}")
print(f"multivariate F({m - 1}, {N - m + 1}) = {F_mv:.3f}, p = {p_mv:.4f}")

How much does the failure of sphericity cost? Figure 33.3.1 answers with a simulation under H. Panel (a) shows the null distribution of the uncorrected F: more dispersed than F(5,115), with a heavier right tail, and tracking Proposition 33.3.3 closely. Panel (b) traces the level of a nominal 5% test as the autoregressive parameter grows. At ρ=0 it is 0.050, as Theorem 33.3.2(b) requires; by ρ=0.8, where ϵ=0.562, it is 0.085. The Greenhouse–Geisser test is conservative throughout (0.038 to 0.049), the Huynh–Feldt test close to nominal (0.049 to 0.056), and the multivariate test exact at 0.049 for every ρ. A spherical covariance of the form Lemma 33.3.1(iv) with unequal aj, far from compound symmetric, gives the uncorrected test a level of 0.050: sphericity, not compound symmetry, is what matters.

33.3.5. The modern treatment

The corrections above patch a fixed analysis; the mixed-model approach fits the covariance instead. Write the data as a linear mixed model (Definition 32.2.1) with a fixed effect for each occasion and a within-subject covariance R of chosen shape, estimate its parameters by restricted maximum likelihood (Definition 32.4.1), and test the fixed effects by a Wald statistic with estimated degrees of freedom (Proposition 32.5.1). Compound symmetric R reproduces the uncorrected univariate F (Exercise (B2)), unstructured R the multivariate test up to the degrees-of-freedom approximation, and anything between is a model the classical scheme has no room for. Unbalanced data and missing visits are handled too, whereas Eq. 33.3.1 and T2 need a complete rectangle. The price is that the covariance must be chosen, and the choice affects the standard errors: Section 33.4.

Remark (Between-subjects factors). With a between-subjects factor the within-subject analysis is the same, applied to the pooled within-group covariance, and the univariate tests are exact iff the pooled Ψ is spherical and the groups share a common Σ. Mauchly’s (1940) test of sphericity itself, which most software reports, is sensitive to non-normality and, like any preliminary test, distorts the level of what follows.

33.3.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that every 2×2 covariance matrix satisfies sphericity, so that with m=2 the repeated-measures F test is exact whatever Σ is. Identify the test in that case.

Solution. With m=2, Ψ=U⊤ΣU is 1×1, hence a multiple of I1. By Theorem 33.3.2(b) the test is exact. It is the paired t test: the single contrast is (yi1−yi2)/2, and F is the square of the paired t statistic on N−1 degrees of freedom.

Exercise (A2)

For m=3 and an autoregressive covariance σjk=ρ|j−k| with 0<ρ<1, compute Ψ for the basis u1=(1,−1,0)⊤/2, u2=(1,1,−2)⊤/6 and show that sphericity fails. Which pairwise difference has the largest variance?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show directly that compound symmetry Σ=σ2{(1−ρ)I+ρ11⊤} gives Ψ=σ2(1−ρ)Im−1, and hence that the split-plot analysis of Theorem 33.1.2 is the repeated-measures F test with ϵ=1. Which λ of Lemma 33.3.1 does it correspond to?

Solution. U⊤1=0, so Ψ=U⊤ΣU=σ2(1−ρ)U⊤U=σ2(1−ρ)Im−1. Hence ϵ=1 and λ=σ2(1−ρ), which is the subplot stratum eigenvalue λs of Lemma 33.1.1. In Lemma 33.3.1(iv) take a=(σ2ρ/2)1.

Exercise (B2)

Consider the mixed model yij=μj+ui+eij with ui and eij independent normal, of variances σu2 and σe2. Show that restricted maximum likelihood gives σ^e2=MSE and σ^u2=(MSS−MSE)/m when this is nonnegative, where MSS is the between-subject mean square, and that the Wald statistic for H:μ1=⋯=μm, divided by m−1, is the uncorrected F of Eq. 33.3.1.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

For m=4, N=20 and Σ compound symmetric, compare by simulation the power of the uncorrected univariate test, the Greenhouse–Geisser test and the multivariate test of Proposition 33.3.4 against a linear trend in the occasion means. Repeat with an autoregressive Σ of the same total variance. Which would you choose if you knew the alternative were a linear trend, and why is a single contrast better still?

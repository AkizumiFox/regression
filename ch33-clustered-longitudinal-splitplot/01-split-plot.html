33.1  Split-plot designs

Some treatments cannot be applied to small units: an irrigation rig waters a strip of field, not a square metre of it; a teaching method is delivered to a class, not to a pupil. An experiment combining such a treatment with one applied as finely as one likes gives the coarse treatment to large units and the fine treatment to subdivisions of them — a split-plot design, named after the field trials it came from. Two independent randomizations mean two error terms, so that an analysis ignoring the distinction gets both wrong, in opposite directions.

33.1.1. The design and its randomization

Take r blocks. Each is divided into a whole plots, carrying the a levels of a factor A in random order: a randomized complete block design (Definition 18.4.1). Each whole plot is then divided into b subplots, carrying the b levels of a factor C in random order. The two randomizations are independent, and the second is repeated separately inside every whole plot. Figure 33.1.1 shows one such layout.

Two things vary from subplot to subplot: whatever makes whole plots differ, shared by all b subplots of a whole plot, and whatever makes subplots differ within a whole plot, which is not. So the model carries two error terms.

Definition 33.1.1 (The balanced split-plot model)

Index blocks by i=1,…,r, whole-plot treatments by j=1,…,a and subplot treatments by k=1,…,b, and write yijk for the response on the subplot of block i receiving whole-plot treatment j and subplot treatment k. The split-plot model is

yijk=μ+ξi+ωj+ηij+τk+γjk+eijk,
(33.1.1)

where ξi, ωj, τk and γjk are fixed effects, the ηij are uncorrelated whole-plot errors with mean zero and variance σw2, the eijk are uncorrelated subplot errors with mean zero and variance σs2, and the two sets are uncorrelated with each other. The normal split-plot model takes both sets to be normal.

The whole plot (i,j) is a cluster of b observations that share ηij. Writing Zw for the n×ra matrix of whole-plot indicators, with n=rab, the combined error ηij+eijk has

Cov(Y)=V=σs2I+σw2ZwZw⊤=σs2I+bσw2PW,
(33.1.2)

where PW=b−1ZwZw⊤ replaces every observation by its whole-plot mean (Example (The one-way layout)). Every pair of subplots in a whole plot has correlation ρ=σw2/(σw2+σs2), pairs in different whole plots none. The next section studies this structure in general; here balance makes everything explicit.

Where does the equicorrelation come from? In a randomized experiment, random assignment plus unit–treatment additivity produces the model rather than assuming it (Section 15.1), and here the argument runs twice: the second randomization is performed anew in every whole plot, making the subplots of a whole plot exchangeable — exactly the covariance Eq. 33.1.2. When the subplot factor is not randomized, as it is not when that factor is time (Section 33.3), the justification disappears and with it the equicorrelation.

33.1.2. Two strata

Order the observations with k fastest, then j, then i, and use the averaging and centring matrices J¯l, Cl of Section 16.1. Define

P0=J¯r⊗J¯a⊗J¯b,PB=Cr⊗J¯a⊗J¯b,PA=J¯r⊗Ca⊗J¯b,PAB=Cr⊗Ca⊗J¯b,PC=J¯r⊗J¯a⊗Cb,PAC=J¯r⊗Ca⊗Cb,
(33.1.3)

and PE=Cr⊗Ia⊗Cb, the terms of the balanced three-way layout with one observation per cell (Theorem 16.3.1): PAB is the block-by-A interaction, and PE collects the block-by-C and three-factor interactions. The split-plot analysis rests on the fact that these seven projections fall into two groups according to how V acts on them.

Lemma 33.1.1 (The two error strata)

Let V be as in Eq. 33.1.2 and put λw=σs2+bσw2 and λs=σs2. Then:

  • (a) PW=Ir⊗Ia⊗J¯b, and V=λwPW+λs(I−PW), so V has the two eigenvalues λw and λs with eigenspaces C(PW) and C(PW)⊥, of dimensions ra and ra(b−1).

  • (b) The six matrices of Eq. 33.1.3 together with PE are mutually orthogonal projections summing to I, with ranks 1, r−1, a−1, (r−1)(a−1), b−1, (a−1)(b−1) and (r−1)a(b−1).

  • (c) P0,PB,PA,PAB have their ranges inside C(PW), and PC,PAC,PE have their ranges inside C(PW)⊥. Hence VP=λwP for the first four and VP=λsP for the last three.

Proof.

  • (a) The whole plot (i,j) consists of the b observations with those first two indices, so averaging within whole plots is Ir⊗Ia⊗J¯b, which is symmetric and idempotent of rank ra by Lemma 16.1.1(b). Substituting PW in Eq. 33.1.2 gives V=σs2(I−PW)+(σs2+bσw2)PW.

  • (b) Ranks, symmetry and idempotence are Lemma 16.1.1(b), noting PE=Cr⊗J¯a⊗Cb+Cr⊗Ca⊗Cb. Distinct terms differ in at least one slot, so they are orthogonal by Lemma 16.1.1(c), and they are the 23 products of Lemma 16.1.1(d) regrouped, so they sum to I.

  • (c) A product E1⊗E2⊗J¯b satisfies PWP=P by the mixed-product rule and J¯bJ¯b=J¯b; a product E1⊗E2⊗Cb satisfies PWP=0 because J¯bCb=0. The first four matrices are of the first kind and the last three of the second. Then VP=λwPWP+λs(P−PWP) gives the two cases.

Part (c) is the whole idea. The whole-plot stratum C(PW) carries the whole-plot error intact, so its variance is λw; in the subplot stratum C(PW)⊥ that error is differenced away and only σs2 remains.

Idea. A split-plot design is two experiments sharing one field: a whole-plot experiment with ra units and error variance σs2+bσw2, and a subplot experiment with rab units and error variance σs2. Effects constant within whole plots belong to the first and effects averaging to zero within every whole plot to the second — the interaction among them, which is why such a design studies an interaction well and the coarse main effect badly.

33.1.3. The analysis

Theorem 33.1.2 (The balanced split-plot design)

Assume Eq. 33.1.1 with r,a,b≥2, and write the cell means μijk=E(yijk). Let M be the orthogonal projection onto the model space C(X)=C(P0+PB+PA+PC+PAC).

  • (a) C(VX)⊆C(X), so least squares is generalized least squares and every least squares estimate of an estimable function is its best linear unbiased estimator, whatever σw2 and σs2 are.

  • (b) The sums of squares are those of the balanced three-way layout, with SSA=rb∑j(y¯⋅j⋅−y¯⋅⋅⋅)2, SSC=ra∑k(y¯⋅⋅k−y¯⋅⋅⋅)2, SSAC=r∑j,k(y¯⋅jk−y¯⋅j⋅−y¯⋅⋅k+y¯⋅⋅⋅)2, and the two error sums of squares SSAB=‖PABy‖2 and SSE=‖PEy‖2.

  • (c) The expected mean squares (Theorem 9.6.1) are

    EMSB=λw+abr−1∑i(ξi−ξ¯)2,EMSAB=λw=σs2+bσw2,EMSA=λw+rba−1∑j(αj−α¯)2,EMSE=λs=σs2,EMSC=λs+rab−1∑k(κk−κ¯)2,EMSAC=λs+r(a−1)(b−1)∑j,kδjk2,
    (33.1.4)

    where αj=ωj+γ¯j⋅, κk=τk+γ¯⋅k and δjk=γjk−γ¯j⋅−γ¯⋅k+γ¯ are the equal-weight effects of Definition 16.2.1 computed from μ¯⋅jk.

  • (d) Under normality the six sums of squares are independent, SSAB/λw∼χ2((r−1)(a−1)), SSE/λs∼χ2((r−1)a(b−1)), and MSAMSAB∼F(a−1,(r−1)(a−1),rbλw∑j(αj−α¯)2), while MSC/MSE and MSAC/MSE have noncentral F distributions on (b−1,(r−1)a(b−1)) and ((a−1)(b−1),(r−1)a(b−1)) degrees of freedom with noncentralities ra∑k(κk−κ¯)2/λs and r∑j,kδjk2/λs. So the whole-plot factor is tested against MSAB and the subplot factor and the interaction against MSE.

  • (e) A contrast ∑jcjαj is estimated by ∑jcjy¯⋅j⋅ with variance λw∑jcj2/(rb); a contrast ∑kdkκk is estimated by ∑kdky¯⋅⋅k with variance λs∑kdk2/(ra). Both are best linear unbiased, and the two estimators are uncorrelated.

  • (f) Fix a subplot treatment k and a contrast ∑jcj=0. The simple effect ∑jcjμ¯⋅jk is estimated by ∑jcjy¯⋅jk, best linear unbiased, with variance

    (∑jcj2)λw+(b−1)λsrb=(∑jcj2)σw2+σs2r,
    (33.1.5)

    which belongs to neither stratum: no single mean square estimates it, and the unbiased estimate is the combination (∑jcj2){MSAB+(b−1)MSE}/(rb).

Proof.

  • (a) Every v∈C(X) splits as v=v1+v2 with v1∈C(P0+PB+PA) and v2∈C(PC+PAC), because the five projections are mutually orthogonal and sum to M. By Lemma 33.1.1(c), Vv=λwv1+λsv2, again in C(X). So C(VX)⊆C(X), and Theorem 6.9.3 says the ordinary and the V−1 projection onto C(X) coincide. The estimator is therefore both the least squares and the generalized least squares estimator, which is the best linear unbiased estimator by Corollary 7.2.3. (This is the balanced case of Theorem 31.2.2.)

  • (b) Each projection averages over the slots carrying J¯ and centres in the slots carrying C. For instance PA=J¯r⊗Ia⊗J¯b−J¯r⊗J¯a⊗J¯b puts y¯⋅j⋅−y¯⋅⋅⋅ in position (i,j,k), a value repeated rb times, giving SSA; the same expansion gives SSC and SSAC, as in Theorem 16.3.1(b). The error sums of squares are the squared lengths of the remaining components.

  • (c) Each P in the list satisfies VP=λP with λ the eigenvalue of its stratum, so 𝛍𝛍𝛍E(Y⊤PY)=tr(PV)+μ⊤Pμ=λrank(P)+‖Pμ‖2 by Theorem 2.3.1, where 𝛍μ is the mean vector. Dividing by the rank gives 𝛍E(MS)=λ+‖Pμ‖2/rank(P). It remains to evaluate 𝛍Pμ. Since 𝛍μ has entries μ+ξi+ωj+τk+γjk, the projection PA returns μ¯⋅j⋅−μ¯⋅⋅⋅=αj−α¯ on each of the rb observations with whole-plot treatment j; likewise PC returns κk−κ¯ on each of ra observations, PAC returns δjk on each of r, and PB returns ξi−ξ¯ on each of ab. Finally 𝛍PABμ=0 and 𝛍PEμ=0, because 𝛍μ has no block-by-treatment terms.

  • (d) Write V1/2=λw1/2PW+λs1/2(I−PW), a symmetric positive definite square root (Theorem 1.7.6), and put 𝛍z=V−1/2Y∼Nn(V−1/2μ,I) (Proposition 2.2.4). For any P in the list, Lemma 33.1.1(c) gives V1/2PV1/2=λP, so Y⊤PY=λz⊤Pz. The projections are mutually orthogonal, so Theorem 4.4.4 applied to z makes the quadratic forms z⊤Pz independent with noncentral chi-squared distributions on rank(P) degrees of freedom and noncentrality 𝛍𝛍‖PV−1/2μ‖2=‖Pμ‖2/λ, the last equality because V−1/2 acts on C(P) as multiplication by λ−1/2. Forming ratios and cancelling λ between numerator and denominator within the same stratum gives the stated F distributions (Definition 4.1.2). Ratios across strata would not cancel, which is precisely why MSA/MSE is not an F statistic.

  • (e) The coefficient vector of ∑jcjy¯⋅j⋅ is 𝛒ρ=(rb)−11r⊗c⊗1b∈C(PA), since c⊥1a, so by Lemma 33.1.1(c) 𝛒𝛒Var(ρ⊤Y)=λw‖ρ‖2=λw∑jcj2/(rb). The same computation with 𝛒ρC=(ra)−11r⊗1a⊗d∈C(PC) gives the second variance, and the two are uncorrelated because 𝛒𝛒ρ⊤ρC=0. Both are least squares estimates of estimable functions, hence best linear unbiased by (a).

  • (f) Split ek=b−11b+(ek−b−11b). The coefficient vector r−11r⊗c⊗ek splits accordingly into 𝛒ρ1∈C(PA) and 𝛒ρ2∈C(PAC), of squared lengths ∑jcj2/(rb) and (b−1)∑jcj2/(rb). They lie in different strata and are orthogonal, so the variance is 𝛒𝛒λw‖ρ1‖2+λs‖ρ2‖2, which is Eq. 33.1.5; the estimate of it is unbiased by Eq. 33.1.4, and the estimator is best linear unbiased by (a).

Dropping (ξω)ij from a full three-way model is what turns the block-by-A interaction into the error term for A, on only (r−1)(a−1) degrees of freedom: the formal content of “whole plots are the experimental units for A”. And MSA tests a contrast in αj=ωj+γ¯j⋅, the whole-plot effect averaged over the subplot treatments used, not in ωj alone (Section 16.2).

Part (f) is the case most often got wrong. “The interaction is tested against MSE” tempts one to compare two whole-plot treatments within a single subplot treatment against MSE too; but that comparison carries the whole-plot error intact, and Eq. 33.1.5 prices it between the two strata. Its estimated variance combines two mean squares, so no exact t or F test exists, and Satterthwaite’s rule (Proposition 32.5.1) gives it

ν={MSAB+(b−1)MSE}2MSAB2/{(r−1)(a−1)}+(b−1)2MSE2/{(r−1)a(b−1)}
(33.1.6)

approximate degrees of freedom, which lie between the two exact values.

Example (An irrigation and variety trial)

Four fields are each divided into three whole plots, and the regimes flood, sprinkler and drip are assigned at random within each field; each whole plot is divided into four subplots carrying four barley varieties in random order, giving n=48 yields in tonnes per hectare, simulated from Eq. 33.1.1 with σw=0.35 and σs=0.25 t/ha. The analysis of variance is

Source df SS MS F p
blocks 3 9.471 3.157
irrigation A 2 9.215 4.608 10.637 0.0106
whole-plot error 6 2.599 0.433
variety C 3 1.755 0.585 9.442 0.0002

The two error mean squares differ by a factor of seven. Solving Eq. 33.1.4 gives σ^s=MSE=0.2489 and σ^w=(MSAB−MSE)/b=0.3046, close to the generating values.

The irrigation means are 4.8075, 5.8000 and 5.6575 t/ha; a difference of two has standard error 2MSAB/(rb)=0.2327 on 6 degrees of freedom, so drip beats flood by 0.8500 t/ha with t=3.653. The variety means are 5.134, 5.364, 5.574 and 5.614, and a difference of two has standard error 2MSE/(ra)=0.1016 on 27 degrees of freedom — more than twice as precise, from the same 48 observations. Comparing drip with flood for one variety is neither: by Eq. 33.1.5 its standard error is {MSAB+3MSE}/8=0.2782, on 11.77 Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.

import numpy as np

r, a, b = 4, 3, 4                                   # blocks, irrigation regimes, varieties
n = r * a * b
block_eff = np.array([-0.30, 0.10, 0.05, 0.15])     # field-to-field fertility
irrig_eff = np.array([0.00, 0.45, 0.70])            # flood, sprinkler, drip
variety_eff = np.array([-0.30, -0.10, 0.15, 0.25])
inter = np.array([[0.10, -0.05, -0.10, 0.05],       # irrigation x variety
                  [-0.05, 0.10, 0.00, -0.05],
                  [-0.05, -0.05, 0.10, 0.00]])
sigma_w, sigma_s = 0.35, 0.25                       # whole-plot and subplot errors

rng = np.random.default_rng(3301)
eta = rng.normal(0, sigma_w, (r, a))                # one error per whole plot
e = rng.normal(0, sigma_s, (r, a, b))               # one error per subplot
mean = (5.0 + block_eff[:, None, None] + irrig_eff[None, :, None]
        + variety_eff[None, None, :] + inter[None, :, :])
Y = np.round(mean + eta[:, :, None] + e, 2)         # yields, t/ha
y = Y.reshape(-1)                                   # stacked block, then regime, then variety

The analysis is seven inner products, each projection a Kronecker product as in Section 16.3, with two denominators in place of one.

from scipy import stats

def Jbar(k):
    return np.full((k, k), 1.0 / k)

def Cen(k):
    return np.eye(k) - Jbar(k)

def kron3(E1, E2, E3):
    return np.kron(np.kron(E1, E2), E3)

P = {"blocks": kron3(Cen(r), Jbar(a), Jbar(b)),
     "irrigation": kron3(Jbar(r), Cen(a), Jbar(b)),
     "whole-plot error": kron3(Cen(r), Cen(a), Jbar(b)),
     "varieties": kron3(Jbar(r), Jbar(a), Cen(b)),
     "irrigation x variety": kron3(Jbar(r), Cen(a), Cen(b)),
     "subplot error": kron3(Cen(r), np.eye(a), Cen(b))}
df = {k: int(round(np.trace(M))) for k, M in P.items()}
SS = {k: y @ M @ y for k, M in P.items()}
MS = {k: SS[k] / df[k] for k in P}

F_irrigation = MS["irrigation"] / MS["whole-plot error"]
F_variety = MS["varieties"] / MS["subplot error"]
F_inter = MS["irrigation x variety"] / MS["subplot error"]
for name, F, d in [("irrigation", F_irrigation, df["whole-plot error"]),
                   ("varieties", F_variety, df["subplot error"]),
                   ("irrigation x variety", F_inter, df["subplot error"])]:
    p = stats.f.sf(F, df[name], d)
    print(f"{name:22s} F({df[name]},{d}) = {F:7.3f}   p = {p:.4f}")

33.1.4. What pooling costs

Pool the two error sums of squares into one residual mean square on 33 degrees of freedom — in Example (An irrigation and variety trial), 0.129 — and by Eq. 33.1.4 the consequences are opposite in the two strata. The whole-plot denominator falls towards λs, inflating the irrigation F to 35.591 on (2,33) degrees of freedom, p=5.78×10−9 against 0.0106, and shrinking the standard error of an irrigation difference from 0.2327 to 0.1272, a factor of 1.829 too small; the subplot denominator exceeds λs, and that of a variety difference rises from 0.1016 to 0.1469, a factor of 1.445 too large. Pooling manufactures significance for the treatment the design studies badly and destroys it for the one it studies well.

Warning. The two error terms are a property of the randomization, not of the data. A split-plot experiment analysed as a completely randomized three-factor experiment produces a plausible table with one residual line, and nothing in the numbers announces the mistake. Wherever one factor is applied to groups of units and another within them, look for two errors.

33.1.5. The same analysis as a mixed model

Eq. 33.1.1 is a linear mixed model (Definition 32.2.1) with Z=Zw, G=σw2Ira and R=σs2In, whose marginal covariance ZGZ⊤+R is Eq. 33.1.2. In the balanced case, fitting it by restricted maximum likelihood (Definition 32.4.1) reproduces the analysis above exactly, provided MSAB≥MSE: the restricted likelihood depends on the data only through the two mean squares, and is maximized at the moment estimates of Example (An irrigation and variety trial) (Exercise (B2)). Satterthwaite’s rule (Proposition 32.5.1) then returns exactly (r−1)(a−1) degrees of freedom for a whole-plot contrast, and Eq. 33.1.6 for a simple effect.

The route earns its keep when the balance fails: a missing subplot, a whole plot lost to flooding, a covariate measured at the subplot level. The strata are then no longer orthogonal, no mean square estimates λw or λs on its own, and Henderson’s equations (Theorem 32.3.2) do the computing. Two variations change nothing essential: the whole-plot design may be completely randomized or a Latin square (Definition 18.5.1), which changes only the whole-plot degrees of freedom, and splitting can be repeated, giving three strata (Exercise (C1)).

33.1.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For the design of Example (An irrigation and variety trial), say which stratum each of the following belongs to, and on how many degrees of freedom its error is estimated: the difference between two block means; between two variety means; between two irrigation means; between two varieties within the drip regime; the difference in the effect of drip versus flood between two varieties; and the difference between drip and flood for the first variety alone.

Solution. The block and irrigation differences are constant within whole plots, so they lie in the whole-plot stratum and use MSAB on 6 degrees of freedom. The next three are contrasts summing to zero within each whole plot, so they lie in the subplot stratum and use MSE on 27 degrees of freedom. The last lies in neither: it is Theorem 33.1.2(f), with estimated variance {MSAB+3MSE}/8 on about 11.8 Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.

Exercise (A2)

With r, a, σw2 and σs2 fixed, what happens to the variance of a whole-plot contrast, and to that of a subplot contrast, as b grows? Which kind of comparison does adding subplots help?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Verify Theorem 33.1.2(a) directly from Theorem 6.9.3 by computing VX for the model matrix X=[1,B,A,C,AC] of indicator columns. Then show that adding the block-by-A columns leaves the condition true but destroys the analysis, and say why.

Exercise (B2)

Show that for the balanced split-plot model the restricted likelihood of (σw2,σs2) depends on the data only through SSAB and SSE, and that it is maximized at λ^w=MSAB, λ^s=MSE provided MSAB≥MSE. Hint: the error contrasts span C(PAB)⊕C(PE), and V acts as a scalar on each.

Solution. The error contrasts are the a⊤y with a∈C(X)⊥=C(PAB)⊕C(PE). Take orthonormal bases of the two summands, of sizes fw=(r−1)(a−1) and fs=(r−1)a(b−1). By Lemma 33.1.1(c) the transformed data are independent, N(0,λw) for the first basis and N(0,λs) for the second. By Definition 32.4.1 the restricted log likelihood is therefore −12{fwlog⁡λw+SSAB/λw+fslog⁡λs+SSE/λs} up to a constant, a sum of two ordinary normal-variance likelihoods maximized at the two mean squares. Since σs2=λs and σw2=(λw−λs)/b, the constraint σw2≥0 is MSAB≥MSE.

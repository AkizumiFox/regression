33.4  Covariance models for longitudinal data

A longitudinal study follows subjects over time. Its data are clustered by subject, but with a structure the previous sections have not used: observations inside a cluster are ordered, and the distance between two of them matters. Compound symmetry ignores the ordering; an unstructured covariance uses it but pays m(m+1)/2 parameters. Between the extremes lies a catalogue of models with two to four parameters, each a different story about where the correlation comes from.

33.4.1. Three sources of correlation

A longitudinal measurement is usefully read as the sum of three parts, a decomposition due to Diggle (1988):

𝛃yij=x(ij)⊤β+z(ij)⊤ui+Wi(tij)+eij.
(33.4.1)

The vector ui holds random effects: a subject’s own level or slope, anything persisting for the whole study. The process Wi(⋅) is serial correlation, making nearby times alike and distant times nearly independent. And eij is measurement error. The three leave different fingerprints — correlation that does not decay with the gap, correlation that does, and a discontinuity at gap zero — and most models in use are Eq. 33.4.1 with one or two parts dropped.

Definition 33.4.1 (Covariance models for a subject’s observations)

Let subject i be observed at times ti1<⋯<timi, and write Σi for the covariance matrix of its response vector, with entries σjk and time gap djk=|tij−tik|. The standard models are

Model σjk Parameters
independence σ2δjk 1
compound symmetry σu2+σ2δjk 2
first-order autoregressive σ2ρ|j−k| 2
exponential (spatial power) σ2ρdjk 2
autoregressive plus random intercept σu2+σ2ρ|j−k| 3
Toeplitz of order q σ2c|j−k|, with c0=1 and cu=0 for u≥q q
random intercept and slope (1,tij)D(1,tik)⊤+σ2δjk 4
unstructured σjk, unrestricted m(m+1)/2

Each model must also be nonnegative definite; the constraint is σu2,σ2≥0 and |ρ|<1 for the first five, D nonnegative definite for the random-coefficient model, and a genuine restriction on (c1,…,cq−1) for the Toeplitz model (Exercise (B2)).

Compound symmetry is Definition 33.2.1, what a random intercept alone produces: it makes the correlation between the first and last visit equal to that between consecutive visits, which over five years it rarely is. The autoregressive model is Definition 21.6.1 within a subject and needs equally spaced occasions; the exponential model is its continuous-time version. A random intercept added to either puts a floor under the decaying correlation, and that two-source version of Eq. 33.4.1 fits much real longitudinal data. The Toeplitz model lets the decay take an arbitrary shape, truncated after q−1 lags. Unstructured assumes nothing, and is right when the occasions are few, common and complete and the subjects comfortably outnumber m(m+1)/2: the covariance implicit in Proposition 33.3.4.

Warning. The covariance model is a choice with consequences. It changes the standard errors of the fixed effects, and — unlike in the balanced cases of the previous sections — the estimates themselves whenever the design is unbalanced or the regressors vary within subjects.

33.4.2. Choosing, and what goes wrong if you choose badly

Proposition 33.4.1 (Working covariances, likelihood comparisons and empirical standard errors)

Let the N subjects be independent, with 𝛃E(Yi)=Xiβ and true covariance Σi0. Let 𝛉Σi(θ) be a working covariance model whose eigenvalues are bounded above and away from zero uniformly in i and, for fixed 𝛉θ, let 𝛃𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉β~(θ)=A(θ)−1∑iXi⊤Σi(θ)−1Yi,A(θ)=∑iXi⊤Σi(θ)−1Xi, assumed nonsingular.

  • (a) 𝛃𝛉β~(θ) is unbiased for every 𝛉θ, whether or not the working model is correct, with exact covariance A−1BA−1 where B=∑iXi⊤Σi−1Σi0Σi−1Xi. If the working model is correct, B=A and the covariance is A−1.

  • (b) The empirical covariance estimate U^E replaces Σi0 by riri⊤, with 𝛃ri=Yi−Xiβ~. Under the conditions (K1)–(K3) of Section 33.2, applied with 𝛉Σi(θ)−1Xi in place of Xi, it satisfies n(U^E−A−1BA−1)→0 in probability as N→∞, whatever Σi0 is.

  • (c) Let K be any n×(n−p) matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis of C(X)⊥. The restricted log likelihood of a covariance model is the log likelihood of K⊤Y (Definition 32.4.1) and does not depend on the choice of K (Theorem 32.4.2). Hence two covariance models fitted with the same X have restricted likelihoods that are densities of one and the same random vector, and their difference is a genuine log likelihood ratio. If the models differ in X, the two are densities of different random vectors and cannot be compared.

Proof.

  • (a) 𝛃β~=A−1∑iXi⊤Σi−1Yi is a fixed linear function of Y, so 𝛃𝛃𝛃Eβ~=A−1∑iXi⊤Σi−1Xiβ=β and, by independence of the subjects, 𝛃Cov(β~)=A−1{∑iXi⊤Σi−1Σi0Σi−1Xi}A−1 by Theorem 2.2.1. If Σi0=Σi, the middle sum telescopes to A.

  • (b) Put X~i=Σi−1Xi, so that 𝛃𝛃𝛆β~−β=(∑iX~i⊤Xi)−1∑iX~i⊤εi and the empirical estimate is the same expression with riri⊤ in the middle. The cluster-sandwich argument of Section 33.2 — that is, the proof of Theorem 21.4.2 with the cluster in place of the observation — goes through with X~g in the meat and Xg in the bread: the assumed bounds on the eigenvalues of Σi make ‖X~i‖ satisfy (K2) whenever ‖Xi‖ does, and (K1) and (K3) are unchanged.

  • (c) The first two sentences are Definition 32.4.1 and Theorem 32.4.2, and K is determined up to an orthogonal rotation by C(X). Two models with the same X therefore attach densities to the same (n−p)-dimensional random vector, so their ratio is a likelihood ratio; two models with different X attach them to different random vectors, and the ratio is not even dimensionless when the two X have different ranks.

Part (c) has a practical corollary: fix the mean model first and compare covariance models by restricted likelihood; compare mean models by ordinary likelihood, or by a Wald or F test, holding the covariance fixed. The criteria of Chapter 29 then apply to the restricted likelihood as to any likelihood (Definition 29.2.2):

AICR=−2ℓR+2q,BICR=−2ℓR+qlog⁡(n−p),
(33.4.2)

with q the number of covariance parameters and n−p the number of error contrasts, the dimension of the data whose likelihood ℓR is.

Part (a) is why all this is less dangerous than it sounds: a wrong covariance model costs efficiency, not validity, since 𝛃β stays unbiased and part (b) supplies a standard error that is right anyway — the longitudinal version of the sandwich argument of Section 21.4, and the basis of the generalized estimating equations (Definition 40.4.1) of Chapter 40.

Warning. The empirical standard error protects against the covariance model and nothing else. If 𝛃E(Yi)≠Xiβ, part (a) fails at the first line and there is nothing left to be robust about; it does not protect against missing data that depend on the unobserved responses; and it needs N large in the sense of (K1)–(K3), being worse than the model-based estimate with a dozen subjects.

33.4.3. Checking the fit of a covariance model

Two displays do most of the work, both built from residuals 𝛃r^ij=yij−x(ij)⊤β^ after a fit of the mean model. The empirical variogram plots, against the time gap u, the average of 12(r^ij−r^ik)2 over same-subject pairs at that gap. Its population version γ(u)=12Var(rij−rik) is, for Eq. 33.4.1, σe2+σs2{1−ρ(u)}, rising from σe2 to σe2+σs2; the gap between that plateau and the total residual variance σu2+σs2+σe2 measures σu2, the part of the correlation that never decays. The residual correlogram plots the average correlation of residuals at gap u: compound symmetry predicts a horizontal line, the autoregressive model a geometric decay to zero, the combination a decay to a positive floor. Comparing empirical with fitted curves shows quickly which model does violence to the data.

Example (Investment in eleven firms)

Grunfeld’s panel records gross investment, market value and capital stock for 11 US firms in each of the 20 years from 1935 to 1954, a complete rectangle with n=220. Take log investment as the response and the mean model E(y)=β0+β1t+β2log⁡(value), with t in decades from the middle of the record, so p=3. An unstructured covariance would need 210 parameters from 11 subjects, which is hopeless. Fitting the alternatives by restricted maximum likelihood gives

Covariance model q −2ℓR AICR BICR
independence 1 476.95 478.95 482.33
compound symmetry 2 114.83 118.83 125.59
autoregressive 2 49.89 53.89 60.65
autoregressive + random intercept 3 29.87 35.87 46.01
random intercept and slope 4 98.66 106.66 120.18
Toeplitz of order 4 4 147.97 155.97 169.49

Independence is hopeless: the firms differ enormously in size, and log investment is nearly constant within a firm relative to the spread between firms. Compound symmetry captures that and improves −2ℓR by 362, the autoregressive model on the same two parameters does better still, and the combination is best on both criteria, by 82.96 of AICR over compound symmetry. The random-coefficient model, which gives each firm its own trend, describes these data worse than a persistent level plus decaying serial correlation.

Figure 33.4.1 shows why. The residual correlations are 0.953 at one year, 0.918 at two, 0.884 at five and 0.839 at ten: high everywhere, because the firm’s level dominates, but visibly decaying, which compound symmetry cannot reproduce. The variogram rises to a plateau of about 0.099, far below the total residual variance of 0.4839; the gap is the between-firm variance. The selected model is not innocent either: its fitted correlogram flattens onto its own floor after three or four years while the data go on decaying. That is what the diagnostic is for, and a reason to read the empirical standard error of Proposition 33.4.1(b) alongside the model-based one.

With the selected model the coefficient of log⁡(value) is 0.6295, with a model-based standard error of 0.0712 and an empirical one of 0.0608, and the coefficient of t is 0.4261 with standard errors 0.0600 and 0.0601. Ordinary least squares, treating the 220 observations as independent, reports 0.9833 and 0.3768 with standard errors 0.0351 and 0.0821. The two disagree substantially about the elasticity with respect to market value, because least squares reads it mostly from differences between firms while generalized least squares, having absorbed the firm levels into the covariance, reads it from movements within firms — the between-versus-within distinction of Section 6.6 again, and a question about the subject matter.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

df = sm.datasets.grunfeld.load_pandas().data
firms = sorted(df["firm"].unique())
years = np.sort(df["year"].unique())
N, m = len(firms), len(years)                        # 11 firms, 20 years
t = (years - years.mean()) / 10                      # centred and scaled time

Ylist, Xlist = [], []
for f in firms:
    d = df[df["firm"] == f].sort_values("year")
    Ylist.append(np.log(d["invest"].to_numpy()))
    Xlist.append(np.column_stack([np.ones(m), t, np.log(d["value"].to_numpy())]))
p = Xlist[0].shape[1]
n = N * m

With a common Σ, −2ℓR is Nlog⁡|Σ|+∑iri⊤Σ−1ri+log⁡|∑iXi⊤Σ−1Xi| plus a constant, evaluated at the generalized least squares 𝛃β~, the third term being what distinguishes it from the ordinary likelihood (Theorem 32.4.2).

from scipy.optimize import minimize

def reml(Sigma, Ylist, Xlist):
    """-2 x the restricted log likelihood, and the GLS estimate, for a common Sigma."""
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)
    logdet = 2 * np.sum(np.log(np.diag(L)))
    solve = lambda B: np.linalg.solve(Sigma, B)
    XtWX = sum(X.T @ solve(X) for X in Xlist)
    XtWy = sum(X.T @ solve(y) for X, y in zip(Xlist, Ylist))
    beta = np.linalg.solve(XtWX, XtWy)
    quad = sum((y - X @ beta) @ solve(y - X @ beta) for X, y in zip(Xlist, Ylist))
    s, ld_info = np.linalg.slogdet(XtWX)
    n, p = len(Ylist) * Sigma.shape[0], len(beta)
    return len(Ylist) * logdet + quad + ld_info + (n - p) * np.log(2 * np.pi), beta

lag = np.abs(np.subtract.outer(np.arange(m), np.arange(m)))
Zrc = np.column_stack([np.ones(m), t])               # random intercept and slope basis

def build(name, th):
    """Sigma for each covariance model, from an unconstrained parameter vector."""
    if name == "independence":
        return np.exp(th[0]) * np.eye(m)
    if name == "compound symmetry":
        return np.exp(th[0]) * np.eye(m) + np.exp(th[1]) * np.ones((m, m))
    if name == "AR(1)":
        return np.exp(th[0]) * np.tanh(th[1])**lag
    if name == "AR(1) + random intercept":
        return (np.exp(th[0]) * np.tanh(th[1])**lag + np.exp(th[2]) * np.ones((m, m)))
    if name == "random intercept and slope":
        D = np.array([[np.exp(th[1]), th[3]], [th[3], np.exp(th[2])]])
        return np.exp(th[0]) * np.eye(m) + Zrc @ D @ Zrc.T
    if name == "Toeplitz(4)":
        a = np.concatenate([[1.0], th[1:4]])         # a moving average of order 3 ...
        g = np.array([a[:4 - u] @ a[u:] for u in range(4)])
        c = np.concatenate([g / g[0], np.zeros(m - 4)])   # ... so the band is nonneg. definite
        return np.exp(th[0]) * c[lag]
    raise ValueError(name)

MODELS = {"independence": 1, "compound symmetry": 2, "AR(1)": 2,
          "AR(1) + random intercept": 3, "random intercept and slope": 4,
          "Toeplitz(4)": 4}

def fit(name, start):
    def obj(th):
        S = build(name, th)
        if np.min(np.linalg.eigvalsh(S)) <= 1e-9:
            return 1e6
        return reml(S, Ylist, Xlist)[0]
    best = min((minimize(obj, s, method="Nelder-Mead",
                         options={"maxiter": 20000, "xatol": 1e-9, "fatol": 1e-9})
                for s in start), key=lambda r: r.fun)
    S = build(name, best.x)
    d2l, beta = reml(S, Ylist, Xlist)
    q = MODELS[name]
    return {"m2ll": d2l, "aic": d2l + 2 * q, "bic": d2l + q * np.log(n - p),
            "q": q, "Sigma": S, "beta": beta}

starts = {"independence": [[-1.0]],
          "compound symmetry": [[-1.0, -1.0]],
          "AR(1)": [[-1.0, 1.0]],
          "AR(1) + random intercept": [[-1.5, 1.0, -1.5]],
          "random intercept and slope": [[-2.0, -1.0, -2.0, 0.0], [-3.0, -2.0, -3.0, 0.0]],
          "Toeplitz(4)": [[-1.0, 1.5, 1.0, 0.5], [-1.0, 2.5, 2.0, 1.0]]}
res = {name: fit(name, starts[name]) for name in MODELS}
for name in MODELS:
    r = res[name]
    print(f"{name:28s} q={r['q']}  -2logL_R={r['m2ll']:9.3f}  "
          f"AIC={r['aic']:9.3f}  BIC={r['bic']:9.3f}")
best_name = min(MODELS, key=lambda k: res[k]["aic"])
Xall = np.vstack(Xlist)
yall = np.concatenate(Ylist)
Sbest = res[best_name]["Sigma"]
A = np.linalg.inv(sum(X.T @ np.linalg.solve(Sbest, X) for X in Xlist))
beta = res[best_name]["beta"]
resid = [y - X @ beta for X, y in zip(Xlist, Ylist)]
meat = sum(np.outer(X.T @ np.linalg.solve(Sbest, r), X.T @ np.linalg.solve(Sbest, r))
           for X, r in zip(Xlist, resid))
cov_robust = A @ meat @ A * N / (N - 1)              # empirical ("sandwich") covariance
XtXinv = np.linalg.inv(Xall.T @ Xall)
beta_ols = XtXinv @ Xall.T @ yall
s2_ols = np.sum((yall - Xall @ beta_ols)**2) / (n - p)
for j, lab in enumerate(["intercept", "time/10", "log value"]):
    print(f"{lab:10s} GLS {beta[j]: .4f}  model se {np.sqrt(A[j, j]):.4f}  "
          f"empirical se {np.sqrt(cov_robust[j, j]):.4f}  "
          f"| OLS {beta_ols[j]: .4f} se {np.sqrt(s2_ols * XtXinv[j, j]):.4f}")

33.4.4. Missing data

Longitudinal data are rarely complete. The mixed-model machinery handles ragged data without complaint — Proposition 33.4.1 never assumed a common mi — an advantage over the classical analysis of Section 33.3, which needs a complete rectangle. But “handles” means “computes”. If the probability of a missing value depends only on the covariates and the responses that were observed, the observed-data likelihood is the right one: the data are missing at random. If it depends on the value that would have been observed — dropout because a patient is not improving — the likelihood is wrong and no covariance model repairs it.

Warning. Software that fits a mixed model to incomplete longitudinal data makes a missing-at-random assumption on the analyst’s behalf, silently, and the empirical standard error of Proposition 33.4.1(b) does not protect against its failure. Chapter 41 treats missing data properly.

33.4.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

A trial measures 60 patients at 8 visits. How many parameters does each model of Definition 33.4.1 need? Which could not be fitted with only 10 patients, and why?

Exercise (A2)

Sketch the population variogram γ(u) for compound symmetry, for the autoregressive model and for their combination, marking the total variance on each. Which feature identifies σu2, and which identifies measurement error?

Solution. Compound symmetry gives γ(u)=σ2 for every u>0, flat and well below the total variance σ2+σu2. The autoregressive model gives γ(u)=σ2(1−ρu), rising from 0 to its total variance σ2. The combination rises from 0 to σ2 and stops, leaving the gap σu2 to the total. The gap between plateau and total variance is σu2; the intercept of the variogram as u→0 is the measurement-error variance.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Two analysts fit the investment data of Example (Investment in eleven firms). The first uses the mean model of the example with an autoregressive covariance; the second adds a quadratic term in t and uses compound symmetry. They compare AICR. Explain what is wrong, and describe a correct sequence of comparisons.

Solution. By Proposition 33.4.1(c) the two restricted likelihoods are densities of different random vectors, of dimensions n−3 and n−4, so their difference is not a log likelihood ratio and AICR cannot compare them. Correct sequence: fix a rich mean model and compare covariance models by AICR; then, with the chosen covariance fixed, compare mean models by a Wald or F test, or by AIC from the full likelihood; then refit the covariance.

Exercise (B2)

Show that the Toeplitz model of Definition 33.4.1 with q=3 is nonnegative definite for every m iff (1,c1,c2,0,…) is the autocorrelation sequence of a moving-average process of order two, and explain why the parameterization cu∝∑lalal+u used in the listing above satisfies this automatically.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

The autoregressive model is nested in the autoregressive-plus-random-intercept model at σu2=0, a boundary of the parameter space. Explain why the likelihood ratio statistic comparing the two does not have a χ2(1) null distribution, state what it does have (Proposition 32.5.1), and say what this means for reading AICR differences as informal evidence.

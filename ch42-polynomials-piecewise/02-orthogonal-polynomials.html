42.2  Orthogonal polynomials

The space Pd, evaluated at the design, is an ordinary (d+1)-dimensional subspace of Rn. What is bad is the spanning set 1,x,…,xd. An orthonormal basis makes X⊤X=I, the condition number one, and the coefficients independent of the degree — and, by Theorem 6.7.1, changes not one fitted value. Section 9.4 built these polynomials by Gram–Schmidt; this section supplies what they are for an arbitrary design, the recurrence that generates them in O(nd) operations, and what changes when the basis is swapped.

42.2.1. The inner product of a design

Definition 42.2.1 (Orthogonal polynomials for a design)

Let x1,…,xn be design points, m of them distinct, with weights w1,…,wn>0. For real functions f,g on the design write ⟨f,g⟩=∑i=1nwif(xi)g(xi),‖f‖2=⟨f,f⟩, an inner product on the functions restricted to the design. Polynomials p0,…,pd with d<m are the orthogonal polynomials of the design if pj has degree exactly j with leading coefficient one (it is monic) and ⟨pj,pk⟩=0 for j≠k. The normalized qj=pj/‖pj‖ are the orthonormal polynomials of the design.

The restriction d<m is the rank condition of Exercise (A1): with fewer distinct points some nonzero polynomial of degree d vanishes on the design, and the form is only nonnegative definite. Two facts make the construction work, and both are one line.

Lemma 42.2.1 (Multiplication by the argument is self-adjoint)

For polynomials f,g let (xf)(x)=xf(x). Then ⟨xf,g⟩=⟨f,xg⟩.

Proof. Both sides equal ∑iwixif(xi)g(xi).

The second is that pj is orthogonal to every polynomial of degree less than j, not only to p0,…,pj−1: those span Pj−1, because their degrees are 0,1,…,j−1.

42.2.2. The three-term recurrence

Theorem 42.2.2 (Existence, uniqueness and the three-term recurrence)

Let d<m. The orthogonal polynomials of the design exist, are unique, and are generated by

p−1=0,p0=1,pj+1(x)=(x−aj)pj(x)−bjpj−1(x),j=0,1,…,d−1,
(42.2.1)

with aj=⟨xpj,pj⟩‖pj‖2,bj=‖pj‖2‖pj−1‖2 (j≥1),b0=0. Every ‖pj‖2 with j≤d is strictly positive.

Proof. Existence and the recurrence together. Argue by induction on j, the claim being that p0,…,pj produced by Eq. 42.2.1 are monic of degrees 0,…,j, mutually orthogonal, and have positive squared norms. For j=0, p0=1 is monic of degree 0 and ‖p0‖2=∑iwi>0.

Suppose the claim holds up to j≤d−1. The polynomial pj+1 defined by Eq. 42.2.1 is monic of degree j+1, since xpj is monic of degree j+1 and the subtracted terms have degree at most j. Take inner products. First, ⟨pj+1,pj⟩=⟨xpj,pj⟩−aj‖pj‖2−bj⟨pj−1,pj⟩=⟨xpj,pj⟩−aj‖pj‖2=0 by the choice of aj and the induction hypothesis. Next, for j≥1, using Lemma 42.2.1 and ⟨pj,pj−1⟩=0, ⟨pj+1,pj−1⟩=⟨pj,xpj−1⟩−aj⟨pj,pj−1⟩−bj‖pj−1‖2=⟨pj,xpj−1⟩−bj‖pj−1‖2. Now xpj−1 is monic of degree j, so xpj−1−pj has degree at most j−1 and is therefore orthogonal to pj. Hence ⟨pj,xpj−1⟩=‖pj‖2, and the choice of bj makes the display vanish. Finally, for k≤j−2, Lemma 42.2.1 gives ⟨pj+1,pk⟩=⟨pj,xpk⟩−aj⟨pj,pk⟩−bj⟨pj−1,pk⟩=⟨pj,xpk⟩=0, because xpk has degree at most j−1<j. So pj+1 is orthogonal to all of p0,…,pj.

It remains to show ‖pj+1‖2>0 when j+1≤d. If it were zero then pj+1, a monic polynomial of degree j+1≤d<m, would vanish at all m distinct design points, which is impossible.

Uniqueness. Suppose p~0,…,p~d also satisfy the definition. Since the degrees are 0,…,j, the set {p0,…,pj} is a basis of Pj, so p~j=∑k≤jckpk. Orthogonality of p~j to Pj−1 gives 0=⟨p~j,pk⟩=ck‖pk‖2 for k<j, hence ck=0; and both being monic forces cj=1.

The recurrence needs only the two previous polynomials, so the work is O(nd) rather than Gram–Schmidt’s O(nd2); it never forms xj for j>1, so nothing overflows or cancels; and aj,bj describe the basis in 2d numbers, which is how a fitted polynomial should be stored.

Remark (Why only three terms). Writing xpj=∑k≤j+1cjkpk, the coefficient cjk‖pk‖2=⟨xpj,pk⟩=⟨pj,xpk⟩ vanishes whenever xpk has degree below j, that is whenever k<j−1. The same argument gives three-term recurrences for the classical families — Legendre, Chebyshev, Hermite, Laguerre — the orthogonal polynomials of ⟨f,g⟩=∫fgw, which Section 39.5 used for numerical integration.

42.2.3. Sequential fitting

Corollary 42.2.3 (Fitting in the orthonormal basis)

Let qj have entries qj(xi) and let the weights be one, so the qj are orthonormal in Rn; write γ^j=qj⊤y. For every d<m:

  • (a) the least squares fit of degree d is f^d(t)=∑j=0dγ^jqj(t), and γ^j does not depend on d;

  • (b) E(γ^j)=⟨f,qj⟩ when E(Yi)=f(xi) for any function f, and 𝛄Cov(γ^)=σ2I;

  • (c) the residual sum of squares equals ‖y‖2−∑j≤dγ^j2, so the sequential sum of squares for degree j is γ^j2 on one degree of freedom, free of the order of entry;

  • (d) the F statistic for H:γd=0 in the model of degree d is γ^d2/σ^d2, with σ^d2 that model’s residual mean square, on (1,n−d−1) degrees of freedom.

Proof. With orthonormal columns X⊤X=I, so 𝛃β^=X⊤y componentwise, which is (a); the columns for degrees below d do not change when d grows, as in Corollary 9.4.2. Part (b) is 𝛍E(qj⊤Y)=qj⊤μ=⟨f,qj⟩ with Cov(X⊤Y)=σ2X⊤X; part (c) is Pythagoras, 𝛆‖y‖2=∑j≤dγ^j2+‖ε^‖2, and Theorem 9.4.1; part (d) is Theorem 11.6.1 for a single coefficient with Var(γ^d)=σ2, squared.

Part (b) is worth a second look. Whatever the true regression function, γ^j estimates the jth coefficient of its expansion in the design’s own orthonormal basis, with variance σ2 for every j. Where the sequence sinks into the noise is where the data stop distinguishing degrees: the practice of choosing the degree from the sequential sums of squares, and its honest statement.

Warning. Stopping at the first small γ^j2 is selection from the same data: the chosen model’s residual mean square is too small, its intervals too narrow, and the F statistic that justified the stop is not F (Proposition 29.5.1). The sequence can also dip and rise again, as it does below, so one insignificant term is a weak reason to stop.

42.2.4. The Nile, revisited

Example (The recurrence on the Nile series)

The annual flow of the Nile at Aswan, 1871–1970, gives n=100 equally spaced design points, rescaled here to [−1,1]. Example (A trend in the Nile) built its orthonormal polynomials by a QR factorization of the matrix of powers; Eq. 42.2.1 gives the same basis, the largest discrepancy between corresponding columns being 1.1×10−14 and the largest inner product between distinct columns 6.7×10−16, machine precision. The design being symmetric about its mean, every aj vanishes (Exercise (B1)), and the first four values of bj are 0.34007, 0.27197, 0.26213, 0.25871; Figure 42.2.1(a) draws the polynomials themselves.

The sequential sums of squares reproduce the table of Example (A trend in the Nile) — 613893, 309415, 1894, 134142 for degrees 1 to 4, and 4225 and 7034 for degrees 5 and 6 — with F statistics 27.08, 15.70, 0.10, 7.18, 0.22 and 0.37. The cubic term is negligible and the quartic is not: a reader stopping at the first insignificant term would have missed it.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

nile = sm.datasets.nile.load_pandas().data
year = nile["year"].to_numpy().astype(float)
y = nile["volume"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)
u = 2 * (year - year.min()) / (year.max() - year.min()) - 1     # design rescaled to [-1, 1]


def orthogonal_polynomials(t, d):
    """Monic polynomials p_0, ..., p_d orthogonal over the design t, by the recurrence."""
    P = np.zeros((len(t), d + 1))
    P[:, 0] = 1.0
    a, b, sq = np.zeros(d), np.zeros(d), [float(len(t))]
    for j in range(d):
        a[j] = (t * P[:, j] @ P[:, j]) / sq[j]
        P[:, j + 1] = (t - a[j]) * P[:, j]
        if j > 0:
            b[j] = sq[j] / sq[j - 1]
            P[:, j + 1] -= b[j] * P[:, j - 1]
        sq.append(P[:, j + 1] @ P[:, j + 1])
    return P, a, b, np.array(sq)


P, a, b, sq = orthogonal_polynomials(u, 8)
Q = P / np.sqrt(sq)                                             # orthonormal columns
print("a_j:", np.round(a[:5], 6))
print("b_j:", np.round(b[1:5], 5))
print("largest inner product between distinct columns:",
      f"{np.max(np.abs(Q.T @ Q - np.eye(9))):.2e}")
coef = Q.T @ y                                    # gamma_j = <q_j, y>, unchanged by the degree
seq_ss = coef[1:] ** 2                            # sequential sum of squares for each degree
sse = np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2) - np.cumsum(seq_ss)
for j in range(1, 7):
    F = seq_ss[j - 1] / (sse[j - 1] / (n - j - 1))
    print(f"degree {j}: SS = {seq_ss[j - 1]:9.0f}   SSE = {sse[j - 1]:9.0f}   F = {F:7.2f}")

Remark (A limit that explains the numbers). For a design filling an interval evenly, n−1∑if(xi)g(xi) is a Riemann sum for 12∫−11fg, so the design’s orthogonal polynomials approach the monic Legendre polynomials, with aj=0 and bj=j2/(4j2−1) (Exercise (B2)): that is 0.33333, 0.26667, 0.25714, 0.25397 against the Nile’s 0.34007, 0.27197, 0.26213, 0.25871. Since bj→1/4, the monic polynomials shrink geometrically: badly scaled even though orthogonal, so normalizing matters as much as orthogonalizing.

42.2.5. What changes and what does not

For the Nile design the condition numbers of four bases for Pd are

Degree raw years rescaled monic orthogonal orthonormal
2 1.83×1010 3.70×100 3.29×100 1
4 3.90×1020 1.85×101 1.26×101 1
6 2.83×1031 9.77×101 4.92×101 1
8 1.42×1040 5.31×102 1.93×102 1

Orthogonality by itself buys a factor growing from about one to about three over this range; the orthonormal basis has X⊤X=I, so its condition number is one at every degree and least squares in it is a matrix–vector product. The gap between the last two columns is entirely scaling.

What does not change is everything else: Y^, 𝛆ε^, every leverage, every band, every F statistic about the space. The degree-16 polynomial of Figure 42.1.1 oscillates identically in the orthonormal basis, and its extrapolation is still nonsense. Orthogonal polynomials answer the first complaint of Section 42.1 and are silent on the second.

Remark (Where these have appeared before). For a factor with quantitative levels, the same construction with weights wk=nk gives the polynomial contrasts of Section 8.7 (Example (Trend in ideology across party identification)): the orthogonal polynomial coding of a factor and the basis that software offers for a continuous regressor are one object.

42.2.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Compute the monic orthogonal polynomials p0,p1,p2 for the design x=(−1,0,1) with unit weights from Eq. 42.2.1, and check that their integer-scaled versions are the contrasts (−1,0,1) and (1,−2,1).

Exercise (A2)

A quartic is fitted in the raw powers and again in the orthonormal polynomials of the design. Which of these differ between the two fits: the linear coefficient, the fitted value at x1, the residual sum of squares, the sequential sum of squares for degree 3, the leverage h11, the condition number of X?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that if x↦2c−x permutes the design and preserves the weights, then aj=c for every j and pj(2c−x)=(−1)jpj(x).

Solution. Take c=0 after a shift, and induct. p0 is even, and p1=x−a0 with a0=⟨x,1⟩/‖1‖2=0 by symmetry, so p1 is odd. If pj−1 and pj have parities (−1)j−1 and (−1)j, then xpj2 is odd, so aj=0, and pj+1=xpj−bjpj−1 is a sum of two polynomials of parity (−1)j+1.

Exercise (B2)

The monic Legendre polynomials are orthogonal for ⟨f,g⟩=∫−11fg. Using Theorem 42.2.2 and the identity ∫−11Pj2=2/(2j+1) for the standard Legendre polynomials, whose leading coefficient is (2jj)2−j, verify that bj=j2/(4j2−1).

Solution. Write P~j=Pj/ℓj with ℓj=(2jj)2−j for the monic version. Then ‖P~j‖2=2/{(2j+1)ℓj2} and bj=‖P~j‖2‖P~j−1‖2=(2j−1)ℓj−12(2j+1)ℓj2. Since ℓj/ℓj−1=(2j−1)/j, the ratio is (2j−1)j2/{(2j+1)(2j−1)2}=j2/(4j2−1).

Exercise (B3)

Given a0,…,ad−1, b1,…,bd−1, ‖pj‖2 and γ^0,…,γ^d, write down an algorithm that evaluates f^d(t) at a new t in O(d) operations and storage, without ever computing a power of t. Why is this preferable to expanding f^d in the monomial basis and using Horner’s rule?

Solution. Run Eq. 42.2.1 forwards at the single point t, accumulating ∑jγ^jpj(t)/‖pj‖ as you go; only pj−1(t) and pj(t) need be kept. (Running it backwards, accumulating the coefficients instead, is Clenshaw’s algorithm, the standard way of summing a Chebyshev or other orthogonal-polynomial series.) Horner’s rule is equally fast but needs the monomial coefficients, and converting the fit to those is the ill-conditioned step: the conversion matrix is the change of basis whose condition number the table above reports.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that pj has j distinct real zeros, all strictly inside the range of the design. (Suppose it changes sign at only r<j interior points z1,…,zr, and consider ⟨pj,∏l(x−zl)⟩.)

Exercise (C2)

For two regressors the analogue of Pd is the span of the monomials xrzs with r+s≤d. List an orthogonal basis ϕ1,ϕ2,… of it in a fixed order of nondecreasing total degree. The one-variable proof works because xpj expands in pj−1,pj,pj+1 alone; exhibit a design in R2 and an index i for which xϕi has a nonzero inner product with some ϕk, k<i−1, so that no scalar three-term recurrence generates the list. (Vector recurrences over a whole block of one total degree do exist; the single-variable bookkeeping is what fails.) Relate this to Chapter 44.

3.4  Independence and zero covariance

For any random vectors with finite second moments, independence implies zero cross-covariance, because the expectation of a product of functions of independent variables factors. The converse fails in general, as Example (A random sign) and Example (A mixture of two correlations) show. For jointly normal vectors it holds. This is the single most used property of the normal distribution in linear-model theory. Every independence statement behind a t or F statistic reduces to it.

Theorem 3.4.1 (Independence)

Let Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) be partitioned into blocks Y=(Y1⊤,…,Yk⊤)⊤, with μ and Σ=(Σij) partitioned to match.

  • (a) The blocks Y1,…,Yk are mutually independent iff Σij=Cov(Yi,Yj)=0 for all i≠j.

  • (b) In particular, Y1 and Y2 are independent iff Cov(Y1,Y2)=0, and the scalar entries Y1,…,Yn are mutually independent iff Σ is diagonal.

Proof. If the blocks are independent, every cross-covariance is zero. Conversely, suppose Σij=0 for i≠j, and partition t to match. Then t⊤μ+12t⊤Σt=∑i=1k(ti⊤μi+12ti⊤Σiiti), because all the cross terms ti⊤Σijtj vanish. Exponentiating, Theorem 3.2.1 gives MY(t)=∏iMi(ti), where Mi is the moment generating function of N(μi,Σii). By Corollary 3.3.2 that is the distribution of Yi, so Mi=MYi. The factorization property of Section 3.2 gives mutual independence. Part (b) is the cases k=2 and k=n.

A consequence worth stating explicitly: for jointly normal blocks, pairwise independence implies mutual independence, because condition (a) involves only pairs. For general random vectors this is false, as the example of three random signs in Example (Pairwise but not mutually independent) shows. Joint normality of the whole vector matters here: joint normality of each pair is not enough (Exercise (C1)).

The theorem is usually applied to two linear functions of the same normal vector.

Corollary 3.4.2

Let Y∼Nn(μ,Σ), and let A and B be matrices with n columns. Then AY and BY are independent iff AΣB⊤=0. In that case g(AY) and h(BY) are independent for all measurable functions g and h.

Proof. The stacked vector ((AY)⊤,(BY)⊤)⊤ is a linear function of Y, so it is normal by Theorem 3.3.1. Its off-diagonal covariance block is Cov(AY,BY)=AΣB⊤ (Theorem 2.2.1). Apply Theorem 3.4.1. Functions of independent random vectors are independent.

The condition AΣB⊤=0 involves only second moments, but the conclusion is full independence. In particular, any quadratic form in BY, such as ‖BY‖2, is independent of AY. Chapter 4 extends this to quadratic forms that are not written as squared lengths (Theorem 4.4.1).

Example (Sample mean and deviations)

Let Y∼Nn(μ1,σ2I), so that Y1,…,Yn is a random sample from N(μ,σ2). The mean is Y¯=n−11⊤Y, and the vector of deviations is d=(I−n−111⊤)Y. As in Example (Centring a sample), Cov(Y¯,d)=σ2n−11⊤(I−n−111⊤)=0⊤, By Corollary 3.4.2, Y¯ is independent of d, and so of every function of the deviations: the sample variance S2=‖d‖2/(n−1), the range, the sample skewness. The distribution of (n−1)S2/σ2 is found in Corollary 4.4.5. The computation used Cov(Y)=σ2I only through Σ1∈span(1), which is the precise condition for the conclusion (Exercise (B1)).

Example (Coefficients and residuals)

In the setting of Example (Least squares under normal errors), Cov(β^,ε^)=(X⊤X)−1X⊤(σ2I)(I−M)=σ2(X⊤X)−1{(I−M)X}⊤=0, since (I−M)X=0. Hence β^ is independent of the residual vector, and so of the residual sum of squares ‖ε^‖2 and of the estimate s2=‖ε^‖2/(n−p). The same argument shows that the fitted values Y^ and the residuals are independent. This independence is what allows a t statistic (λ⊤β^−λ⊤β)/{s2λ⊤(X⊤X)−1λ}1/2 to have a t distribution (Chapter 11).

Remark (What joint normality buys). The two examples use only a covariance calculation. Without joint normality, zero covariance between β^ and ε^ still holds under the second-moment assumptions of Definition 5.3.1, but independence does not follow, and neither do exact t and F distributions. Chapter 21 examines what survives approximately.

3.4.1. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let Y1,Y2 be independent N(μ,σ2). Show that Y1+Y2 and Y1−Y2 are independent. What if the variances differ?

Exercise (A2)

Let Y∼Nn(0,I) and let u be a unit vector. Show that u⊤Y and ‖Y−(u⊤Y)u‖2 are independent.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let Y∼Nn(μ1,Σ) with Σ arbitrary. Show that Y¯ is independent of the deviations Yi−Y¯ iff Σ1∈span(1), that is, iff all row sums of Σ are equal. Check the condition for the equicorrelation matrix σ2{(1−ρ)I+ρ11⊤}, and for a two-group matrix: the first m1 and the last m2 observations form groups, all variances are 1, the correlation is ρ within a group and τ between groups.

Solution. Y¯=n−11⊤Y and the deviations are (I−n−111⊤)Y, so by Corollary 3.4.2 they are independent iff n−11⊤Σ(I−n−111⊤)=0⊤, that is, (I−n−111⊤)Σ1=0. The null space of I−n−111⊤ is span(1), and Σ1 is the vector of row sums. For the equicorrelation matrix, Σ1=σ2(1−ρ+nρ)1, so independence holds for every admissible ρ. In the two-group matrix, a row in the first group sums to 1+(m1−1)ρ+m2τ and a row in the second to 1+(m2−1)ρ+m1τ. The difference is (m1−m2)(ρ−τ), so independence holds iff the groups have equal size or ρ=τ, and the latter is the equicorrelated case.

Exercise (B2)

Let Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I) with rank(X)=r<p, let G be a generalized inverse of X⊤X and M=XGX⊤. Show that the least squares estimate GX⊤Y and the residual vector (I−M)Y are independent, and that every estimable function λ⊤β with λ∈C(X⊤) has an estimate independent of the residual sum of squares.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let φ be the N(0,1) density, 0<ε≤e3/2, and f(x,y,z)=φ(x)φ(y)φ(z){1+εxyze−(x2+y2+z2)/2}. Show that f is a probability density, and that under f every pair of coordinates is N2(0,I). So the three variables are pairwise jointly normal and pairwise independent. Show that they are not mutually independent and that (X,Y,Z) is not jointly normal. Which step of Theorem 3.4.1 fails?

Solution. Since |t|e−t2/2≤e−1/2 for all t, the perturbation satisfies |εxyze−(x2+y2+z2)/2|≤εe−3/2≤1, so f≥0. The function z↦zφ(z)e−z2/2 is odd and integrable, so integrating f over z removes the perturbation and leaves φ(x)φ(y). Integrating further shows that f integrates to 1, and by symmetry each pair has density φ⊗φ, the N2(0,I) density. Mutual independence would force f=φ(x)φ(y)φ(z) almost everywhere, but the perturbation is nonzero off the coordinate planes. Directly, E(XYZ)=ε(∫t2φ(t)e−t2/2dt)3>0, whereas independence with mean-zero margins gives 0. If (X,Y,Z) were jointly normal, its covariance matrix would be I (unit variances from the margins, zero covariances from the pairs), and by Theorem 3.4.1 the coordinates would be independent, which they are not. The theorem’s hypothesis is joint normality of the whole vector. Here only the pairs are normal, and the moment generating function of the triple does not factor.

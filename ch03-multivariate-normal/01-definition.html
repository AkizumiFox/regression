3.1  Definition through linear combinations

Nearly every exact distributional statement in the first half of this book has the same form. The response is 𝛃𝛆Y=Xβ+ε with normal errors, and the object of interest is a linear function of Y (a coefficient estimate, a fitted value, a residual) or a quadratic function of Y (a sum of squares). A usable notion of normality for random vectors must therefore do two things. It must survive every linear map, including maps that collapse dimensions. And it must say when two linear functions of the same vector are independent.

The residual vector shows why the first requirement has to include singular cases. In a model with p linearly independent columns its covariance matrix has rank n−p (Chapter 6), so the residuals have no density on Rn. Defining multivariate normality through a density would exclude it. This chapter uses a definition that does not mention densities at all: a random vector is normal when every linear combination of its entries is normal. The density, the moment generating function and the representation 𝛍μ+AZ all follow as theorems.

3.1.1. The univariate normal, including the degenerate case

For μ∈R and σ2>0, a random variable X has the N(μ,σ2) distribution when it has density (2πσ2)−1/2exp⁡{−(x−μ)2/(2σ2)}. We also allow σ2=0, and take N(μ,0) to be the point mass at μ. Treating constants as normal variables with zero variance costs nothing and removes a great many special cases later.

Lemma 3.1.1

Let X∼N(μ,σ2) with σ2≥0.

  • (a) EesX=exp⁡(μs+12σ2s2) for every real s.

  • (b) A random variable whose moment generating function is exp⁡(μs+12σ2s2) on an interval around 0 has the N(μ,σ2) distribution.

  • (c) aX+b∼N(aμ+b,a2σ2), and if X1∼N(μ1,σ12) and X2∼N(μ2,σ22) are independent, then X1+X2∼N(μ1+μ2,σ12+σ22).

Proof.

  • (a) For σ2=0 this is eμs. Otherwise, completing the square, sx−(x−μ)2/(2σ2)=μs+σ2s2/2−(x−μ−σ2s)2/(2σ2), and the remaining Gaussian integral equals (2πσ2)1/2.
  • (b) is the uniqueness theorem for moment generating functions (Theorem 2.6.1(a)), in its scalar form.
  • (c) The moment generating function of aX+b is ebsEe(as)X=exp⁡{(aμ+b)s+12a2σ2s2}. For the sum, independence gives Ees(X1+X2)=EesX1EesX2, which is the normal moment generating function with the added parameters. Apply (b).

3.1.2. The definition

Definition 3.1.1 (Multivariate normal distribution)

A random vector Y=(Y1,…,Yn)⊤ has a multivariate normal distribution if a⊤Y has a univariate normal distribution (possibly degenerate) for every a∈Rn. Such a Y is also said to be jointly normal, and its entries are said to be jointly normal random variables.

Taking a to be a coordinate vector shows that each Yi is normal, so EYi2<∞. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality every product YiYj is integrable, so the mean vector 𝛍μ=EY and the covariance matrix Σ=Cov(Y) of Definition 2.1.2 exist. By Theorem 2.2.1, 𝛍E(a⊤Y)=a⊤μ and Var(a⊤Y)=a⊤Σa, and a normal variable is determined by its mean and variance. Hence

𝛍a⊤Y∼N(a⊤μ, a⊤Σa)for every a∈Rn.
(3.1.1)

We write 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,Σ). The notation claims that the joint distribution of Y depends only on 𝛍μ and Σ. That is true, but it is not obvious: the definition fixes the law of each one-dimensional projection, and it is not yet clear that these laws pin down the joint law. The claim is proved in Theorem 3.2.1. Until then, 𝛍Nn(μ,Σ) should be read as “normal, with mean 𝛍μ and covariance Σ”.

3.1.3. Existence

Theorem 3.1.2 (Existence and the representation 𝛍μ+AZ)

Let Z1,…,Zm be independent N(0,1) variables and Z=(Z1,…,Zm)⊤.

  • (a) Z∼Nm(0,Im).

  • (b) For any n×m matrix A and 𝛍μ∈Rn, 𝛍Y=μ+AZ is multivariate normal with mean 𝛍μ and covariance AA⊤.

  • (c) For every 𝛍μ∈Rn and every nonnegative definite n×n matrix Σ there is a random vector with the 𝛍Nn(μ,Σ) distribution, for example 𝛍μ+Σ1/2Z with m=n.

No other matrices occur: the covariance matrix of a normal vector is nonnegative definite.

Proof.

  • (a) a⊤Z=∑iaiZi is a sum of independent normal variables aiZi∼N(0,ai2), which is normal by Lemma 3.1.1(c).
  • (b) 𝛍a⊤Y=a⊤μ+(A⊤a)⊤Z is a constant plus a linear combination of Z, hence normal by (a). Its moments come from Theorem 2.2.1: 𝛍EY=μ and Cov(Y)=AIA⊤=AA⊤.
  • (c) By Theorem 1.7.6 a nonnegative definite Σ has a symmetric nonnegative definite square root with Σ1/2Σ1/2=Σ. Take A=Σ1/2 in (b). The last sentence is Theorem 2.2.3.

Any factorization Σ=AA⊤ serves in (b). The symmetric square root is convenient in proofs. The Cholesky factor, a lower triangular L with LL⊤=Σ, is cheaper to compute and is what software uses. A factor with fewer columns than rows is natural when Σ is singular. Once Theorem 3.2.1 is proved, all of these give the same distribution. The first listing checks this by simulation. It draws 400,000 vectors from a three-dimensional normal distribution with each of two factors. Both sample covariance matrices agree with Σ to within 0.016 in every entry, and the quantiles of a linear combination match the exact normal quantiles.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(301)
mu = np.array([1.0, -2.0, 0.5])
Sigma = np.array([[4.0, 1.2, -0.8],
                  [1.2, 2.0, 0.6],
                  [-0.8, 0.6, 1.5]])
N = 400_000

def draw_normal(mu, A, size, rng):
    """Rows are independent copies of mu + A Z with Z ~ N(0, I)."""
    Z = rng.standard_normal((size, A.shape[1]))
    return mu + Z @ A.T

L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)                  # lower triangular, L L^T = Sigma
lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(Sigma)
root = U @ np.diag(np.sqrt(lam)) @ U.T         # symmetric square root

Y_chol = draw_normal(mu, L, N, rng)
Y_root = draw_normal(mu, root, N, rng)
for Y in (Y_chol, Y_root):
    print(np.round(np.cov(Y, rowvar=False) - Sigma, 3))

3.1.4. Singular normal distributions

When Σ is singular, Definition 3.1.1 still applies, and the distribution is called singular or degenerate. If Σv=0, then Var(v⊤Y)=v⊤Σv=0, so 𝛍v⊤Y=v⊤μ with probability one. Every vector in N(Σ) gives such a linear constraint, and together they say that Y lies in the affine subspace 𝛍μ+C(Σ) with probability one (Theorem 2.2.3). A subspace of dimension rank(Σ)<n has Lebesgue measure zero, so no density on Rn is possible.

Example (Three overlapping sums)

Let Z∼N3(0,I) and U=(Z1+Z2, Z2−Z3, Z1+Z3)⊤. Then U=AZ with rows (1,1,0), (0,1,−1) and (1,0,1), so U is normal with mean zero and covariance AA⊤=(21112−11−12). The vector (1,−1,−1)⊤ lies in N(AA⊤), and the rank is 2. Correspondingly U1−U2−U3=0 for every outcome, not merely with probability one. Each Ui is N(0,2), each pair (Ui,Uj) has a nonsingular covariance matrix (determinant 3) and a bivariate density, and yet the triple lives on a plane.

In regression the singular case is the rule. With 𝛃Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I), the fitted values and the residuals are normal vectors of ranks rank(X) and n−rank(X) (Example (Least squares under normal errors)). The last part of the listing draws a normal vector in R3 from a 3×2 factor. Every draw lies on the plane 𝛍μ+C(A) to within 2.7×10−15, which is rounding error.

3.1.5. Why linear combinations suffice

Definition 3.1.1 describes a joint distribution entirely through its one-dimensional projections. That this loses no information is a general fact about random vectors, the Cramér–Wold device proved with characteristic functions in Proposition 2.6.2: if a⊤Y and a⊤W have the same distribution for every a∈Rn, then Y and W have the same distribution. We do not need it in this generality. For normal vectors the moment generating function is finite everywhere, and the next section runs the same argument with it. The device explains why the definition is natural. It also warns that all directions a matter. Normality of the n coordinate variables, which are only n directions, is not enough (Section 3.3 and Exercise (B3)).

3.1.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let Y1,…,Yn be independent with Yi∼N(μi,σi2). Show directly from Definition 3.1.1 that Y=(Y1,…,Yn)⊤ is multivariate normal, and find its mean and covariance. Show also that a constant vector c is Nn(c,0).

Exercise (A2)

For the bivariate normal of Example (The bivariate normal), find the Cholesky factor L of Σ and write Y1 and Y2 in terms of two independent standard normals. Do the same with the symmetric square root when σ1=σ2=1. Why do the two representations define the same distribution?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let Z∼N(0,1) and Y=(Z, 2Z−1, 3−Z)⊤. Show that Y is normal, find 𝛍μ and Σ, and find the rank of Σ. Describe the set in which Y lies, and write it as the solution set of two linear equations.

Solution. 𝛍Y=μ+AZ with 𝛍μ=(0,−1,3)⊤ and A=(1,2,−1)⊤, so Y is normal by Theorem 3.1.2 with Σ=AA⊤=(12−124−2−1−21),rank(Σ)=1. Y lies on the line 𝛍{μ+sA:s∈R}. The null space of Σ is A⊥, spanned by (2,−1,0)⊤ and (1,0,1)⊤. The corresponding constraints 𝛍v⊤Y=v⊤μ are 2Y1−Y2=1 and Y1+Y3=3, and both hold identically.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

The text says that every factorization Σ=AA⊤ gives the same distribution, and proves it later from the moment generating function. Here is the algebraic reason.

  • (a) Let A and B be n×m with AA⊤=BB⊤. Show that B=AQ for some m×m orthogonal Q. Hint: ‖A⊤x‖=‖B⊤x‖ for every x, so A⊤x↦B⊤x is a well-defined linear isometry of C(A⊤) onto C(B⊤); extend it to Rm.

  • (b) Deduce that 𝛍μ+AZ and 𝛍μ+BZ have the same distribution, with no appeal to Theorem 3.2.1. Hint: the density of Z depends on z only through ‖z‖. Conclude for the Cholesky factor and the symmetric square root of a positive definite Σ.

  • (c) Show that rank(A)=rank(Σ) for every such factor, so that no representation 𝛍μ+AZ uses fewer than rank(Σ) standard normal variables, and that rank(Σ) of them suffice.

Solution.

  • (a) For every x, ‖A⊤x‖2=x⊤AA⊤x=x⊤BB⊤x=‖B⊤x‖2. If A⊤x=A⊤x′ then ‖B⊤(x−x′)‖=‖A⊤(x−x′)‖=0, so the assignment Q1:A⊤x↦B⊤x is a well-defined linear map from C(A⊤) onto C(B⊤), and it preserves norms, hence inner products by polarization. The two column spaces have the same dimension r=rank(A)=rank(AA⊤)=rank(BB⊤)=rank(B) by Proposition 1.2.3, so their orthogonal complements in Rm have dimension m−r; extend Q1 by mapping an orthonormal basis of C(A⊤)⊥ onto one of C(B⊤)⊥. The extension is an orthogonal matrix with Q1A⊤=B⊤, so B=AQ1⊤ and Q=Q1⊤ works.

  • (b) By (a), BZ=A(QZ). The density of Z is (2π)−m/2exp⁡(−‖z‖2/2), and the substitution z=Q⊤w has Jacobian |detQ⊤|=1 and leaves ‖z‖ unchanged, so QZ has that same density. Hence BZ and AZ have the same distribution, and so do 𝛍μ+AZ and 𝛍μ+BZ. With Σ=LL⊤=Σ1/2Σ1/2 both factors are square, so the Cholesky and square-root constructions differ by an orthogonal factor and produce the same law.

  • (c) rank(A)=rank(AA⊤)=rank(Σ) by Proposition 1.2.3, and an n×m matrix has rank at most m, so m≥rank(Σ). The factor A=ΓrΛr1/2 built from the r=rank(Σ) positive eigenvalues attains it.

Exercise (C2)

Suppose 𝛍Yk∼Nn(μk,Σk) converges in distribution to a random vector Y.

  • (a) Treat first n=1: if N(mk,vk) converges in distribution, show that mk→m and vk→v for finite limits with v≥0, and that the limit law is N(m,v), degenerate when v=0. Hint: the characteristic functions are exp⁡(itmk−t2vk/2); a limit that is continuous at 0 keeps vk from escaping to ∞, and eitmk can converge for every t only if mk does.

  • (b) Deduce for general n, using Eq. 3.1.1 and Definition 3.1.1, that 𝛍𝛍μk→μ, Σk→Σ entrywise and 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,Σ). So the normal family, singular members included, is closed under limits in distribution.

  • (c) Show conversely that every singular 𝛍Nn(μ,Σ) is a limit of nonsingular ones. The degenerate distributions this section insists on admitting are exactly the ones that would otherwise be missing.

Solution.

  • (a) Let ϕ be the characteristic function of the limit, so that exp⁡(itmk−t2vk/2)→ϕ(t) for every t, and ϕ is continuous at 0 with ϕ(0)=1. Taking absolute values, e−t2vk/2→|ϕ(t)|. Choose t0>0 with |ϕ(t0)|>0; then vk→v=−2log⁡|ϕ(t0)|/t02, finite and nonnegative. Hence eitmk→ψ(t)=ϕ(t)et2v/2 for every t, with |ψ|≡1. If |mk|→∞ along a subsequence, then ∫0Teitmkdt=(eiTmk−1)/(imk)→0 for every T, while dominated convergence makes the limit ∫0Tψ; so ψ=0 almost everywhere, contradicting |ψ|=1. So (mk) is bounded, and if one subsequence converged to m and another to m′, then eitm=eitm′ for all t, forcing m=m′. Thus mk→m and ϕ(t)=exp⁡(itm−t2v/2), the characteristic function of N(m,v).

  • (b) For each a, the continuous mapping theorem gives a⊤Yk→a⊤Y in distribution, and 𝛍a⊤Yk∼N(a⊤μk,a⊤Σka) by Eq. 3.1.1. By (a), 𝛍a⊤μk and a⊤Σka converge and a⊤Y is normal. Taking a=ei gives 𝛍𝛍μk→μ entrywise and σk,ii→σii; taking a=ei+ej and subtracting gives σk,ij→σij. The limit Σ is nonnegative definite as a limit of nonnegative definite matrices, and every a⊤Y is normal with mean 𝛍a⊤μ and variance a⊤Σa, which is Definition 3.1.1.

  • (c) Σ+εI is positive definite for ε>0, and the characteristic functions 𝛍exp⁡(it⊤μ−12t⊤Σt−12ε‖t‖2) converge pointwise as ε↓0 to that of 𝛍Nn(μ,Σ).

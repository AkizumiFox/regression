3.6  Partial correlation

Two variables can be strongly correlated only because both depend on a third. Example (Holding a third variable fixed) showed a positive association between Y1 and Y2 turning negative once Y3 is held fixed. The conditional covariance matrix Σ11⋅2 measures association that remains after conditioning, and its correlation form is the partial correlation.

3.6.1. Definition and interpretations

Definition 3.6.1 (Partial correlation)

Let Y=(Y1⊤,Y2⊤)⊤ be a random vector with covariance matrix Σ, partitioned as in Section 3.5, and write Σ11⋅2=Σ11−Σ12Σ22−Σ21=(σij⋅2). The partial covariance of Yi and Yj (two entries of Y1) given Y2 is σij⋅2, and their partial correlation given Y2 is ρij⋅2=σij⋅2σii⋅2σjj⋅2, provided σii⋅2 and σjj⋅2 are positive. When the conditioning variables are Yk,Yl,… we also write ρij⋅kl…. The matrix of partial correlations is D−1/2Σ11⋅2D−1/2 with D=diag(Σ11⋅2), the correlation matrix (Definition 2.4.1) built from Σ11⋅2.

The definition uses only Σ, so partial correlations exist for any random vector with finite second moments. The next result gives them two meanings, one that always applies and one that needs normality.

Proposition 3.6.1

In the setting of Definition 3.6.1:

  • (a) Σ11⋅2 is the covariance matrix of the prediction errors W=Y1−μ1−Σ12Σ22−(Y2−μ2), and W is uncorrelated with Y2. If Σ22 is positive definite, the entries of W are the errors of the best linear predictors L(Yi∣Y2), so ρij⋅2 is the correlation between Yi−L(Yi∣Y2) and Yj−L(Yj∣Y2).

  • (b) If Y is jointly normal, ρij⋅2 is the correlation of Yi and Yj in their conditional distribution given Y2=y2, the same for every y2. Moreover ρij⋅2=0 iff Yi and Yj are conditionally independent given Y2.

Proof.

  • (a) This is Theorem 2.5.2, restated: Cov(W,Y2)=0 and Cov(W)=Σ11⋅2 are the moment calculations in the proof of Theorem 3.5.2(a), which use no normality. When Σ22 is positive definite, the ith entry of μ1+Σ12Σ22−1(Y2−μ2) is L(Yi∣Y2) by Theorem 6.11.1.
  • (b) By Theorem 3.5.2, the conditional distribution of (Yi,Yj) given Y2=y2 is bivariate normal with covariance matrix the corresponding 2×2 block of Σ11⋅2. Its correlation is ρij⋅2, and by Theorem 3.4.1 applied to this conditional normal distribution, the two are independent iff the off-diagonal entry is zero.

Part (a) is the definition to remember: a partial correlation is the correlation of two residuals. Remove from each variable the part that is linearly predictable from the conditioning variables, and correlate what is left. This is the population version of the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell construction (Theorem 6.6.2).

3.6.2. Computing partial correlations

Two routes avoid computing Σ11⋅2 in full.

With a single conditioning variable, Σ11⋅2 is a 2×2 matrix with entries σij−σi3σj3/σ33, and in correlation units this is the familiar three-variable formula of Exercise (B1),

ρ12⋅3=ρ12−ρ13ρ23(1−ρ132)(1−ρ232),|ρ13|<1, |ρ23|<1.
(3.6.1)

The residual interpretation shows that the same formula can be applied one conditioning variable at a time.

Proposition 3.6.2 (Recursion)

Let S be a set of indices and k∉S∪{1,2} one more. Suppose σ11⋅S, σ22⋅S and σkk⋅S are positive and |ρ1k⋅S|<1, |ρ2k⋅S|<1. Then σ12⋅Sk=σ12⋅S−σ1k⋅Sσ2k⋅Sσkk⋅S,ρ12⋅Sk=ρ12⋅S−ρ1k⋅Sρ2k⋅S(1−ρ1k⋅S2)(1−ρ2k⋅S2). With S empty this is Eq. 3.6.1.

Proof. No normality is needed. For i∈{1,2,k} let ei be the error of the best linear predictor of Yi from YS, as in Proposition 3.6.1(a). Then each ei has mean zero, is uncorrelated with YS, and Cov(ei,ej)=σij⋅S. Put ri=ei−ciek with ci=σik⋅S/σkk⋅S, for i=1,2.

We claim that ri is the error of the best linear predictor of Yi from (YS,Yk). First, Yi−ri is an affine function of (YS,Yk), because ei and ek differ from Yi and Yk by affine functions of YS. Second, ri has mean zero and is uncorrelated with YS and with ek, by the choice of ci, hence with Yk=ek+(affine in YS). Now let ri′ be the error from Proposition 3.6.1(a) for predicting Yi from (YS,Yk), which has the same two properties. The difference d=ri−ri′ is an affine function of (YS,Yk) with mean zero and is uncorrelated with (YS,Yk), so Var(d)=Cov(d,d)=0. Thus ri=ri′ with probability one.

Therefore σ12⋅Sk=Cov(r1,r2)=σ12⋅S−c2σ1k⋅S−c1σ2k⋅S+c1c2σkk⋅S, which simplifies to the first formula, and similarly σii⋅Sk=σii⋅S(1−ρik⋅S2), which is positive by hypothesis. Dividing σ12⋅Sk by σ11⋅Skσ22⋅Sk and then numerator and denominator by σ11⋅Sσ22⋅S gives the second.

Proposition 3.6.3 (Partial correlations from the precision matrix)

Let Σ be positive definite and Ω=Σ−1=(ωij). The partial correlation of Yi and Yj given all the other entries of Y is ρij⋅rest=−ωijωiiωjj, and the conditional variance of Yi given all the others is 1/ωii.

Proof. Permute Y so that (Yi,Yj) comes first. By Theorem 3.5.2(c) (a statement about matrices, valid whether or not Y is normal), the leading 2×2 block of Ω is Σ11⋅2−1. Inverting, Σ11⋅2=(ωiiωijωijωjj)−1=1ωiiωjj−ωij2(ωjj−ωij−ωijωii), whose correlation is −ωij/ωiiωjj. The same argument with a 1×1 leading block gives the conditional variance.

Proposition 3.6.3 has a striking consequence for normal vectors: a zero entry ωij of the inverse covariance matrix means that Yi and Yj are conditionally independent given all the other variables. Zeros in Σ describe marginal independence, zeros in Σ−1 describe conditional independence. The pattern of zeros in Σ−1 defines a graph on the variables, which is the starting point of Gaussian graphical models (Dempster 1972; Lauritzen 1996).

Example (The trivariate example, continued)

For Σ of Example (Holding a third variable fixed), the listing gives the covariance of (Y1,Y2) given Y3 as (2−1−11). So ρ12=0.289 while ρ12⋅3=−0.707. The script confirms that Eq. 3.6.1, Proposition 3.6.3, and the correlation of simulated residuals all give this value.

3.6.3. Sample partial correlations

For data, replace Σ by the sample covariance matrix. Because the empirical distribution of the rows is a probability distribution in its own right (Section 6.11), all the identities above hold exactly for the sample versions. In particular, for two variables y,x and conditioning variables collected with an intercept in Z, the following numbers are identical:

  • (1) the correlation of the residuals of y and of x after least squares regression on Z;

  • (2) the recursion Eq. 3.6.1 applied to sample correlations (one conditioning variable) or repeatedly (several);

  • (3) −w12/w11w22 computed from the inverse of the sample covariance or correlation matrix of (y,x,conditioning variables);

  • (4) sign(t)t2/(t2+n−p), where t is the t statistic of x in the regression of y on [Z,x] with p columns (Exercise (B3)).

Example (Employment and unemployment)

Longley’s macroeconomic series record, among other things, total employment and the number unemployed in the United States for the 16 years 1947–1962. Their correlation is 0.502: years with more people employed had more people unemployed. Both series grew with the population. Their correlations with the calendar year are 0.971 and 0.668. Adjusting for year, the partial correlation is −0.829. Once the common trend is removed, a year with unemployment above its trend is a year with employment below its trend, as one would expect. Figure 3.6.1 shows both scatterplots. The listing computes the partial correlation in all four ways above. They agree to ten decimal places. The slope in panel (b), −0.996, is the coefficient of unemployment in the regression of employment on unemployment and year, as Theorem 6.6.2 requires. Its t statistic is −5.34.

Removing a linear time trend before correlating two series is the problem that Frisch and Waugh (1933) solved, and it is a special case of partial correlation.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.longley.load_pandas().data

emp = data["TOTEMP"].to_numpy() / 1000       # thousands -> millions of persons
unemp = data["UNEMP"].to_numpy() / 1000
year = data["YEAR"].to_numpy()
n = len(emp)
Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), year])      # the variables we adjust for

def resid(v, Z):
    coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Z, v, rcond=None)
    return v - Z @ coef

# 1. correlation of residuals (detrended series)
r_resid = np.corrcoef(resid(emp, Z), resid(unemp, Z))[0, 1]

# 2. the one-variable recursion from ordinary correlations
R = np.corrcoef(np.vstack([emp, unemp, year]))
r12, r13, r23 = R[0, 1], R[0, 2], R[1, 2]
r_formula = (r12 - r13 * r23) / np.sqrt((1 - r13**2) * (1 - r23**2))

# 3. from the inverse of the correlation (or covariance) matrix
W = np.linalg.inv(R)
r_precision = -W[0, 1] / np.sqrt(W[0, 0] * W[1, 1])

# 4. from the t statistic of unemployment in the regression of emp on (1, unemp, year)
fit = sm.OLS(emp, np.column_stack([Z, unemp])).fit()
t = fit.tvalues[2]
r_t = np.sign(t) * np.sqrt(t**2 / (t**2 + n - 3))

print(f"marginal r = {r12:.4f}")
print(f"partial r:  {r_resid:.10f} {r_formula:.10f} {r_precision:.10f} {r_t:.10f}")

Remark (Adjusting is not always right). A partial correlation answers a precise question: how are the parts of two variables not linearly explained by Y2 related? Whether that is the right question depends on what Y2 is. Adjusting for a common cause, as the year stands in for population growth here, removes a spurious association. Adjusting for a common effect can create an association between variables that are independent. Chapter 25 treats the difference. Under normality, the sampling distribution of a sample partial correlation given q variables is that of an ordinary sample correlation from n−q observations, a result used for tests in Chapter 14.

3.6.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that ρ12⋅3=0 iff ρ12=ρ13ρ23. Find ρ12⋅3 when ρ12=0 and ρ13=ρ23=0.6, and interpret.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let X, Z and e be independent, with X,Z∼N(0,1) and e∼N(0,τ2), and let C=X+Z+e. Show that ρXZ=0 but ρXZ⋅C=−1/(1+τ2). What happens as τ→0 and as τ→∞?

Solution. The covariance matrix of (X,Z,C) is (101011112+τ2). Conditioning on C, σXZ⋅C=0−12+τ2,σXX⋅C=σZZ⋅C=1−12+τ2=1+τ22+τ2, so ρXZ⋅C=−1/(1+τ2). As τ→0 it tends to −1: if X+Z is known exactly, a larger X forces a smaller Z. As τ→∞ it tends to 0, since C carries little information about X+Z. Two independent causes become dependent once their common effect is held fixed.

Exercise (B2)

Let Σ be positive definite. Show that the coefficient of Yj in the best linear predictor of Y1 from all the other entries is −ω1j/ω11. Deduce that ρ1j⋅rest2 equals the product of the coefficient of Yj for predicting Y1 and the coefficient of Y1 for predicting Yj, each from all the other variables.

Solution. Put Y1 first. By Exercise (B5) with a scalar first block, the coefficient vector of the best linear predictor of Y1 from the rest is −Ω12/ω11, whose jth entry is −ω1j/ω11. Likewise the coefficient of Y1 in the predictor of Yj is −ωj1/ωjj. Their product is ω1j2/(ω11ωjj), which is ρ1j⋅rest2 by Proposition 3.6.3.

Exercise (B3)

Let D be an n×k data matrix whose columns are centred, and let S=D⊤D. Show, using Theorem 6.6.2 and the partitioned inverse, that −w12/w11w22, computed from S−1=(wij), equals the correlation of the residuals of columns 1 and 2 after regression on the remaining columns.

Exercise (B4)

Using the Longley data of Example (Employment and unemployment), compute the partial correlation of employment and unemployment given both year and GNP, in at least two of the four ways. Draw the corresponding residual scatterplot. Does adjusting for GNP as well as year change the conclusion?

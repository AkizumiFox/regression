3.5  Conditional distributions

Throughout this section Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) is split into Y1∈Rn1 and Y2∈Rn2, with μ=(μ1μ2),Σ=(Σ11Σ12Σ21Σ22),Σ21=Σ12⊤, as in Theorem 2.5.1. The question is what Y1 looks like once Y2 has been observed. The answer is the probabilistic core of regression.

3.5.1. Partial covariance with a generalized inverse

The natural answer involves Σ22−1, but Σ22 may be singular, for instance when Y2 contains a sum and its summands. A generalized inverse (Definition 1.3.1) removes the need for a separate treatment. The facts that make this work were proved for arbitrary random vectors in Section 2.5. We collect them in the notation of this chapter.

Lemma 3.5.1

Let Σ be nonnegative definite and partitioned as above, and let G be any generalized inverse of Σ22. Then:

  • (a) C(Σ21)⊆C(Σ22), so Σ21=Σ22H for some matrix H;

  • (b) Σ12GΣ22=Σ12 and Σ22GΣ21=Σ21;

  • (c) Σ12GΣ21=H⊤Σ22H, which does not depend on the choice of G and is nonnegative definite.

The matrix Σ11⋅2=Σ11−Σ12Σ22−Σ21, the Schur complement of Σ22 in Σ, is therefore well defined. It is the partial covariance matrix of Y1 given Y2 in the sense of Theorem 2.5.2.

Proof. By Theorem 3.1.2, Σ is the covariance matrix of some random vector, so (a) and (b) are Theorem 2.5.1(c). For (c), Σ12GΣ21=H⊤Σ22GΣ22H=H⊤Σ22H.

3.5.2. The conditional distribution

Theorem 3.5.2 (Conditional distribution)

Let Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) be partitioned as above, let G be a generalized inverse of Σ22, and put B=Σ12G. Then:

  • (a) W=Y1−μ1−B(Y2−μ2) is independent of Y2 and W∼Nn1(0,Σ11⋅2);

  • (b) the conditional distribution of Y1 given Y2=y2 is

    Y1∣Y2=y2 ∼ Nn1(μ1+Σ12Σ22−(y2−μ2), Σ11−Σ12Σ22−Σ21),
    (3.5.1) 
        and for \( \y_2 \) in \( \bmu_2+\C(\bSigma_{22}) \), where \( \Y_2 \) lies with probability
    one, the conditional mean does not depend on the choice of \( \G \);

  • (c) if Σ is positive definite, then Σ22−=Σ22−1, Σ11⋅2 is positive definite, Σ11⋅2−1 is the leading n1×n1 block of Σ−1, and detΣ=detΣ22detΣ11⋅2.

Proof.

  • (a) W=[I,−B](Y−μ), so (W⊤,Y2⊤)⊤ is an affine function of Y and is jointly normal (Theorem 3.3.1). The vector W is the error of the best linear predictor of Y1 from Y2, so by Theorem 2.5.2(a,b) it has mean zero, Cov(W,Y2)=0 and Cov(W)=Σ11⋅2. These are moment calculations, valid for any random vector. What normality adds is that zero covariance becomes independence (Theorem 3.4.1), and that W, a linear function of Y, is normal.

  • (b) Write Y1=μ1+B(Y2−μ2)+W. The first two terms are a function of Y2, and W is independent of Y2. For independent W and Y2, the conditional distribution of h(W,Y2) given Y2=y2 is the distribution of h(W,y2) (Billingsley 1995). With h(w,y2)=μ1+B(y2−μ2)+w, this is the normal distribution

  1. If y2−μ2=Σ22k, then B(y2−μ2)=Σ12GΣ22k=Σ12k, which does not involve G.
  • (c) If Σ is positive definite, so is its principal submatrix Σ22, and its only generalized inverse is its inverse. By the block determinant formula (Theorem 1.6.3), which needs only Σ22 nonsingular, detΣ=detΣ22detΣ11⋅2. The left side and detΣ22 are positive, so Σ11⋅2 is nonsingular. It is nonnegative definite, being Cov(W) by (a), hence positive definite. With both Σ22 and its Schur complement nonsingular, the partitioned-inverse formula (Theorem 1.6.4) gives Σ11⋅2−1 as the leading block of Σ−1.

When Σ is positive definite, (b) can also be read off the densities. By (c), Σ11⋅2 is positive definite, so W has a density. The map (y1,y2)↦(w,y2) has unit Jacobian, so fY(y1,y2)=fW(y1−μ1−B(y2−μ2))fY2(y2) by independence, and dividing by fY2(y2) gives the conditional density.

Idea. Every jointly normal vector splits as Y1=μ1+Σ12Σ22−(Y2−μ2)⏟function of Y2+W⏟independent of Y2. The first term is a linear regression of Y1 on Y2. The second is a normal error whose distribution is the same whatever value Y2 takes.

3.5.3. What the theorem says

Three features of 1 are special to the normal distribution, and each is an assumption of the classical linear model.

  • (i) The regression is linear. E(Y1∣Y2=y2) is an affine function of y2, with coefficient matrix Σ12Σ22−.

  • (ii) The errors are homoscedastic. The conditional covariance Σ11⋅2 is the same for every y2. Observing Y2 tells us where Y1 is centred, but not how spread out it is.

  • (iii) Conditioning cannot increase uncertainty. Σ11−Σ11⋅2=Σ12Σ22−Σ21 is nonnegative definite (Lemma 3.5.1(c)), so every linear combination a⊤Y1 has conditional variance at most its unconditional variance. The reduction is zero iff Σ12=0.

None of these holds in general. In the mixture of Example (A mixture of two correlations), given Y1=y1 the variable Y2 is an equal mixture of N(ρy1,1−ρ2) and N(−ρy1,1−ρ2). Its conditional mean is 0 for every y1, but its conditional variance is 1−ρ2+ρ2y12, which grows with |y1|. Linearity and constant variance are not consequences of normal margins or of zero correlation. They are consequences of joint normality.

One response, several predictors. The case used most in this book has a scalar response Y and a vector X of k predictors, jointly normal, with Var(Y)=σY2, σXY=Cov(X,Y) and ΣXX=Cov(X) positive definite. Theorem 3.5.2 says

Y=α+β⊤X+e,β=ΣXX−1σXY,α=μY−β⊤μX,e∼N(0,σ2) independent of X,
(3.5.2)

with σ2=σY2−σXY⊤ΣXX−1σXY=σY2(1−ρY⋅X2), where ρY⋅X2=σXY⊤ΣXX−1σXY/σY2 is the population squared multiple correlation (Proposition 2.5.3). So if observations (Yi,Xi) are drawn independently from a joint normal distribution, then conditionally on the predictors, the normal linear model with independent homoscedastic errors holds exactly. Inference that treats the predictors as fixed is valid for random predictors, as Chapter 14 develops.

The bivariate case. With k=1, correlation ρ and standard deviations σX,σY, E(Y∣X=x)=μY+ρσYσX(x−μX),Var(Y∣X=x)=σY2(1−ρ2). In standard units, (E(Y∣X=x)−μY)/σY=ρ(x−μX)/σX. The predicted response is fewer standard deviations from its mean than the predictor is from its mean, whenever |ρ|<1. This is regression toward the mean, Galton’s observation about the heights of parents and children (Galton 1886), and the origin of the word “regression”. It is also a warning. Regressing X on Y is a different line, E(X∣Y=y)=μX+ρ(σX/σY)(y−μY), and when 0<|ρ|<1 neither line is the major axis of the density contours.

Example (Two regression lines)

Take μX=1, μY=2, σX=1, σY=1.5 and ρ=0.7. The regression of Y on X has slope 1.05 and residual standard deviation 1.071. Drawn in the same (x,y) plane, the regression of X on Y has slope 2.14, and the major axis of the ellipses lies between them, with slope 1.76. Figure 3.5.1 shows the three lines. The line E(Y∣X=x) passes through the points where each contour ellipse has a vertical tangent (Exercise (B2)): on a vertical line X=x, the density is highest at the conditional mean. A least squares line fitted to 60 draws has slope 1.12 (standard error 0.16). It estimates the regression of Y on X, not the major axis.

Example (Holding a third variable fixed)

Let Y∼N3(μ,Σ) with μ=(10−1),Σ=(412132222). Conditioning Y1 on Y2 alone gives slope σ12/σ22=13 and conditional variance 113. Conditioning on (Y2,Y3), the listing computes B=Σ12Σ22−1 in exact arithmetic and finds E(Y1∣Y2=y2,Y3=y3)=3−y2+2y3,Var(Y1∣Y2,Y3)=1. The coefficient of y2 changes sign. Among outcomes with the same Y3, larger Y2 goes with smaller Y1. Across all outcomes, larger Y2 goes with larger Y1, because both tend to be large when Y3 is large. The conditional variance 1 also equals detΣ/detΣ22=2/2, as Theorem 3.5.2(c) predicts.

from fractions import Fraction as F
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(305)

Sigma = np.array([[sx**2, rho * sx * sy], [rho * sx * sy, sy**2]])
slope_y_on_x = rho * sy / sx                    # E(Y | X = x)

cond_sd = sy * np.sqrt(1 - rho**2)
n = 60
XY = rng.multivariate_normal([mx, my], Sigma, size=n)
b_ls, a_ls = np.polyfit(XY[:, 0], XY[:, 1], 1)
Linv = np.linalg.inv(np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma))
theta = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 200_001)
circle = np.vstack([np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)])
L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)
big = rng.multivariate_normal([mx, my], Sigma, size=4_000_000)
x0 = 2.0
sl = big[np.abs(big[:, 0] - x0) < 0.01, 1]
resid = XY[:, 1] - a_ls - b_ls * XY[:, 0]

lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(Sigma)
slope_major = U[1, 1] / U[0, 1]

xs = np.linspace(-2.2, 4.2, 2)
ys = np.linspace(-3.0, 7.0, 2)

mu = [F(1), F(0), F(-1)]
S = [[F(4), F(1), F(2)],
     [F(1), F(3), F(2)],
     [F(2), F(2), F(2)]]

def inv2(M):
    det = M[0][0] * M[1][1] - M[0][1] * M[1][0]
    return [[M[1][1] / det, -M[0][1] / det], [-M[1][0] / det, M[0][0] / det]]

# Y1 given (Y2, Y3): B = sigma_12 Sigma_22^{-1}, variance sigma_11 - B sigma_21
S22inv = inv2([[S[1][1], S[1][2]], [S[2][1], S[2][2]]])
s12 = [S[0][1], S[0][2]]
B = [s12[0] * S22inv[0][j] + s12[1] * S22inv[1][j] for j in range(2)]
var_1_23 = S[0][0] - (B[0] * s12[0] + B[1] * s12[1])
const = mu[0] - B[0] * mu[1] - B[1] * mu[2]
print("E(Y1 | y2, y3) =", const, "+", B[0], "y2 +", B[1], "y3;  variance", var_1_23)

# (Y1, Y2) given Y3, and the partial correlation of Y1 and Y2 given Y3
C = [[S[i][j] - S[i][2] * S[2][j] / S[2][2] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]
print("Cov((Y1, Y2) | Y3) =", C)

3.5.4. The conditional mean is the best predictor

Section 6.11 defines the best linear predictor L(Y1∣Y2) as a projection, and shows that the conditional mean is the best predictor among all square-integrable functions of Y2. For a scalar response, the gap between the two is the approximation error of Proposition 6.11.2(c). Under joint normality the gap is zero.

Proposition 3.5.3

Let Y be jointly normal and partitioned as above, and let m(Y2)=μ1+Σ12Σ22−(Y2−μ2). For every function g with E‖g(Y2)‖2<∞, E‖Y1−g(Y2)‖2=tr(Σ11⋅2)+E‖m(Y2)−g(Y2)‖2. So m(Y2) minimizes the mean squared prediction error over all predictors, it equals the best linear predictor of Theorem 6.11.1 (entry by entry, when Σ22 is positive definite), and the minimum is tr(Σ11⋅2).

Proof. Y1−g(Y2)=W+(m(Y2)−g(Y2)) with W as in Theorem 3.5.2. The cross term E[W⊤(m(Y2)−g(Y2))] vanishes, because W has mean zero and is independent of Y2. And E‖W‖2=trCov(W)=tr(Σ11⋅2). The minimizer m is affine, so it is also the best affine predictor, which is the best linear predictor.

The proof is short because independence of W and Y2 is much stronger than the zero correlation that defines a projection. For non-normal vectors only zero correlation is available, and the best linear predictor is merely the best linear predictor.

3.5.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For μ and Σ of Example (Holding a third variable fixed), find the conditional distribution of (Y1,Y2) given Y3=y3 and of Y3 given (Y1,Y2)=(y1,y2). Verify that the conditional variances are smaller than the unconditional ones.

Exercise (A2)

In the setting of Eq. 3.5.2, let Ω be the inverse of Cov((Y,X⊤)⊤). Show that the row of Ω belonging to Y is (1,−β⊤)/σ2, where σ2=σY2(1−ρY⋅X2). So the regression coefficients and the error variance can be read off one row of the precision matrix.

Solution. Put Y first and apply Exercise (B5) with the scalar block Y and Y2=X. The conditional variance is ωYY−1=Var(Y∣X)=σ2, and the coefficient vector of the conditional mean is −ωYY−1ΩYX=β⊤. Hence ωYY=1/σ2 and ΩYX=−β⊤/σ2. By Eq. 3.5.2, σ2=σY2(1−ρY⋅X2).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Suppose Y2∼N(μ2,Σ22) and that, given Y2=y2, Y1 is N(a+By2,S) with a, B and S not depending on y2. Show that (Y1⊤,Y2⊤)⊤ is jointly normal and find its mean and covariance. Explain why Example (A mixture of two correlations) does not contradict this.

Solution. Condition on Y2: Eet1⊤Y1+t2⊤Y2=E[et2⊤Y2et1⊤(a+BY2)+12t1⊤St1]=et1⊤a+12t1⊤St1MY2(t2+B⊤t1). Inserting MY2 from Theorem 3.2.1, the exponent is linear plus 12t⊤Ct with C=(S+BΣ22B⊤BΣ22Σ22B⊤Σ22), so the pair is normal with mean ((a+Bμ2)⊤,μ2⊤)⊤ and covariance C. In Example (A mixture of two correlations), the conditional distribution of Y2 given Y1 is a mixture, not a normal distribution, and its variance depends on y1.

Exercise (B2)

Show that for a bivariate normal density with |ρ|<1, the points of each contour ellipse at which the tangent is vertical lie on the line y=E(Y∣X=x). Explain the result in terms of the conditional density of Y given X=x.

Solution. Write the ellipse as {μ+u:u⊤Σ−1u=c2}. At a point with vertical tangent, the normal vector Σ−1u is horizontal, so Σ−1u=λ(1,0)⊤ and u=λ(σX2,ρσXσY)⊤. The slope u2/u1=ρσY/σX is that of the conditional mean line, and both such points (λ>0 and λ<0) lie on it. Along the vertical line X=x, the joint density is proportional to the conditional density of Y, which is highest at E(Y∣X=x). The contour that just touches the vertical line touches it at the point of highest density on that line.

Exercise (B3)

Let Z1,…,Zn be independent N(0,1) and Sk=Z1+⋯+Zk. Show that (S1,…,Sn) is jointly normal with Cov(Sj,Sk)=min(j,k). Find the conditional distribution of Sj given Sn=s, and comment on its variance as a function of j.

Solution. (S1,…,Sn)⊤=LZ with L lower triangular and all entries on and below the diagonal equal to one, so the vector is normal with covariance LL⊤, whose (j,k) entry counts the common indices, min(j,k). By Theorem 3.5.2 with Y1=Sj, Y2=Sn, Sj∣Sn=s ∼ N(jns, j−j2n)=N(jns, j(n−j)n). The conditional mean interpolates linearly between 0 and s. The variance is zero at both ends, where the value is known, and largest in the middle.

Exercise (B4)

Let (Y,X1,X2) be normal with positive definite covariance, and let Y2=(X1,X2,X1+X2)⊤. Show that Σ22 is singular. Using a generalized inverse, show that the conditional distribution of Y given Y2 from Theorem 3.5.2 coincides with its conditional distribution given (X1,X2).

Exercise (B5)

With Ω=Σ−1 partitioned like Σ, show that E(Y1∣Y2=y2)=μ1−Ω11−1Ω12(y2−μ2) and Cov(Y1∣Y2)=Ω11−1.

Solution. By Theorem 1.6.4, Ω11=Σ11⋅2−1 and Ω12=−Σ11⋅2−1Σ12Σ22−1. Hence −Ω11−1Ω12=Σ12Σ22−1 and Ω11−1=Σ11⋅2. Substitute into 1.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Assume Σ is positive definite. Prove Theorem 3.5.2(b) by dividing the joint density by the marginal density of Y2 directly, using the partitioned inverse and the block determinant formula.

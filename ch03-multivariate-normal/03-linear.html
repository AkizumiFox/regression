3.3  Linear transformations and marginals

3.3.1. Affine maps

Theorem 3.3.1 (Affine transformations)

Let Y∼Nn(μ,Σ), let A be any m×n matrix and b∈Rm. Then AY+b∼Nm(Aμ+b, AΣA⊤).

Proof. For c∈Rm, c⊤(AY+b)=(A⊤c)⊤Y+c⊤b is a linear combination of Y plus a constant, which is normal by Definition 3.1.1 and Lemma 3.1.1(c). So AY+b is normal, and its mean and covariance are given by Theorem 2.2.1.

The proof is one line because the definition was chosen to make it one line. There is no rank condition on A. If A has more rows than rank(Σ), the result is a singular normal vector, and that is allowed. With a density-based definition, the same theorem needs A to have full row rank and Σ to be positive definite, and the most useful cases fall outside it. The moment generating function gives a second proof: Eet⊤(AY+b)=et⊤bMY(A⊤t)=exp⁡{t⊤(Aμ+b)+12t⊤AΣA⊤t}.

Corollary 3.3.2 (Marginal distributions)

Every subvector of a normal vector is normal. If Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) and Y1 consists of the entries with indices in a set I, then Y1∼N(μI,ΣII), where μI and ΣII keep the entries, rows and columns indexed by I. In particular Yi∼N(μi,σii).

Proof. Y1=AY, where the rows of A are the coordinate vectors ei⊤, i∈I. Then Aμ=μI and AΣA⊤=ΣII.

Corollary 3.3.3 (Standardization and rotation)

Let Y∼Nn(μ,Σ).

  • (a) If Σ is positive definite and Σ=AA⊤ with A square, then A−1(Y−μ)∼Nn(0,I). Its entries are independent standard normal variables.

  • (b) If Σ=σ2I and Q is an n×n orthogonal matrix, then Q⊤Y∼Nn(Q⊤μ,σ2I).

Proof. Both follow from Theorem 3.3.1: A−1AA⊤(A−1)⊤=I and Q⊤(σ2I)Q=σ2I. Independence in (a) was shown in the proof of Proposition 3.2.3.

Part (b) is the probabilistic basis of the canonical form of the linear model. Spherical normal errors look the same in every orthonormal coordinate system, so one may rotate to coordinates adapted to C(X), in which fitted values and residuals occupy separate independent coordinates. Chapter 4 uses this rotation to find the distributions of sums of squares.

3.3.2. The rank of a normal vector

Theorem 3.1.2 writes a normal vector as an affine function of independent standard normals in distribution. When Σ is singular the representation can be made exact, with as few standard normals as the rank.

Proposition 3.3.4

Let Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) with rank(Σ)=r≥1, and let Σ=QΛQ⊤, where Q is n×r with orthonormal columns and Λ=diag(λ1,…,λr) holds the positive eigenvalues. Put W=Λ−1/2Q⊤(Y−μ). Then W∼Nr(0,Ir), and Y=μ+QΛ1/2Wwith probability one.

Proof. By Theorem 3.3.1, W is normal with covariance Λ−1/2Q⊤QΛQ⊤QΛ−1/2=Ir. Next, QΛ1/2W=QQ⊤(Y−μ), and QQ⊤ is the symmetric idempotent matrix with range C(Σ) (the orthogonal projection onto it, in the language of Chapter 6). Since Y−μ∈C(Σ) with probability one (Theorem 2.2.3), QQ⊤(Y−μ)=Y−μ with probability one.

So a normal vector of rank r is exactly r independent standard normals placed in Rn by an injective affine map. Exercise (B4) gives the same construction for any random vector, with uncorrelated entries in place of independent normal ones. Singular normal vectors are lower-dimensional normal vectors in disguise.

Example (Least squares under normal errors)

Let Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I) with X of full column rank p, and let M=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤, the symmetric idempotent matrix of rank p with MX=X (Chapter 5 and Chapter 6). By Theorem 3.3.1:

  • (a) β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤Y∼Np(β,σ2(X⊤X)−1), a nonsingular normal vector;

  • (b) λ⊤β^∼N(λ⊤β,σ2λ⊤(X⊤X)−1λ) for every λ∈Rp;

  • (c) the fitted values Y^=MY∼Nn(Xβ,σ2M), singular of rank p;

  • (d) the residuals ε^=(I−M)Y∼Nn(0,σ2(I−M)), singular of rank n−p.

No density for Y^ or ε^ was needed, and none exists.

3.3.3. Independent normal vectors

Lemma 3.3.5

If Y1∼Nn1(μ1,Σ1) and Y2∼Nn2(μ2,Σ2) are independent, then (Y1⊤,Y2⊤)⊤ is jointly normal with mean (μ1⊤,μ2⊤)⊤ and block-diagonal covariance diag(Σ1,Σ2). Consequently, if also n1=n2, then Y1+Y2∼N(μ1+μ2,Σ1+Σ2).

Proof. a1⊤Y1+a2⊤Y2 is a sum of independent normal variables, which is normal by Lemma 3.1.1(c). Independence makes the cross-covariance zero. The sum is [I,I] applied to the stacked vector.

Independence is essential in this lemma. Two normal vectors defined on the same probability space need not be jointly normal, as we now see.

3.3.4. Normal marginals are not enough

Corollary 3.3.2 cannot be reversed. A vector whose entries are each normal need not be jointly normal. Two examples make the point, one extreme and one realistic.

Example (A random sign)

Let Z∼N(0,1) and let S be independent of Z with Pr(S=1)=Pr(S=−1)=12. Put W=SZ. By symmetry of Z, W∼N(0,1). Also Cov(Z,W)=E(S)E(Z2)=0. But Z+W=(1+S)Z equals zero with probability 12 and is otherwise N(0,4). A variable with an atom at zero and a continuous part is not normal, so (Z,W) is not jointly normal. And Z and W are far from independent: |W|=|Z| always.

Example (A mixture of two correlations)

Fix 0<ρ<1. With probability 12 draw (Y1,Y2) from the bivariate normal with unit variances and correlation ρ, and otherwise from the one with correlation −ρ. Both components have N(0,1) margins, so the mixture does too, exactly. The covariance is 12ρ+12(−ρ)=0. The pair is nevertheless dependent: since E(Y12Y22)=1+2r2 for a standard bivariate normal with correlation r, the squares have correlation ρ2, not zero. The direction a=(1,1)⊤/2 exposes the failure of joint normality. S=(Y1+Y2)/2 is an equal mixture of N(0,1+ρ) and N(0,1−ρ). It has variance 1 but kurtosis ES4=32{(1+ρ)2+(1−ρ)2}=3(1+ρ2), where a normal variable would have 3.

Figure 3.3.1 shows a simulation with ρ=0.8 and 200,000 draws. Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests of normality for the two margins give p-values 0.84 and 0.29. The sample correlation is −0.0033, the correlation of the squares is 0.640 (theory 0.64), and the kurtosis of S is 4.88 (theory 4.92). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance of S from N(0,1) is 0.067, which with this many draws rejects normality at any conventional level.

import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate, stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(302)
rho, N = 0.8, 200_000

sign = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=N)          # which component each draw uses
Z1, Z2 = rng.standard_normal((2, N))
Y1 = Z1
Y2 = sign * rho * Z1 + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * Z2  # corr(Y1, Y2 | sign) = sign * rho

S = (Y1 + Y2) / np.sqrt(2)                     # a direction that is not normal
print("marginal KS p-values:", stats.kstest(Y1, "norm").pvalue, stats.kstest(Y2, "norm").pvalue)
print("correlation:", np.corrcoef(Y1, Y2)[0, 1])
print("corr of squares:", np.corrcoef(Y1**2, Y2**2)[0, 1])
print("kurtosis of S:", stats.kurtosis(S, fisher=False), " theory:", 3 * (1 + rho**2))
print("KS p-value of S:", stats.kstest(S, "norm").pvalue)

Idea. Normality of a vector is a property of every direction, not of the coordinate axes. Normal margins with zero correlation can hide strong dependence. The theorems of the next two sections, which turn zero covariance into independence and give linear conditional means, need joint normality. They are not about variables that merely happen to be normal one at a time.

3.3.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let Y∼N3(μ,Σ) with μ=(0,1,2)⊤ and Σ=(210121012). Find the joint distribution of U=Y1+Y2+Y3, V=Y1−Y3 and W=Y1−2Y2+Y3. Which of the pairs are independent? Is V independent of (U,W)?

Exercise (A2)

In Example (Least squares under normal errors), show that the ith residual is N(0,σ2(1−mii)), where mii is the ith diagonal entry of M, and that it is zero with probability one iff mii=1.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let Y∼Nn(μ1,σ2I) and let Q be an orthogonal matrix whose first row is n−1/21⊤. Show that the entries of V=QY are independent, with V1∼N(nμ,σ2) and Vi∼N(0,σ2) for i≥2. Show that ∑i(Yi−Y¯)2=∑i≥2Vi2, and conclude again that Y¯ and S2 are independent.

Exercise (B2)

Let Z∼N(0,1) and, for c>0, let W=Z if |Z|≤c and W=−Z otherwise. Show that W∼N(0,1), that there is a c with Cov(Z,W)=0, and that (Z,W) is not jointly normal for any c.

Solution. Because Z and −Z have the same distribution and the event {|Z|>c} is unchanged by the sign change, Pr(W≤w)=Pr(Z≤w,|Z|≤c)+Pr(−Z≤w,|Z|>c)=Pr(Z≤w). Next, Cov(Z,W)=E(Z2;|Z|≤c)−E(Z2;|Z|>c)=2E(Z2;|Z|≤c)−1, which is continuous and increasing in c, tends to −1 as c→0 and to 1 as c→∞, and so vanishes at some c. Finally 𝟙Z+W=2Z1{|Z|≤c} equals 0 with probability Pr(|Z|>c)∈(0,1) but is not constant. A normal variable is either constant or has a density, so Z+W is not normal.

Exercise (B3)

For the pair (Z,W) of Example (A random sign), find every a≠0 for which a1Z+a2W is normal. Hint: an equal mixture of N(0,v1) and N(0,v2) has fourth moment 32(v12+v22). Conclude that (Z,W) is normal along exactly two lines through the origin, the coordinate axes.

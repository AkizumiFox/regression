16.1  Two-way additive models

Two factors are observed together. Factor A has levels i=1,…,a and factor B has levels j=1,…,b, with a,b≥2. Every one of the ab combinations, or cells, is observed the same number m≥1 of times. We write yijk for the kth observation in cell (i,j), so that n=abm. Example (Additive two-factor model, one observation per cell) found the projections of the additive model for m=1 by a direct argument. This section redoes it with the tools used throughout the chapter, and adds replication, estimable functions, tests, and a test of additivity with one observation per cell.

16.1.1. Averaging and centring matrices

Stack the observations with the replicate index k running fastest, then j, then i: y=(y111,…,y11m, y121,…,y12m, …, yab1,…,yabm)⊤. With this order a matrix of the form Ea⊗Eb⊗Em acts on each index separately, as in Example (The balanced two-way layout). For k≥1 write J¯k=1k1k1k⊤,Ck=Ik−J¯k. The averaging matrix J¯k replaces each entry of a k-vector by the mean of the entries. The centring matrix Ck subtracts that mean. With the usual dot notation for means, the following products replace yijk by (Ia⊗Ib⊗J¯m)y: y¯ij⋅,(Ia⊗J¯b⊗J¯m)y: y¯i⋅⋅,(J¯a⊗Ib⊗J¯m)y: y¯⋅j⋅,(J¯a⊗J¯b⊗J¯m)y: y¯⋅⋅⋅, the cell, row, column and grand means. Each operator averages over the indices where it has a J¯ and leaves the others alone. Every projection in this chapter is a Kronecker product of factors I, J¯ and C, and the following lemma is all the algebra they need.

Lemma 16.1.1 (Kronecker products of averaging and centring matrices)

Let l1,…,lt≥1.

  • (a) J¯k and Ck are symmetric and idempotent, with ranks 1 and k−1, and J¯kCk=0, J¯k+Ck=Ik.

  • (b) If each Es is one of Ils, J¯ls, Cls, then E1⊗⋯⊗Et is an orthogonal projection whose rank is the product of the ranks of the Es.

  • (c) If each Es and each Fs is one of J¯ls, Cls, and Es≠Fs for at least one s, then (E1⊗⋯⊗Et)(F1⊗⋯⊗Ft)=0.

  • (d) The 2t products E1⊗⋯⊗Et with each Es∈{J¯ls,Cls} sum to I.

Proof.

  • (a) J¯k is symmetric and J¯k2=k−21(1⊤1)1⊤=J¯k. Its trace is 1. Then Ck=I−J¯k is the complementary projection, with trace k−1, and J¯kCk=J¯k−J¯k2=0. Ranks equal traces by Proposition 6.3.4. (b) By Proposition 1.10.1(e) a Kronecker product of symmetric idempotent matrices is symmetric and idempotent, and by Proposition 1.10.1(c) its rank is the product of the ranks. (c) By the mixed-product rule the product equals E1F1⊗⋯⊗EtFt, and the factor with Es≠Fs is J¯C=0 or CJ¯=0. (d) Expand I=(J¯l1+Cl1)⊗⋯⊗(J¯lt+Clt) by bilinearity of the Kronecker product.

The rank k−1 of Ck is the source of every “levels minus one” in the analysis of variance, and part (d) is the source of every analysis of variance table in this chapter. Part (b) allows k=1: then J¯1=1 and C1=0.

import itertools

import numpy as np

def Jbar(k):
    return np.full((k, k), 1.0 / k)          # averaging: replaces a k-vector by its mean


def Cen(k):
    return np.eye(k) - Jbar(k)               # centring: subtracts the mean


def kron_all(mats):
    out = np.ones((1, 1))
    for A in mats:
        out = np.kron(out, A)
    return out


def factorial_projections(levels, m):
    """P_S for every subset S of the factors (a 0/1 tuple), and the error projection."""
    P = {}
    for S in itertools.product([0, 1], repeat=len(levels)):
        P[S] = kron_all([Cen(l) if s else Jbar(l) for l, s in zip(levels, S)] + [Jbar(m)])
    P["error"] = kron_all([np.eye(l) for l in levels] + [Cen(m)])
    return P


def rank(A):
    return int(round(np.trace(A)))           # rank = trace for a projection

16.1.2. The additive model

The additive two-way model is

E(yijk)=μ+αi+βj,Cov(Y)=σ2I,
(16.1.1)

with model matrix X=[1n, ZA, ZB], where ZA=Ia⊗1b⊗1m and ZB=1a⊗Ib⊗1m are the indicator matrices of the two factors. It says that the difference between two levels of A is the same in every column and, symmetrically, that the difference between two levels of B is the same in every row. The model has 1+a+b parameters and, by Theorem 8.6.1(a), rank a+b−1. Define

P0=J¯a⊗J¯b⊗J¯m,PA=Ca⊗J¯b⊗J¯m,PB=J¯a⊗Cb⊗J¯m,
(16.1.2)

and R=I−P0−PA−PB. By the table of means above, PAy has entry y¯i⋅⋅−y¯⋅⋅⋅ in every position of row i, and PBy has entry y¯⋅j⋅−y¯⋅⋅⋅ in every position of column j.

16.1.3. Additivity with one observation per cell

Many two-factor data sets have one observation per cell, such as methods each run once on several test problems. By Example (Additive two-factor model, one observation per cell) the residual space of the additive model then has dimension (a−1)(b−1), and the residuals are the interaction terms yij−y¯i⋅−y¯⋅j+y¯⋅⋅. The residual sum of squares then serves two purposes. It estimates σ2 if the model is additive, and it is the only place where nonadditivity could show. There is no model-free estimate of σ2, and no test of additivity against a general alternative.

A test is still possible against a specific kind of departure. Suppose the cell means are additive on some other scale, μij=f(ν+ai+bj), for a smooth increasing function f and small effects ai, bj with ∑ai=∑bj=0. A second-order Taylor expansion about ν gives μij≈f(ν)+f′(ν)(ai+bj)+12f″(ν)(ai2+bj2)+f″(ν)aibj. The terms in ai2 and bj2 are additive. Only the product term is not. To first order the row and column effects of μij are αi≈f′(ν)ai and βj≈f′(ν)bj, so the interaction is approximately

μij−μ¯i⋅−μ¯⋅j+μ¯⋅⋅≈θαiβj,θ=f″(ν)f′(ν)2.
(16.1.3)

Tukey (1949) proposed to look for interaction of exactly this shape. The row and column effects are estimated by ri=y¯i⋅−y¯⋅⋅ and cj=y¯⋅j−y¯⋅⋅ (with m=1 we drop the third subscript). The residuals are then regressed on the products ricj. The table (ricj) has zero row and column sums, so it lies in the residual space. The regression through the origin of the residual table on it has slope and sum of squares

θ^=∑i,jricjyij∑iri2∑jcj2,SSN=(∑i,jricjyij)2∑iri2∑jcj2.
(16.1.4)

The regressor is computed from the same data, so Proposition 14.7.1 does not apply. But a regressor that is a function of the fitted values alone leaves the F test exact (Theorem 14.7.2), and part (f) of the next theorem shows that Tukey’s regressor is of this kind.

16.1.4. Estimation and tests

The following theorem collects what the additive model delivers in a balanced layout, with and without replication.

Theorem 16.1.2 (The additive two-way model)

Assume Eq. 16.1.1 with m≥1 observations in every cell.

  • (a) P0,PA,PB and R are mutually orthogonal projections summing to I, with ranks 1, a−1, b−1 and n−a−b+1. The projection onto C(X) is M=P0+PA+PB, and the fitted values are y^ijk=y¯i⋅⋅+y¯⋅j⋅−y¯⋅⋅⋅.

  • (b) λ0μ+∑iλiαi+∑jκjβj is estimable iff λ0=∑iλi=∑jκj. The BLUE of a contrast ∑iciαi is ∑iciy¯i⋅⋅, with variance σ2∑ici2/(bm). These are the estimate and variance of the one-way layout in A that ignores B. The estimate of a contrast in the αi is uncorrelated with the estimate of any contrast in the βj.

  • (c) Under normal errors, SSA=‖PAy‖2=bm∑i(y¯i⋅⋅−y¯⋅⋅⋅)2, SSB=‖PBy‖2=am∑j(y¯⋅j⋅−y¯⋅⋅⋅)2 and SSE=‖Ry‖2 are independent, SSE/σ2∼χ2(n−a−b+1), and FA=SSA/(a−1)SSE/(n−a−b+1)∼F(a−1, n−a−b+1, γA),γA=bmσ2∑i(αi−α¯)2, with α¯=a−1∑iαi. So FA is an exact test of α1=⋯=αa. Its numerator is that of the one-way F test in A that ignores B (Theorem 15.3.1); only the error term differs. The same holds for B, with γB=(am/σ2)∑j(βj−β¯)2.

  • (d) If m≥2, then R=PAB+PE with PAB=Ca⊗Cb⊗J¯m and PE=Ia⊗Ib⊗Cm, of ranks (a−1)(b−1) and ab(m−1). The squared lengths ‖PABy‖2 and ‖PEy‖2 are the lack-of-fit and pure-error sums of squares of the additive model (Definition 14.6.1). If the cell means μij are arbitrary, the lack-of-fit F test of additivity has noncentrality γAB=mσ2∑i,j(μij−μ¯i⋅−μ¯⋅j+μ¯⋅⋅)2.

  • (e) If m=1, then R=Ca⊗Cb, and the residual space consists of the a×b tables whose rows and columns all sum to zero. There is no pure error.

  • (f) (Tukey’s test.) If m=1, the errors are normal and (a−1)(b−1)≥2, then with SSN as in Eq. 16.1.4, FN=SSN(SSE−SSN)/((a−1)(b−1)−1)∼F(1, (a−1)(b−1)−1).

Proof.

  • (a) By Lemma 16.1.1(c) the three projections P0,PA,PB are mutually orthogonal, and their ranks are 1, a−1, b−1 by Lemma 16.1.1(b). Moreover P0+PA=Ia⊗J¯b⊗J¯m replaces each observation by its row mean. It is the projection onto C(ZA), the vectors that are constant on rows (Example (The one-way layout)). Likewise P0+PB projects onto C(ZB). Hence C(X)=C(ZA)+C(ZB)=(C(P0)⊕C(PA))+(C(P0)⊕C(PB))=C(P0)⊕C(PA)⊕C(PB), a sum of mutually orthogonal subspaces. By Theorem 6.3.8 its projection is P0+PA+PB. Then R=I−M is the complementary projection, orthogonal to the other three, with rank n−(a+b−1). The fitted values are My=(P0+PA)y+(P0+PB)y−P0y.

  • (b) The design is complete, hence connected, and the estimability condition is Exercise (C1) with a single component. For the estimator, E(y¯i⋅⋅)=μ+αi+β¯, so ∑iciy¯i⋅⋅ is unbiased for ∑iciαi when ∑ici=0. It equals 𝛒ρ⊤y with 𝛒ρ=(bm)−1c⊗1b⊗1m, and 𝛒𝛒ρ=PAρ∈C(X) because c⊥1a. So 𝛒(I−M)ρ=0, and by Eq. 7.2.1 its variance equals that of the BLUE. By Theorem 7.2.1(c) it is the BLUE. Its variance is 𝛒σ2‖ρ‖2=σ2∑ici2/(bm). The one-way layout in A has bm observations per level, and Theorem 8.7.1 gives the same estimator and variance. A contrast in the βj has coefficient vector 𝛒ρB∈C(PB), and 𝛒𝛒𝛒𝛒Cov(ρ⊤Y,ρB⊤Y)=σ2ρ⊤ρB=0.

  • (c) The spaces C(P0),C(PA),C(PB) form an analysis of variance decomposition of C(X) (Definition 9.1.1), so Corollary 9.6.2 applies. The mean vector has entries μ+αi+βj. Its row means minus its grand mean are αi−α¯, so 𝛍‖PAμ‖2=bm∑i(αi−α¯)2. This is zero iff all αi are equal. The formula for SSA is the same computation applied to y.

  • (d) By Lemma 16.1.1(d) with t=3, I is the sum of the eight products Ea⊗Eb⊗Em. The four with Em=J¯m are P0,PA,PB,PAB. The four with Em=Cm add up to Ia⊗Ib⊗Cm=PE. So R=PAB+PE, with ranks from Lemma 16.1.1(b). The matrix I−PE=Ia⊗Ib⊗J¯m replaces each observation by its cell mean. It is the projection MZ onto the cell-indicator space, the Z of Definition 14.6.1. Hence PE=I−MZ and PAB=MZ−M. By Theorem 14.6.2 the noncentrality is 𝛉‖(I−M)θ‖2/σ2, where 𝛉θ has entries μij. Here 𝛉𝛉(I−M)θ=PABθ, because 𝛉PEθ=0. Its entries are the displayed interaction terms, each repeated m times.

  • (e) With m=1, J¯1=1 and C1=0, so PE=0 and R=PAB=Ca⊗Cb. A vector v, read as an a×b table V, satisfies (Ca⊗Cb)v=v iff CaVCb=V, which holds iff V1b=0 and 1a⊤V=0⊤.

  • (f) Write y^ij=y¯⋅⋅+ri+cj, let r=(ri) and c=(cj), and put U=r⊗c, the table (ricj). Expanding, y^ij2=(y¯⋅⋅2+ri2+2y¯⋅⋅ri)+(cj2+2y¯⋅⋅cj)+2ricj. The first bracket depends on i alone and the second on j alone, so both lie in C(X). Since 1a⊤r=1b⊤c=0, RU=Car⊗Cbc=U by (e). Hence the column W=(y^ij2), a function of MY, satisfies (I−M)W=2U. By Theorem 9.2.2 the extra sum of squares from adding W to the additive model is (U⊤y)2/U⊤U, and U⊤U=∑iri2∑jcj2, so it is SSN, and SSE−SSN is the residual sum of squares of the augmented model. The rank condition of Theorem 14.7.2 holds because U≠0 with probability one: U=0 only if PAY=0 or PBY=0, and each event has probability zero, because ‖PAY‖2/σ2 has a continuous (noncentral χ2) distribution on a−1≥1 degrees of freedom, and likewise for B. So rank[X,W]=a+b, which is less than n=ab because (a−1)(b−1)≥2. Theorem 14.7.2 with q=1 and n−r−q=(a−1)(b−1)−1 gives the distribution of FN.

Part (b) is the practical content of balance: rows are compared exactly as if the columns did not exist. The columns still matter, because they remove their own variation from the error.

The listing builds the five projections of part (d) for a=3, b=4, m=2 and checks their ranks, orthogonality and sum. The script also checks each identification in the proof against projections computed from indicator columns.

a, b, m = 3, 4, 2
P = factorial_projections([a, b], m)
names = {(0, 0): "mean", (1, 0): "A", (0, 1): "B", (1, 1): "AB", "error": "error"}
for key, name in names.items():
    print(f"{name:6s} rank {rank(P[key])}")
keys = list(names)
worst = max(np.abs(P[s] @ P[t]).max() for s, t in itertools.combinations(keys, 2))
print("largest entry of any product P_s P_t, s != t:", f"{worst:.1e}")
print("sum is the identity:", np.allclose(sum(P[k] for k in keys), np.eye(a * b * m)))

16.1.5. Tukey’s test in practice

Tukey’s test (Theorem 16.1.2(f)) is exact against every additive model, whatever the values of μ, αi, βj. It has power against interactions roughly proportional to αiβj, the kind produced by measuring on the wrong scale, and little against anything else. The estimated slope says which scale. If f is the inverse of a power transformation, so that yp is additive, then f(t)=t1/p, and Eq. 16.1.3 gives θ=(1−p)/f(ν) (Exercise (B3)). Replacing f(ν) by the grand mean suggests the power

p^=1−θ^y¯⋅⋅,
(16.1.5)

with p^=0 read as the logarithm. The rule is rough, but it turns a vague complaint into a specific suggestion.

Example (Solvers on benchmark problems)

Six solvers were each run once on eight benchmark problems, and the running time was recorded in seconds. The data are synthetic, generated by the script tukey_runtime.py so that the logarithm of time is additive in solver and problem, with a little noise:

The additive model in seconds leaves a residual sum of squares of 29396 on 35 degrees of freedom. Tukey’s single direction takes SSN=26126 of it, a fraction 0.889, and FN=271.7 on 1 and 34 degrees of freedom (p=9×10−18). The interaction has exactly the shape of Eq. 16.1.3: fast problems separate the solvers by fractions of a second, slow problems by minutes. The slope is θ^=0.01563 and the grand mean is 61.46, so Eq. 16.1.5 suggests the power 0.04, essentially the logarithm. Figure 16.1.1 shows the diagnostic plot. On the log scale, FN=0.27 (p=0.60), and the residuals show no trend against ricj/y¯⋅⋅.

The scale changes the conclusion about the solvers. In seconds, FA=3.88 (p=0.007), because the nonadditivity inflates the error term. In log seconds, FA=77.7. The residual standard deviation is s=0.105, so differences between solvers are measured in proportions of about ten per cent. By Exercise (B1) the solver means of log time are independent with common variance σ2/8 and independent of s2. Tukey’s method therefore gives simultaneous intervals for all 15 ratios of solver speeds, with multiplier q0.05(6,35)=4.261. Solver 4, the slowest, takes 2.50 times as long as solver 3, the fastest, with simultaneous 95% interval [2.14, 2.94].

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

gen_rng = np.random.default_rng(16_01)
a, b = 6, 8
solver_log = np.array([0.0, 0.25, -0.35, 0.55, 0.1, -0.2])            # log speed factors
problem_log = np.log([2.0, 4.5, 7.0, 12.0, 25.0, 60.0, 110.0, 240.0])  # typical seconds
noise = gen_rng.normal(scale=0.12, size=(a, b))
times = np.round(np.exp(solver_log[:, None] + problem_log[None, :] + noise), 1)

def additive_fit(Y):
    """Row effects, column effects and residuals of the additive fit to an a x b table."""
    g = Y.mean()
    r = Y.mean(axis=1) - g                    # ybar_i. - ybar..
    c = Y.mean(axis=0) - g                    # ybar_.j - ybar..
    resid = Y - g - r[:, None] - c[None, :]
    return g, r, c, resid


def tukey_test(Y):
    """Tukey's one-degree-of-freedom test for nonadditivity in an a x b table."""
    a, b = Y.shape
    g, r, c, resid = additive_fit(Y)
    u = np.outer(r, c)                        # lies in the interaction space
    ss_n = (u * Y).sum() ** 2 / (u * u).sum()
    sse = (resid ** 2).sum()
    df = (a - 1) * (b - 1) - 1
    F = ss_n / ((sse - ss_n) / df)
    theta = (u * Y).sum() / (u * u).sum()     # coefficient of a_i b_j
    return F, stats.f.sf(F, 1, df), theta, g, sse, ss_n


for label, Y in [("seconds", times), ("log seconds", np.log(times))]:
    F, p, theta, g, sse, ss_n = tukey_test(Y)
    print(f"{label:12s} F = {F:9.2f}  p = {p:.2g}  theta = {theta:.4f}"
          f"  suggested power 1 - theta*ybar = {1 - theta * g:.2f}")

The listing below checks Theorem 16.1.2(f) by simulation. It draws 20000 tables from an additive normal model with a=6 and b=8. The 5% test rejects in a fraction 0.0508 of them, and a Kolmogorov–Smirnov comparison of the simulated statistics with F(1,34) gives p=0.98.

rng = np.random.default_rng(20260919)
reps = 20_000
Fs = np.empty(reps)
for t in range(reps):
    Y = 3.0 + np.arange(a)[:, None] * 0.5 + np.arange(b)[None, :] + rng.normal(size=(a, b))
    Fs[t] = tukey_test(Y)[0]
size = np.mean(Fs > stats.f.ppf(0.95, 1, (a - 1) * (b - 1) - 1))
ks = stats.kstest(Fs, stats.f(1, (a - 1) * (b - 1) - 1).cdf)
print(f"simulated size of the 5% test: {size:.4f}   Kolmogorov-Smirnov p-value: {ks.pvalue:.2f}")

Warning. A nonsignificant Tukey test does not establish additivity. It rules out only interaction of the form θαiβj. A single aberrant cell or row can leave FN small and still distort every conclusion, so look at the residual table and an interaction plot (Section 16.2) as well.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In the additive model, suppose α1=δ and α2=⋯=αa=0. Find γA. With a=4, b=5 and δ=σ, compare the power of the 5% test for m=1 and m=2 using Theorem 11.5.1, and explain why doubling m more than doubles the error degrees of freedom.

Solution. Here α¯=δ/a, and ∑i(αi−α¯)2=δ2(1−1/a)2+(a−1)δ2/a2=δ2(a−1)/a. So γA=bmδ2(a−1)/(aσ2). With a=4, b=5, δ=σ this is 3.75m. The error degrees of freedom are n−a−b+1=20m−8, which is 12 for m=1 and 32 for m=2. The power is Pr{F(3,20m−8,3.75m)>F0.05(3,20m−8)}, about 0.25 for m=1 and 0.56 for m=2 (computed by the script exercise_checks.py). The noncentrality doubles, and the error degrees of freedom more than double because the a+b−1 degrees of freedom spent on the mean structure are a fixed cost. Both effects raise the power.

Exercise (B2)

Show that Tukey’s statistic FN is unchanged if y is replaced by λy+t, where λ≠0 and t is any additive table tij=ui+vj. Conclude that FN has the same distribution under every additive model with the same σ2.

Exercise (B3)

Suppose yp is additive for some p≠0, so that μij=f(ν+ai+bj) with f(t)=t1/p. Verify that θ=f″(ν)/f′(ν)2=(1−p)/f(ν), and derive Eq. 16.1.5. What does θ^y¯⋅⋅≈1 suggest, and why is p=0 the right reading?

Solution. f′(t)=p−1t1/p−1 and f″(t)=p−1(p−1−1)t1/p−2. So f″(ν)f′(ν)2=p−1(p−1−1)ν1/p−2p−2ν2/p−2=p(1p−1)ν−1/p=1−pf(ν). Solving θ=(1−p)/f(ν) for p, and estimating θ by θ^ and f(ν), the typical size of a cell mean, by y¯⋅⋅, gives p^=1−θ^y¯⋅⋅. A value θ^y¯⋅⋅≈1 gives p^≈0. For f(t)=et, which makes log⁡y additive, f″/f′2=e−ν=1/f(ν), which is the limit p→0 of the power formula. This matches the convention that (yp−1)/p→log⁡y.

Exercise (B4)

With m=1 and normal errors, suppose the cell means are μij=μ+αi+βj+θαiβj with ∑iαi=∑jβj=0, exactly the shape of Eq. 16.1.3. Show that ri=αi+ε¯i⋅−ε¯⋅⋅, and that, given PAY and PBY, E(θ^∣PAY,PBY)=θ∑iriαi∑iri2⋅∑jcjβj∑jcj2. Explain why θ^ is nearly unbiased when the row and column effects are large compared with σ, and why it tends to be pulled towards zero when they are not. What does this imply for the power rule Eq. 16.1.5?

Solution. The interaction terms θαiβj have zero row and column means, so μ¯i⋅−μ¯⋅⋅=αi and ri=αi+ε¯i⋅−ε¯⋅⋅; likewise for cj. With U=r⊗c as in the proof of Theorem 16.1.2(f), θ^=U⊤RY/U⊤U. By Theorem 4.4.4, RY is independent of (PAY,PBY), whatever the mean, so given those two vectors U is fixed and 𝛍E(RY)=Rμ, the table (θαiβj).

10.2  The Cholesky factorization

If the cross-product matrix is to be used at all, the normal equations should be solved by factoring it as a triangular matrix times its transpose. One factorization of the augmented cross-product matrix then delivers every standard regression quantity.

10.2.1. The algorithm

Chapter 1 proved that a positive definite matrix has a unique factorization A=LL⊤ with L lower triangular and positive on the diagonal (Theorem 1.7.4(d)). Numerical work usually writes it with the upper triangular factor R=L⊤.

Theorem 10.2.1 (Cholesky factorization)

Let A be a p×p positive definite matrix.

  • (a) There is exactly one upper triangular R with positive diagonal entries such that A=R⊤R.

  • (b) Its rows are given, for j=1,…,p in turn, by

    rjj=(ajj−∑k<jrkj2)1/2,rji=1rjj(aji−∑k<jrkjrki),i>j,
    (10.2.1)

    and every radicand in it is positive.

  • (c) The computation takes p3/3+O(p2) flops.

  • (d) If A=X⊤X and X=QR0 is a QR factorization with Q⊤Q=Ip and R0 upper triangular with positive diagonal, then R0=R.

Proof.

  • (a) is Theorem 1.7.4(d) with R=L⊤.
  • (b) Entry (j,i), i≥j, of R⊤R is ∑k≤jrkjrki, because rkj=0 for k>j. Setting it equal to aji and isolating the k=j term gives the recursion Eq. 10.2.1. So the entries of the unique factor of (a) satisfy Eq. 10.2.1, and Eq. 10.2.1 determines row j from rows 1,…,j−1. By induction on j, the rows computed by Eq. 10.2.1 are those of the factor in (a). In particular the jth radicand is rjj2>0.
  • (c) Row j needs about 2(j−1) flops for each of its p−j+1 entries, so the total is ∑j2(j−1)(p−j+1)=p3/3+O(p2).
  • (d) X⊤X=R0⊤Q⊤QR0=R0⊤R0, and uniqueness in (a).

By part (d), the triangle of a QR factorization of X and the Cholesky factor of X⊤X are the same matrix (see also Exercise (B2)). The two routes of this chapter compute the same object, one from X, the other from X⊤X after the small singular values may already have been damaged (Proposition 10.1.3).

The algorithm itself is backward stable for the factorization. The computed R^ satisfies R^⊤R^=A+ΔA with |ΔA|≤γp+1|R^|⊤|R^| entrywise, and it runs to completion whenever A is not too close to singular (Higham 2002, chapter 10), with no pivoting. The weakness of the normal equations lies entirely in forming A=X⊤X, not in factoring it.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

def cholesky_upper(A):
    """Upper triangular R with positive diagonal and R'R = A (A positive definite)."""
    A = np.array(A, dtype=float)
    p = A.shape[0]
    R = np.zeros_like(A)
    for j in range(p):
        d = A[j, j] - R[:j, j] @ R[:j, j]
        if d <= 0:
            raise np.linalg.LinAlgError(f"not positive definite at step {j + 1}")
        R[j, j] = np.sqrt(d)
        R[j, j + 1:] = (A[j, j + 1:] - R[:j, j] @ R[:j, j + 1:]) / R[j, j]
    return R

def solve_upper(R, b):
    """Back substitution for R x = b, R upper triangular."""
    x = np.zeros(np.shape(b))
    for i in range(len(b) - 1, -1, -1):
        x[i] = (b[i] - R[i, i + 1:] @ x[i + 1:]) / R[i, i]
    return x

def solve_lower(L, b):
    """Forward substitution for L x = b, L lower triangular."""
    x = np.zeros(np.shape(b))
    for i in range(len(b)):
        x[i] = (b[i] - L[i, :i] @ x[:i]) / L[i, i]
    return x

10.2.2. Everything from one factorization

Solving the normal equations R⊤Rb=X⊤y takes two triangular solves: R⊤z=X⊤y for z, then Rb=z. Each costs p2 flops. It is neater to factor the cross-product matrix of the augmented matrix Z=[X,y]. The factor then contains the solution of the first triangular system and the residual sum of squares.

Proposition 10.2.2 (The augmented factorization)

Let X be n×p of full column rank, suppose y∉C(X), and let Z⊤Z=(X⊤XX⊤yy⊤Xy⊤y)=T⊤T,T=(Rz0⊤d), be the Cholesky factorization (Theorem 10.2.1) of the cross-product matrix of Z=[X,y]. Then:

  • (a) R is the Cholesky factor of X⊤X, the least squares estimate solves 𝛃Rβ^=z, and 𝛃d2=SSE=‖y−Xβ^‖2;

  • (b) for each k≤p, the residual sum of squares of the regression of y on the first k columns of X is d2+∑j>kzj2. The drop in residual sum of squares when column k joins columns 1,…,k−1 is zk2;

  • (c) det(X⊤X)=∏jrjj2, (X⊤X)−1=R−1R−⊤, and the leverage of row x(i)⊤ is hii=‖R−⊤x(i)‖2.

Proof. Z has full column rank because y∉C(X), so Z⊤Z is positive definite and T exists. Multiplying out T⊤T and comparing blocks with Z⊤Z gives R⊤R=X⊤X,R⊤z=X⊤y,z⊤z+d2=y⊤y. The first identity and uniqueness make R the Cholesky factor of X⊤X. The second gives 𝛃R⊤Rβ^=R⊤z for the solution of the normal equations, so 𝛃Rβ^=z.

For (b), and for the rest of (a), let Q=ZT−1. Then Q⊤Q=T−⊤Z⊤ZT−1=I, so the columns q1,…,qp+1 of Q are orthonormal, and Z=QT. Because T is upper triangular with nonzero diagonal, the first k columns of X span the same subspace as q1,…,qk. The last column of Z=QT reads y=∑j≤pzjqj+dqp+1. By Proposition 6.3.2 the projection of y onto the span of the first k columns is ∑j≤kzjqj, and the residual sum of squares is ‖y‖2−∑j≤kzj2=d2+∑j>kzj2. With k=p this is d2, which completes (a). Taking differences gives the rest of (b).

  • (c) The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal. The inverse formula follows from X⊤X=R⊤R. Finally hii=x(i)⊤(X⊤X)−1x(i)=x(i)⊤R−1R−⊤x(i)=‖R−⊤x(i)‖2.

Part (b) says that the squares z12,…,zp2 are the sequential sums of squares of the regressors in the order of the columns (Definition 9.3.1). A single factorization gives the whole nested sequence of fits. This is the content of Theorem 6.5.1, now in coordinates. Part (c) costs about np2 flops for all n leverages, each a triangular solve of p2 flops.

Example (Murder rates by the augmented factorization)

For the state data of Example (Poverty and murder rates) (n=50, with an intercept and the percentages in poverty, in single-parent households and urban, so p=4), the augmented factorization (Proposition 10.2.2) gives the coefficients −9.0946, 0.2538, 0.3980 and 0.0046, and the corner entry d=10.0976, so SSE=d2=101.96. The condition number of X is only 509, and the solution agrees with a QR solution to a relative difference of 2×10−14. The largest leverage is 0.260, and log10⁡det(X⊤X)=11.14. The script also checks the standard errors s‖ej⊤R−1‖ against statsmodels.

data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data.drop(index="District of Columbia")
y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), data[["poverty", "single", "urban"]]])
n, p = X.shape
Z = np.column_stack([X, y])              # augmented matrix [X, y]
S = Z.T @ Z                              # (p+1) x (p+1) cross-product matrix
T = cholesky_upper(S)                    # T = [[R, z], [0, d]]
R, z, d = T[:p, :p], T[:p, p], T[p, p]

beta = solve_upper(R, z)                 # R b = z
sse = d ** 2                             # the last diagonal entry is sqrt(SSE)
K = solve_upper(R, np.eye(p))            # R^{-1}
XtX_inv = K @ K.T                        # (X'X)^{-1} = R^{-1} R^{-T}
h = np.array([np.sum(solve_lower(R.T, x) ** 2) for x in X])   # leverages
print("coefficients:", np.round(beta, 4))
print(f"SSE = {sse:.4f},  d = {d:.4f}")

10.2.3. When the normal equations are good enough

Proposition 10.1.3 and Eq. 10.1.2 predict a relative error of order κ2u in 𝛃β^ from the normal equations, where, as Section 10.5 shows, κ is the condition number of X after its columns are scaled to equal length. With κ≤104 about eight digits survive, more than any coefficient is known statistically. The normal equations are therefore sound for well-conditioned designs, when only cross-products are available, and inside iterations such as the reweighted least squares of Chapter 34. Nearly collinear observational data are where the κ2 loss bites.

10.2.4. The sweep operator

Before QR became standard, regression programs worked on the augmented cross-product matrix with an operation that adds one variable at a time to the fit. It is still used for fitting many subsets of variables.

Definition 10.2.1 (Sweep)

Let A be symmetric with akk≠0. Sweeping A on pivot k produces the symmetric matrix B with bkk=−1akk,bik=bki=aikakk,bij=aij−aikakjakk(i,j≠k).

Proposition 10.2.3 (What sweeping computes)

Let S=Z⊤Z be the cross-product matrix of Z=[z1,…,zm]. Let K be a set of column indices such that ZK has full column rank, and let L be the remaining indices. Sweeping S on the pivots in K, one at a time and in any order, meets only positive pivots and produces the matrix W with blocks WKK=−(ZK⊤ZK)−1,WKL=WLK⊤=(ZK⊤ZK)−1ZK⊤ZL,WLL=ZL⊤(I−MK)ZL, where MK is the projection onto C(ZK). Column j∈L of WKL holds the least squares coefficients of zj regressed on ZK, and WLL holds the residual cross-products. With Z=[X,y] and K the columns of X, the last column of W contains 𝛃β^ and, in its corner, the residual sum of squares.

Proof. We induct on the size of K; the case K=∅ is W=S. Add a pivot m∈L with ZK∪{m} of full column rank, and write G=(ZK⊤ZK)−1, Bj=GZK⊤zj and w=(I−MK)zm. The pivot is zm⊤(I−MK)zm=‖w‖2>0. For i,j∈L∖{m} the new entries are zi⊤(I−MK−ww⊤/‖w‖2)zj=zi⊤(I−MK∪{m})zj by Lemma 6.6.1. The new entries w⊤zj/‖w‖2 in row m, and Bj−Bmw⊤zj/‖w‖2 in the rows of K, are the coefficients of zj on [ZK,zm] by Theorem 6.6.2. The partitioned inverse (Theorem 1.6.4), with Schur complement ‖w‖2, shows that (ZK∪{m}⊤ZK∪{m})−1 has blocks G+BmBm⊤/‖w‖2, −Bm/‖w‖2 and 1/‖w‖2, whose negatives are what the sweep produces: −G−BmBm⊤/‖w‖2, Bm/‖w‖2 and −1/‖w‖2. The result depends only on the set K, so the order of the sweeps does not matter.

Sweeping a pivot costs about (p+1)2 flops, so a new variable enters a fitted model for O(p2) work. The price is the same as for the normal equations: the sweep works with cross-products and inherits their κ2 sensitivity. For the state data, sweeping all four columns reproduces the coefficients and SSE of Example (Murder rates by the augmented factorization). Sweeping only the intercept and the single-parent column puts the SSE of that two-column model, 121.92, in the corner.

def sweep(A, k):
    """Sweep the symmetric matrix A on pivot k."""
    A = np.array(A, dtype=float)
    a = A[k, k]
    B = A - np.outer(A[:, k], A[k, :]) / a
    B[k, :] = A[k, :] / a
    B[:, k] = A[:, k] / a
    B[k, k] = -1 / a
    return B

W = S.copy()
for k in range(p):                       # sweep the p columns of X, one at a time
    W = sweep(W, k)
print("coefficients from the sweep:", np.round(W[:p, p], 4))
print(f"SSE from the sweep: {W[p, p]:.4f}")

10.2.5. Forming cross-products: the centring problem

With an intercept in the model, the cross-product matrix is usually computed in centred form, ∑i(xij−x¯j)(xik−x¯k), which by Theorem 6.7.1 changes the parameterization but not the fit. How it is computed matters a great deal. The “textbook” formula ∑ixi2−nx¯2 subtracts two large, nearly equal numbers whenever the mean is large compared with the spread. The loss is governed by the ratio κx=‖x‖‖x−x¯1‖=(1+nx¯2∑i(xi−x¯)2)1/2, which is the condition number of the column, and it is large when the coefficient of variation is small. Three algorithms are in common use:

  • textbook: accumulate ∑xi and ∑xi2 in one pass, then subtract;
  • two-pass: compute x¯ in a first pass, then sum (xi−x¯)2 in a second;
  • updating: one pass, maintaining the running mean and the running centred sum by the recursions below.
Proposition 10.2.4 (Updating means and centred cross-products)

For vectors x1,x2,… in Rq let mk be the mean of the first k and Ck=∑i≤k(xi−mk)(xi−mk)⊤. With dk=xk−mk−1, mk=mk−1+dkk,Ck=Ck−1+k−1kdkdk⊤.

Proof. The first identity is kmk=(k−1)mk−1+xk. For the second, write Ck=∑i≤kxixi⊤−kmkmk⊤ and put m=mk−1, d=dk. Then Ck−Ck−1=xkxk⊤−kmkmk⊤+(k−1)mm⊤. Substituting xk=m+d and kmkmk⊤=kmm⊤+md⊤+dm⊤+dd⊤/k, all the terms in m cancel and dd⊤(1−1/k) remains.

The update adds a centred rank-one term, so it never subtracts large quantities. It is the cross-product analogue of the row updates of Section 10.6. Chan, Golub and LeVeque (1983) give error bounds of roughly nκx2u for the textbook formula, nκxu for updating and nu+n2κx2u2 for the two-pass algorithm.

Example (Centring a column with a large mean)

A sample of 1000 standard normal values is shifted by c=1,10,…,108, and the centred sum of squares is computed by the three algorithms and compared with its exact value (found in rational arithmetic). At c=106, where κx=1.0×106, the relative errors are 8.9×10−5 for the textbook formula, 8.1×10−12 for updating and 2.7×10−17 for two passes. At c=108 the textbook formula has relative error 3.1: not a single correct digit, and possibly a negative “sum of squares”. Updating gives 7.4×10−10 and two passes 2.3×10−17. Figure 10.2.1 shows the three growth rates κ2u, κu and u.

def ss_textbook(x):
    return np.sum(x * x) - len(x) * np.mean(x) ** 2        # one pass, then subtract

def ss_two_pass(x):
    m = np.mean(x)                                         # first pass: the mean
    return np.sum((x - m) ** 2)                            # second pass: deviations

def ss_updating(x):
    m, s = 0.0, 0.0
    for k, xk in enumerate(x, start=1):                    # one pass, no cancellation
        d = xk - m
        m = m + d / k
        s = s + (k - 1) / k * d * d
    return s

Calendar years, population totals and price indices have large means and small spread. A program that forms cross-products should centre them with two passes (or update), solve for the slopes, and recover the intercept as 𝛃y¯−x¯⊤β^slopes.

10.2.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Compute the Cholesky factor of A=(42−22102−226) by Eq. 10.2.1, and use it to solve Ab=(2,14,6)⊤ and to find detA.

Solution. Row 1: r11=2, r12=1, r13=−1. Row 2: r22=10−1=3, r23=(2−(1)(−1))/3=1. Row 3: r33=6−1−1=2. So R=(21−1031002) and detA=(2⋅3⋅2)2=144. Forward substitution in R⊤z=(2,14,6)⊤ gives z1=1, z2=(14−1)/3=13/3, z3=(6+1−13/3)/2=4/3. Back substitution in Rb=z gives b3=2/3, b2=(13/3−2/3)/3=11/9 and b1=(1−11/9+2/3)/2=2/9. Check: Ab=(8/9+22/9−12/9, 4/9+110/9+12/9, −4/9+22/9+36/9)=(2,14,6).

Exercise (A2)

For simple regression, X=[1,x], find the Cholesky factor (Theorem 10.2.1) of X⊤X and of the augmented matrix, and show that it reproduces the slope Sxy/Sxx of Theorem 5.2.1. Where does the centring happen?

Solution. X⊤X=(nnx¯nx¯∑xi2) gives r11=n, r12=nx¯ and r22=(∑xi2−nx¯2)1/2=Sxx1/2. The augmented column gives z1=ny¯/n=ny¯ and z2=(∑xiyi−nx¯y¯)/Sxx1/2=Sxy/Sxx1/2. Back substitution gives the slope b2=z2/r22=Sxy/Sxx and the intercept b1=(z1−r12b2)/r11=y¯−x¯b2. The centring happens in the subtraction a22−r122, which is the textbook formula of Example (Centring a column with a large mean). So the Cholesky factorization of an uncentred cross-product matrix inherits its cancellation.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that the standard error of β^j is s‖ej⊤R−1‖, where s2=SSE/(n−p) (Theorem 5.6.1). Show that all p standard errors cost p3/3+O(p2) flops, and all n leverages np2+O(np) flops. Why is computing M itself a bad idea?

Solution. By Theorem 5.5.1 the estimated variance of β^j is s2[(X⊤X)−1]jj, and [(X⊤X)−1]jj=ej⊤R−1R−⊤ej=‖ej⊤R−1‖2. The inverse of a triangular matrix is triangular, and column j of R−1 is found by back substitution on its leading j×j block, at about j2 flops. The total is ∑jj2≈p3/3. Each leverage is a triangular solve with R⊤ (p2 flops) and a squared norm. The projection M has n2 entries, which for n=106 is too many to store, and only its diagonal is ever needed.

Two groups of observations have sizes na,nb, means ma,mb and centred cross-product matrices Ca,Cb. Show that the pooled data have centred cross-product matrix C=Ca+Cb+nanbna+nb(ma−mb)(ma−mb)⊤. Show that Proposition 10.2.4 is the special case nb=1, and explain how the formula allows cross-products to be computed in parallel on separate blocks of data.

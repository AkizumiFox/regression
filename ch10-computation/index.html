Computation: QR, SVD and Conditioning

Every least squares estimate in this book is written as β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y or as X(X⊤X)−X⊤y. These are the right formulas to reason with. They are not how a computer should produce the numbers. This chapter explains what a computer should do instead, and how accurate the result is. It separates two questions: how sensitive the least squares problem is to small changes in the data, which depends on κ(X) and the size of the residual, and whether an algorithm adds more error than that. The answers give a practical ranking. The Cholesky factorization of X⊤X is fast and is enough when X is well conditioned. Householder or Givens QR is the default. The singular value decomposition is the tool for rank deficiency and near dependence. Pivoted QR decides the rank cheaply, which is how statistical software chooses what to do with aliased columns.

What you need. Chapter 1: the Cholesky factorization (Theorem 1.7.4), QR (Theorem 1.5.2), the singular value decomposition (Theorem 1.8.1), the Eckart–Young inequality (Proposition 1.8.2) and the condition number (Definition 1.8.1). Chapter 5: the linear model and the moments of β^ (Theorem 5.5.1). Chapter 6: least squares as projection, and the first look at computation in Section 6.10. A few remarks use the sampling theory of Chapter 7 and the sums of squares of Chapter 9.

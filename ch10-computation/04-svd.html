10.4  The singular value decomposition

QR is the workhorse when X has full column rank. When it does not, or nearly does not, the singular value decomposition tells the whole story: every least squares solution, the one of minimum norm, and, in the smallest singular values, the combinations of the columns that the data cannot resolve. Theorem 1.8.1 established the decomposition, and Proposition 6.10.1 previewed its use for least squares.

10.4.1. Least squares through the SVD

Throughout, X is n×p of rank r≥1, with singular value decomposition X=∑i≤rσiuivi⊤ (Theorem 1.8.1). We complete u1,…,ur and v1,…,vr to orthonormal bases u1,…,un of Rn and v1,…,vp of Rp, and write ci=ui⊤y for the coordinates of the data in the left singular basis.

Theorem 10.4.1 (Least squares via the SVD)

  • (a) b is a least squares estimate iff vi⊤b=ci/σi for i=1,…,r. The coordinates vi⊤b for i>r are arbitrary, and the set of least squares estimates is X+y+N(X) with N(X)=span(vr+1,…,vp).

  • (b) X+y=∑i≤r(ci/σi)vi is the unique least squares estimate of smallest Euclidean norm.

  • (c) Y^=∑i≤rciui and SSE=∑i>rci2=‖y‖2−∑i≤rci2.

  • (d) If r=p and Cov(Y)=σ2I, the estimate β^=X+Y has Cov(β^)=σ2∑i≤pσi−2vivi⊤. In particular Var(vi⊤β^)=σ2/σi2, and the total variance is trCov(β^)=σ2∑iσi−2.

Proof. Let U=[u1,…,un], an orthogonal matrix. For any b, Xb=∑i≤rσi(vi⊤b)ui, so the coordinates of Xb in the basis u1,…,un are σivi⊤b for i≤r and zero for i>r. By orthogonal invariance,

‖y−Xb‖2=‖U⊤y−U⊤Xb‖2=∑i≤r(ci−σivi⊤b)2+∑i>rci2.
(10.4.1)

The second sum does not involve b, and the first vanishes exactly when vi⊤b=ci/σi for i≤r. This proves (a) and (c), since b=∑i(vi⊤b)vi and N(X) is spanned by the vi with i>r (Theorem 1.8.1). Part (b) is Proposition 6.10.1. In coordinates, ‖b‖2=∑i≤p(vi⊤b)2, so the minimum-norm estimate is the one whose free coordinates are zero, ∑i≤r(ci/σi)vi. For (d), β^=∑iσi−1viui⊤Y, and Cov(ui⊤Y,uj⊤Y)=σ2ui⊤uj is σ2 or 0. By Theorem 2.2.1, Cov(β^)=σ2∑iσi−2vivi⊤. Its trace is σ2∑iσi−2 because ‖vi‖=1.

Part (d) is the statistical face of ill-conditioning. The combination vp⊤β of the coefficients, along the direction in which the columns of X are nearly dependent, is estimated with standard deviation σ/σp. When σp is tiny, the data carry almost no information about that combination, and no algorithm can change this.

import numpy as np

def svd_ls(X, y, tol=None):
    """Minimum-norm least squares solution, treating singular values <= tol as zero."""
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
    if tol is None:
        tol = max(X.shape) * np.finfo(float).eps * s[0]   # the usual default
    r = int(np.sum(s > tol))                               # numerical rank
    c = U[:, :r].T @ y                                     # coordinates of the fit
    b = Vt[:r].T @ (c / s[:r])
    sse = y @ y - c @ c
    return b, r, sse, s

10.4.2. Computing the SVD

The SVD is not computed from the eigenvalues of X⊤X, which has already lost the small singular values (Section 10.1). The standard method (Golub and Kahan 1965; Golub and Reinsch 1970) first applies Householder reflections alternately from the left and the right to reduce X to upper bidiagonal form, at about twice the cost of QR, and then drives the superdiagonal to zero by an implicitly shifted QR-type iteration that never forms a cross-product. When n≫p, it pays to compute X=Q1R first and then an SVD R=URΣV⊤ of the triangle, since X=(Q1UR)ΣV⊤ (Exercise (B2)). Least squares needs only U⊤y, never U itself, which gives the count 2np2+11p3 of Section 10.1 (Golub and Van Loan 2013).

The computed singular values are those of a nearby matrix X+E with ‖E‖2≤cu‖X‖2. The next result turns this into accuracy.

Proposition 10.4.2 (Singular values are well conditioned)

For any matrices A and E of the same size and every k, |σk(A+E)−σk(A)|≤‖E‖2.

Proof. For any matrix C and any B of rank at most k−1, σk(C)≤‖C−B‖2. When k−1<rankC this is the Eckart–Young inequality (Proposition 1.8.2(c)), and otherwise σk(C)=0. Let Ak−1 be the truncated SVD of A with k−1 terms, or A itself if rankA<k, so that ‖A−Ak−1‖2=σk(A). Then σk(A+E)≤‖A+E−Ak−1‖2≤σk(A)+‖E‖2. Exchanging the roles of A and A+E gives the other inequality.

So a backward stable SVD computes every singular value with absolute error of order uσ1, and a singular value of that size or below is indistinguishable from zero. An exactly rank-deficient design, such as a one-way layout with an intercept and all its indicators, therefore shows a smallest singular value near 10−16σ1 rather than zero: 5×10−17 times the largest in the script’s example.

10.4.3. Numerical rank

In floating point, and with data measured to finite precision, the meaningful question is how many singular values are clearly nonzero.

Definition 10.4.1 (Numerical rank)

For a tolerance τ>0, the numerical rank of X is rτ=#{i:σi>τ}. The truncated SVD with k terms is Xk=∑i≤kσiuivi⊤.

By the Eckart–Young inequality, rτ is the least rank of any matrix within spectral distance τ of X (Exercise (B1)), so τ should reflect how well X is known. The default in numpy and LAPACK-based software, τ=max(n,p)ϵMσ1, accounts for rounding only; data recorded to six significant figures cannot resolve singular values below about 10−6σ1. The choice matters, because the minimum-norm solution is not a continuous function of X.

Example (The pseudoinverse jumps)

Let x1=(1,2,3,4)⊤, let d=(1,−1,−1,1)⊤ be orthogonal to it, and let Xt=[x1, x1+td], with y=(1,3,2,5)⊤. For every t≠0 the column space is the same plane span(x1,d), so the fitted values do not depend on t. The coefficients do. At t=10−6 they are −249999 and 250000, because the coefficients must reproduce a fixed multiple of d using the small difference td of the two columns. At t=0 the rank drops to one. The minimum-norm solution jumps to 0.550 for both columns, and the fitted values jump too, because the d-direction has disappeared from the model. Treating singular values below a tolerance as zero makes the two answers agree. Whether it should is a statistical question: is the difference between the columns signal, or recording error?

x1 = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0])
d = np.array([1.0, -1.0, -1.0, 1.0])            # orthogonal to x1
yd = np.array([1.0, 3.0, 2.0, 5.0])
for t in [1e-2, 1e-6, 1e-10, 0.0]:
    Xt = np.column_stack([x1, x1 + t * d])        # second column tends to the first
    b = np.linalg.pinv(Xt, rcond=1e-15) @ yd
    print(f"t = {t:7.0e}   b = {np.round(b, 3)}   fit = {np.round(Xt @ b, 3)}")

10.4.4. Truncated SVD as an estimator

The truncated solution bk=∑i≤k(ci/σi)vi is more than a numerical safeguard. It is a statistical estimator in its own right, and it trades bias for variance.

Proposition 10.4.3 (Bias and variance of truncated SVD)

Suppose rankX=p, E(Y)=Xβ and Cov(Y)=σ2I, and let bk=∑i≤k(ui⊤Y/σi)vi for k≤p. Then E(bk)=∑i≤k(vi⊤β)vi,Cov(bk)=σ2∑i≤kvivi⊤σi2,

E‖bk−β‖2=∑i>k(vi⊤β)2+σ2∑i≤k1σi2.
(10.4.2)

Hence bk has smaller mean squared error than the least squares estimate bp iff ∑i>k(vi⊤β)2<σ2∑i>kσi−2.

Proof. E(ui⊤Y)=ui⊤Xβ=σivi⊤β, which gives the mean. The covariance is part (d) of Theorem 10.4.1 with the sum stopped at k. For the mean squared error, write E‖bk−β‖2=‖Ebk−β‖2+trCov(bk). The bias Ebk−β is −∑i>k(vi⊤β)vi, with squared length ∑i>k(vi⊤β)2. Comparing Eq. 10.4.2 for k and p gives the last claim.

Each dropped component costs its squared coefficient (vi⊤β)2 in bias and saves σ2/σi2 in variance. It is worth dropping exactly when the signal along vi is weaker than the noise in its estimate, that is, when σi|vi⊤β|<σ. The estimator bk is principal components regression in its uncentred form. Ridge regression replaces the hard cut-off by weights σi2/(σi2+λ). Both are developed in Chapter 27, and choosing k from data is a model-selection problem (Chapter 29).

Example (Truncating a polynomial design)

The powers 1,t,…,t7 at 60 equally spaced points in [0,1] form a design with κ(X)=1.1×105 and smallest singular value 9.0×10−5. Take σ=0.1 and a coefficient vector whose components along v1,…,v8 decay (2,−1.5,1,−0.6,0.3,−0.1,0.05,−0.02), as they do for smooth regression functions. By Eq. 10.4.2, least squares has mean squared error 1231055. The best truncation keeps 4 components and has mean squared error 0.21, made up of squared bias 0.10 and variance 0.11. A simulation with 4000 replicates agrees with the formula at every k (Figure 10.4.1). The default numerical tolerance treats the design as having full rank, which it does to working precision. The statistical “effective rank” is set by the noise level instead.

n, p, sigma = 60, 8, 0.1
t = np.linspace(0, 1, n)
X = np.vander(t, p, increasing=True)            # 1, t, ..., t^7: badly conditioned
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
a = np.array([2.0, -1.5, 1.0, -0.6, 0.3, -0.1, 0.05, -0.02])   # decaying components v_i' beta
beta = Vt.T @ a
print("condition number:", s[0] / s[-1])
print("singular values:", np.array2string(s, precision=2))
bias2 = np.array([np.sum((Vt[k:] @ beta) ** 2) for k in range(1, p + 1)])   # ||(I - V_k V_k') beta||^2
var = np.array([sigma ** 2 * np.sum(1 / s[:k] ** 2) for k in range(1, p + 1)])
mse = bias2 + var
print("k    bias^2      variance     MSE")
for k in range(p):
    print(f"{k + 1}  {bias2[k]:10.3e}  {var[k]:10.3e}  {mse[k]:10.3e}")

Idea. The SVD gives every least squares solution, the precision σ/σi of each combination vi⊤β, and, relative to a tolerance, how many combinations are determined at all.

10.4.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let X=(111100) and y=(1,3,5)⊤. Find the SVD of X, the set of least squares estimates, the minimum-norm estimate and the SSE.

Solution. X=2u1v1⊤ with u1=(1,1,0)⊤/2, v1=(1,1)⊤/2, σ1=2 and r=1. Here c1=u1⊤y=4/2, so the least squares estimates are the b with v1⊤b=c1/σ1=2, that is, b1+b2=2. The minimum-norm estimate is 2v1=(1,1)⊤. The fitted vector is c1u1=(2,2,0)⊤, and SSE=‖y‖2−c12=35−8=27, which is (1−2)2+(3−2)2+52.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that if τ is not a singular value of X, then rτ=min{rankB:‖X−B‖2≤τ}, and that the minimum is attained by the truncated SVD Xrτ.

Solution. ‖X−Xk‖2=σk+1. For k=rτ, σk+1≤τ (with σk+1=0 if k=rankX), so Xrτ is within τ and has rank rτ. If rankB=k<rτ, then by Proposition 1.8.2(c) ‖X−B‖2≥σk+1≥σrτ>τ. So no matrix of smaller rank is within τ.

Exercise (B2)

Let X=Q1R be a thin QR and R=URΣV⊤ an SVD of the triangle. Show that X=(Q1UR)ΣV⊤ is an SVD of X, and that the minimum-norm least squares solution can be computed from R and Q1⊤y alone. Why is this attractive when n is much larger than p?

Exercise (B3)

Show that bk minimizes ‖y−Xb‖ over b∈span(v1,…,vk), and that it is the minimum-norm least squares estimate for the matrix Xk. Interpret bk as least squares after deleting the directions in which X is nearly degenerate.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that the ridge estimate (X⊤X+λI)−1X⊤y equals ∑iσiσi2+λcivi. Derive its bias and variance in the form of Proposition 10.4.3, and show that for every β there is a λ>0 (depending on β and σ2) for which its mean squared error is below that of least squares.

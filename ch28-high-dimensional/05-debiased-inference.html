28.5  Post-selection and debiased inference

This section turns to confidence intervals and tests for a single coefficient βj when p>n. Two tempting approaches fail: least squares intervals for the model the lasso selected, as if it had been fixed in advance, and intervals around the lasso estimate itself, whose shrinkage bias is at least as large as its standard error. The fix is a one-step correction of the lasso, which turns out to be the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem with a lasso in place of a projection.

28.5.1. Why intervals after selection fail

The least squares interval of Theorem 12.1.1 has coverage 1−α for a model fixed before the data are seen. After selection, the interval for βj is computed only in the samples where xj was selected, which are those in which its estimated effect happened to be large. Conditional on selection the estimate is biased away from zero, and the interval is centred in the wrong place. This is the winner’s curse of Chapter 29 (Proposition 29.5.1), and the multiplicity problem of Chapter 13 in another form. With the lasso and p>n it is severe, because the selection is among hundreds or thousands of candidates.

Leeb and Pötscher (2006) proved that this cannot be repaired by estimating the distribution of the post-selection estimator, which depends on the unknown coefficients in a way that no estimator can track uniformly. One can change the target (the selected model’s coefficients, conditional on the selection) or the estimator. This section changes the estimator.

The lasso estimate itself is no better as a centre for an interval. On the event of Theorem 28.4.2, Eq. 28.4.1 shows that it equals least squares on S minus 𝛃λΣ^SS−1sign(βS). The shift is of order λ≍σlog⁡p/n, which is larger than the standard error σ/n of the oracle estimate by the factor log⁡p. An interval of the usual width centred at the lasso estimate misses the truth more and more often as p grows.

28.5.2. The debiased lasso

Write Σ^=X⊤X/n, and let Θ^ be a p×p matrix computed from X alone, meant to act like an inverse of Σ^ (which does not exist when p>n). Its rows are 𝛉𝛉θ^1⊤,…,θ^p⊤.

Definition 28.5.1 (Debiased lasso)

Given a lasso estimate 𝛃β^λ and Θ^, the debiased (or desparsified) lasso is

𝛃𝛃b^=β^λ+1nΘ^X⊤(Y−Xβ^λ).
(28.5.1)

Its jth entry uses only the row 𝛉θ^j. The quantity 𝛉μj=‖Σ^θ^j−ej‖∞ measures how far 𝛉θ^j is from being the jth row of an inverse of Σ^.

The correction adds to the lasso a multiple of the residual correlations 𝛃X⊤(Y−Xβ^λ)/n. If Σ^ were invertible and Θ^=Σ^−1, b^ would be the least squares estimate (Exercise (A1)).

Theorem 28.5.1 (Debiased lasso)

In the setting of Section 28.3, let Θ^ depend on X only, and let b^ be the debiased lasso.

  • (a) (Decomposition.) 𝛃n(b^−β)=w+r, where 𝛆w=Θ^X⊤ε/n∼Np(0,σ2Θ^Σ^Θ^⊤) exactly, and 𝛃𝛃r=−n(Θ^Σ^−I)(β^λ−β).

  • (b) (Remainder.) 𝛃𝛃|rj|≤nμj‖β^λ−β‖1. If λ≥2λ0, ϕ2(S)>0 and T occurs, then |rj|≤4nμjλs/ϕ2(S).

  • (c) (Variance.) If μj<1, then 𝛉𝛉ωj2=θ^j⊤Σ^θ^j≥(1−μj)2.

  • (d) (Asymptotic normality.) Consider a sequence of such problems with n→∞, in which p, s, X, 𝛃β and Θ^ may change with n and σ is fixed. Suppose λ=2λ0 with δ=δn→0 and log⁡(1/δn)=O(log⁡p); ϕ2(S)≥ϕ02>0; μj≤Clog⁡p/n for a constant C; slog⁡p/n→0; and σ^/σ→1 in probability. Then

    Tj=n(b^j−βj)σ^ωj → N(0,1)in distribution,
    (28.5.2)

    and the interval b^j±zα/2σ^ωj/n has coverage tending to 1−α.

Proof.

  • (a) Substitute 𝛃𝛆Y=Xβ+ε in Eq. 28.5.1 and write 𝛃𝛃Δ=β^λ−β: 𝛃𝛆𝛆b^−β=Δ+1nΘ^X⊤(ε−XΔ)=1nΘ^X⊤ε−(Θ^Σ^−I)Δ. Multiply by n. Since Θ^ is a fixed matrix, w is a linear function of 𝛆ε, hence normal with mean zero and covariance Θ^X⊤(σ2I)XΘ^⊤/n=σ2Θ^Σ^Θ^⊤ (Theorem 3.3.1).

  • (b) The jth row of Θ^Σ^−I is 𝛉(Σ^θ^j−ej)⊤, because Σ^ is symmetric. By Hölder’s inequality |rj|≤nμj‖Δ‖1. The second bound follows from Theorem 28.3.4(a).

  • (c) The columns of X have ‖xj‖2=n, so ej⊤Σ^ej=1. The jth entry of 𝛉Σ^θ^j differs from 1 by at most μj, so 𝛉ej⊤Σ^θ^j≥1−μj>0. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the nonnegative definite form Σ^ (Proposition 1.1.1 applied to Σ^1/2ej and 𝛉Σ^1/2θ^j) gives 𝛉(1−μj)2≤(ej⊤Σ^θ^j)2≤(ej⊤Σ^ej)ωj2=ωj2.

  • (d) Write Tj=(σ/σ^){wj/(σωj)+rj/(σωj)}. By (a), wj/(σωj) has exactly the N(0,1) law. For the remainder, log⁡(2p/δn)=log⁡2+log⁡p+log⁡(1/δn)≤C′log⁡p for a constant C′ and large n, so λ=2λ0≤2σ2C′log⁡p/n. On T, which has probability at least 1−δn→1, part (b) gives |rj|≤4n⋅Clog⁡pn⋅2σ2C′log⁡pn⋅sϕ02=82C′Cσϕ02⋅slog⁡pn → 0. By (c) and μj→0, ωj≥12 for large n. Hence rj/(σωj)→0 in probability, and Slutsky’s lemma (with σ/σ^→1) gives Eq. 28.5.2. The coverage statement is Pr(|Tj|≤zα/2)→1−α.

The theorem separates what the design must deliver (a Θ^ with small μj, and ϕ2(S) bounded below) from what the lasso delivers (an ℓ1 error of order slog⁡p/n). The condition slog⁡p/n→0 is stronger than the slog⁡p/n→0 that suffices for prediction: the remainder must be small compared with the standard error 1/n.

28.5.3. Constructing the approximate inverse

The remaining question is how to find a Θ^ with small μj when Σ^ is singular. The node-wise lasso regresses each column on all the others.

Lemma 28.5.2 (Node-wise lasso)

For a fixed j, let 𝛄γ^j∈Rp−1 be a lasso estimate, with penalty λj, for the regression of xj on the other columns X−j. Put 𝛄z^j=xj−X−jγ^j and τ^j2=xj⊤z^j/n, and let 𝛉θ^j have entry 1/τ^j2 in position j and 𝛄−γ^j/τ^j2 elsewhere. Then:

  • (a) 𝛄τ^j2=‖z^j‖2/n+λj‖γ^j‖1≥0;

  • (b) if τ^j2>0, then 𝛉(Σ^θ^j)j=1 and 𝛉μj=‖Σ^θ^j−ej‖∞≤λj/τ^j2;

  • (c) the jth debiased estimate is

    𝛃b^j=z^j⊤(Y−X−jβ^λ,−j)z^j⊤xj.
    (28.5.3)

Proof. The optimality conditions Eq. 28.3.2 for the node-wise regression say that 𝛋X−j⊤z^j/n=λjκ^, where 𝛋κ^ has entries of size at most 1 and equal to sign(γ^j,k) where γ^j,k≠0. Hence 𝛄𝛄γ^j⊤X−j⊤z^j/n=λj‖γ^j‖1, and since 𝛄xj=z^j+X−jγ^j, 𝛄τ^j2=xj⊤z^jn=‖z^j‖2n+λj‖γ^j‖1, which is (a). For (b), 𝛉𝛄Xθ^j=(xj−X−jγ^j)/τ^j2=z^j/τ^j2, so the kth entry of 𝛉Σ^θ^j is xk⊤z^j/(nτ^j2). For k=j this is 1 by the definition of τ^j2. For k≠j it is λjκ^k/τ^j2, of size at most λj/τ^j2. For (c), b^j is 𝛃β^λ,j+z^j⊤(Y−Xβ^λ)/(nτ^j2). Put nτ^j2=z^j⊤xj and 𝛃𝛃Xβ^λ=xjβ^λ,j+X−jβ^λ,−j; the terms in β^λ,j cancel.

Part (c) shows what the debiased lasso is. By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem (Theorem 6.6.2), the least squares coefficient of xj comes from regressing Y on the part of xj orthogonal to the other columns, which is zero when p>n. The node-wise lasso replaces it by a lasso residual z^j, nearly orthogonal to every other column by (b), and removes the other regressors’ effect approximately through 𝛃X−jβ^λ,−j. Javanmard and Montanari (2014) instead choose 𝛉θ^j to minimize 𝛉𝛉θ^⊤Σ^θ^ subject to 𝛉‖Σ^θ^−ej‖∞≤μ.

Remark (Sketch: when the assumptions of part (d) hold). This is a sketch; the full arguments are in van de Geer, Bühlmann, Ritov and Dezeure (2014) and Javanmard and Montanari (2014). Suppose the rows of X are independent Np(0,Σ), the eigenvalues of Σ are bounded away from 0 and ∞, and row j of Σ−1 has at most sj nonzero entries. The population regression of xj on the others then has sj nonzero coefficients and residual variance τj2=1/(Σ−1)jj. By the lemma μj≤λj/τ^j2 deterministically, so with λj≍log⁡p/n it remains to show τ^j2≈τj2. This follows from Section 28.3 applied to the node-wise regression, with the random-design bound of Section 28.2, when sjlog⁡p/n→0; the scaled lasso (Sun and Zhang 2012) supplies σ^. Then ωj2→(Σ−1)jj, which van de Geer and coauthors show to be the smallest asymptotic variance of a regular estimator.

Warning. The theorem covers one prespecified coefficient, or a fixed finite set. Coefficients chosen because they look large bring back the selection problem, and simultaneous intervals need a multiplicity correction such as Bonferroni (Theorem 13.4.1). With moderate n and a truth that is not very sparse, the remainder rj can be comparable to the noise, and coverage suffers.

28.5.4. A simulation

Example (Coverage of three intervals)

Take n=200, p=400, a fixed Gaussian design with correlations 0.5|j−k|, σ=1, and β1=1, β6=0.15, β11=−1, β16=0.15, all other coefficients zero. The targets are a strong coefficient β1, a weak one β6, and β2=0, whose regressor is correlated 0.5 with x1. In each of 1000 data sets we fit the lasso with λ=σ2log⁡p/n=0.245, below 2λ0 for the reason seen in Example (How tight are the bounds?), and compute three 95% intervals:

  • the naive interval: least squares on the selected variables and its t interval, reported only when the target was selected;
  • the debiased interval b^j±1.96σ^ωj/n, with node-wise lasso rows (λj=0.024) and σ^2 the lasso residual sum of squares divided by n minus the number of selected variables;
  • the oracle t interval from least squares on the true support plus the target.

The results are in Figure 28.5.1. For the null coefficient β2, the lasso selected x2 in a fraction 0.069 of the data sets, and in those the naive interval covered zero only in a fraction 0.174. For the weak coefficient, selected in a fraction 0.329, the naive coverage was 0.903, and for the strong one 0.915. The debiased intervals covered β1, β6 and β2 in fractions 0.960, 0.946 and 0.940 respectively, close to the oracle’s 0.943, 0.956 and 0.958 for the same three coefficients.

On the strong coefficient the lasso’s average error is −0.210, close to −λ, and the debiased estimate’s is 0.014. The standardized estimates T1 have mean 0.14 and standard deviation 0.95 (panel (a)). Two costs remain.

The debiased intervals are wider than the oracle’s (0.393 against 0.288 for β1): part of this is the price of not knowing the support, and part is tuning. The node-wise penalty λj=0.024, a tenth of 2log⁡p/n, keeps the bound μj≤λj/τ^j2 of Lemma 28.5.2, and so the remainder, small. But it lets the node-wise fits explain much of each column: τ^j2 (0.28, 0.25 and 0.27 for β1, β6 and β2) is well below the population value 1/(Σ−1)jj, which is 34 for j=1 and 35 otherwise, and that allows a larger ωj (Exercise (B2)).

The second cost is a remainder bias: 0.049 for β2 and 0.046 for the weak β6, about a third of its value. This is the term rj/n of Theorem 28.5.1, at most 𝛃𝛃μj‖β^λ−β‖1, with μj between 0.088 and 0.099 and an average lasso ℓ1 error of 0.74. At n=200 that product is not negligible.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def soft(z, t):
    """Soft thresholding S(z, t) = sign(z) max(|z| - t, 0)."""
    return np.sign(z) * np.maximum(np.abs(z) - t, 0.0)

def lasso_cd(X, Y, lam, B=None, tol=1e-9, max_sweeps=2000):
    """Minimize ||y - X b||^2 / (2n) + lam ||b||_1 for every column y of Y, by coordinate descent."""
    n, p = X.shape
    Y = Y.reshape(n, -1)
    B = np.zeros((p, Y.shape[1])) if B is None else B.copy()
    R = Y - X @ B                                    # residuals, one column per response
    scale = np.sum(X ** 2, axis=0) / n
    active = np.arange(p)
    for sweep in range(max_sweeps):
        change = 0.0
        for j in active:
            z = X[:, j] @ R / n + scale[j] * B[j]
            new = soft(z, lam) / scale[j]
            d = new - B[j]
            if np.any(d != 0):
                R -= np.outer(X[:, j], d)
                B[j] = new
                change = max(change, np.max(np.abs(d)))
        if change < tol:
            if len(active) == p:                     # converged over all coordinates
                break
            active = np.arange(p)                    # check every coordinate once more
        else:
            active = np.flatnonzero(np.any(B != 0, axis=1)) if sweep % 5 else np.arange(p)
    return B

def nodewise_row(X, j, lam_j):
    """Row theta_j of Theta from the lasso of x_j on the other columns, and tau_j^2."""
    n, p = X.shape
    others = np.delete(np.arange(p), j)
    gamma = lasso_cd(X[:, others], X[:, j], lam_j)[:, 0]
    z = X[:, j] - X[:, others] @ gamma               # lasso residual of x_j on the rest
    tau2 = X[:, j] @ z / n
    theta = np.zeros(p)
    theta[j] = 1.0
    theta[others] = -gamma
    return theta / tau2, tau2

def debiased_interval(X, Y, B, j, theta, level=0.95):
    """Debiased estimate of beta_j and its normal-theory interval, one per column of Y."""
    n = X.shape[0]
    R = Y - X @ B
    b = B[j] + theta @ X.T @ R / n                    # one-step correction
    sigma_hat = np.sqrt(np.sum(R ** 2, axis=0) / (n - np.sum(B != 0, axis=0)))
    G = X.T @ X / n
    se = sigma_hat * np.sqrt(theta @ G @ theta / n)
    z = stats.norm.ppf((1 + level) / 2)
    return b, b - z * se, b + z * se

def toeplitz_design(rng, n, p, rho):
    idx = np.arange(p)
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(rho ** np.abs(idx[:, None] - idx[None, :]))
    X = rng.normal(size=(n, p)) @ L.T
    return X / np.sqrt(np.sum(X ** 2, axis=0) / n)

rng = np.random.default_rng(2850)
n, p, sigma = 200, 400, 1.0
X = toeplitz_design(rng, n, p, rho=0.5)
beta = np.zeros(p)
beta[[0, 5, 10, 15]] = [1.0, 0.15, -1.0, 0.15]
lam = sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.log(p) / n)
targets = {"strong": 0, "weak": 5, "null": 1}

Ys = (X @ beta)[:, None] + sigma * rng.normal(size=(n, 200))
Bs = lasso_cd(X, Ys, lam)
for name, j in targets.items():
    theta, tau2 = nodewise_row(X, j, 0.1 * np.sqrt(2 * np.log(p) / n))
    b, lo, hi = debiased_interval(X, Ys, Bs, j, theta)
    print(f"{name:6s}: lasso bias {Bs[j].mean() - beta[j]:+.3f}, debiased bias {b.mean() - beta[j]:+.3f},"
          f" coverage {np.mean((lo <= beta[j]) & (beta[j] <= hi)):.3f}")

28.5.5. Other routes to valid inference

Other approaches answer different questions. Sample splitting (Wasserman and Roeder 2009) selects on one half of the data and fits on the other (Exercise (B1)). Selective inference (Lee, Sun, Sun and Taylor 2016) conditions on the lasso having chosen the reported model, a union of polyhedra in Y. Simultaneous inference over all models (Berk, Brown, Buja, Zhang and Zhao 2013) widens the intervals, as Scheffé’s method does. These target the coefficients of the selected model, which generally differ from βj. Double selection (Belloni, Chernozhukov and Hansen 2014) targets βj: it keeps the controls that predict the response or xj and refits least squares, the idea of Eq. 28.5.3.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

(Sample splitting.) Split the observations into halves I1 and I2 chosen independently of the data. Select a set S^ of columns using only the data in I1, and suppose XI2,S^ has full column rank and |S^|<|I2|. Show that if S^⊇S, then conditionally on the data in I1, the usual t interval for βj (j∈S^) from the least squares fit of YI2 on XI2,S^ has exact coverage 1−α. What does the fit estimate when S^ misses an active variable?

Solution. The errors in I2 are independent of those in I1, so conditionally on the data in I1 the set S^ is fixed and 𝛃YI2∼N(XI2β,σ2I). If S^⊇S, then 𝛃𝛃XI2β=XI2,S^βS^, so the fit on XI2,S^ is a correctly specified normal linear model with coefficients 𝛃βS^, and the t interval of Theorem 12.1.1 has exact conditional coverage. Averaging over the data in I1, coverage is exact on the event S^⊇S. If S^ misses an active variable, the fit estimates the projected coefficients 𝛃(XI2,S^⊤XI2,S^)−1XI2,S^⊤XI2β (Proposition 5.5.7), which differ from 𝛃βS^ when the missed variables are correlated with the selected ones, and the residual mean square overestimates σ2.

Exercise (B2)

In Lemma 28.5.2, show that 𝛉𝛉ωj2=θ^j⊤Σ^θ^j=‖z^j‖2/(nτ^j4), and hence ωj2≤1/τ^j2. Interpret: a small τ^j2 (a column that the others explain well) means a large variance for b^j. Compare with the variance inflation factor (Definition 26.2.1).

Solution. 𝛉Xθ^j=z^j/τ^j2, so 𝛉ωj2=‖Xθ^j‖2/n=‖z^j‖2/(nτ^j4). By Lemma 28.5.2(a), ‖z^j‖2/n≤τ^j2, so ωj2≤1/τ^j2. With least squares in place of the lasso (p<n, λj=0), τ^j2=‖(I−M(j))xj‖2/n, and ωj2=1/τ^j2 is the variance factor n/{Sjj(1−Rj2)} of Eq. 6.6.2 for a column with ‖xj‖2=n (and an intercept among the other columns). The node-wise lasso residual plays the role of the FWL residual, and 1/τ^j2 the role of the variance inflation.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

The two quantities in Theorem 28.5.1 pull against each other: μj controls the remainder and ωj the standard error, and a 𝛉θ^j that makes one small makes the other large. This exercise computes the trade-off exactly.

  • (a) Suppose p<n and Σ^ is invertible. Show that the only 𝛉θ with μj=0, that is with 𝛉Σ^θ=ej, gives ωj2=(Σ^−1)jj, the least squares variance factor of Exercise (B2).

  • (b) Let p=2, Σ^=(1ρρ1) with 0<ρ<1, and j=1. Substituting 𝛉(a,b)⊤=Σ^θ, show that 𝛉𝛉θ⊤Σ^θ=(a2−2ρab+b2)/(1−ρ2) while the constraint 𝛉‖Σ^θ−e1‖∞≤μ reads |a−1|≤μ and |b|≤μ.

  • (c) Show that for 0<μ≤ρ/(1+ρ) the minimum is at (a,b)=(1−μ,μ), with value ω12(μ)=1−2(1+ρ)μ(1−μ)1−ρ2, so that ω12(0)=(Σ^−1)11 and ddμω12(0)=−2/(1−ρ).

  • (d) Read the result against Theorem 28.5.1(b) and (c), and against the tuning reported in

Solution.

  • (a) 𝛉Σ^θ=ej has the unique solution 𝛉θ=Σ^−1ej, and 𝛉𝛉θ⊤Σ^θ=ej⊤Σ^−1ej=(Σ^−1)jj. By Exercise (A1) the debiased estimate is then least squares, with exactly that variance factor.

  • (b) 𝛉θ=Σ^−1(a,b)⊤, so 𝛉𝛉θ⊤Σ^θ=(a,b)Σ^−1(a,b)⊤, and Σ^−1=(1−ρ2)−1(1−ρ−ρ1) gives the stated form. The constraint is a constraint on (a,b) directly.

  • (c) Write f(a,b)=(a2−2ρab+b2)/(1−ρ2) on the box [1−μ,1+μ]×[−μ,μ]. Then ∂f/∂a=2(a−ρb)/(1−ρ2), and on the box a−ρb≥(1−μ)−ρμ>0 because μ≤ρ/(1+ρ)<1/(1+ρ); so f increases in a and a=1−μ. Next ∂f/∂b=2(b−ρa)/(1−ρ2) vanishes at b=ρ(1−μ), which exceeds μ exactly when μ<ρ/(1+ρ); so f decreases in b throughout [−μ,μ] and b=μ. Substituting, (1−μ)2−2ρ(1−μ)μ+μ2=1−2(1+ρ)μ+2(1+ρ)μ2, which is the stated numerator. Setting μ=0 gives 1/(1−ρ2), and differentiating at μ=0 gives −2(1+ρ)/(1−ρ2)=−2/(1−ρ).

  • (d) A relaxation of the constraint from μ=0 to a small μ lowers the variance factor at the linear rate 2/(1−ρ), fast when the columns are correlated, while by (b) of the theorem the remainder bound 𝛃𝛃nμj‖β^λ−β‖1 grows linearly in μ at the same time. The theorem’s condition μj≤Clog⁡p/n is a choice of a point on this curve: it is what makes the remainder vanish beside 1/n, at the price of the variance the exact inverse would have delivered. Part (c) of the theorem gives the floor ωj2≥(1−μj)2, so the variance cannot be driven below about one however μ is chosen. The simulation of 1 sits at the other end: its node-wise penalty is a tenth of 2log⁡p/n, which keeps μj near 0.09 and the remainder small, and pays for it with a τ^j2 far below its population value and hence intervals wider than the oracle’s.

Exercise (C2)

Why one correction and not two? Write T(b)=b+Θ^X⊤(Y−Xb)/n, so that 𝛃b^=T(β^λ), and put K=Θ^Σ^−I.

  • (a) Show that 𝛃𝛃T(b)−β=−K(b−β)+w/n with w as in Theorem 28.5.1(a), and deduce that the k-fold iterate satisfies 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃n(Tk(β^λ)−β)=(−K)knΔ+∑i=0k−1(−K)iw,Δ=β^λ−β.

  • (b) With μ=maxkμk, show that ‖KΔ‖1≤pμ‖Δ‖1, and hence that the bound of Theorem 28.5.1(b) on the jth remainder is multiplied by pμ for each extra iteration.

  • (c) Show that under the conditions of Theorem 28.5.1(d) with p>n one has pμ≥nlog⁡pC−1→∞ up to the constant, so that the guarantee is lost after one extra step, however small μ is.

  • (d) Show that when p<n and Θ^=Σ^−1, K=0 and every iterate equals the least squares estimate, so nothing of this happens in low dimension.

Solution.

  • (a) Substituting 𝛃𝛆Y=Xβ+ε, 𝛃𝛆Y−Xb=X(β−b)+ε, so 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛆𝛃T(b)−β=(b−β)−Θ^Σ^(b−β)+Θ^X⊤ε/n=−K(b−β)+w/n, with 𝛆w=Θ^X⊤ε/n. The map is affine with linear part −K; iterating it k times from 𝛃β^λ and multiplying by n gives the display. At k=1 it is Theorem 28.5.1(a).

  • (b) The kth row of K is 𝛉(Σ^θ^k−ek)⊤, so |(KΔ)k|≤μk‖Δ‖1≤μ‖Δ‖1 by Hölder, and summing over the p coordinates, ‖KΔ‖1≤pμ‖Δ‖1. Applying the jth-row bound once more, |{(K)2Δ}j|≤μj‖KΔ‖1≤pμ⋅μj‖Δ‖1, and by induction each further step costs a factor pμ.

  • (c) The one-step remainder is controlled by taking μ of the order log⁡p/n, which is what the condition μj≤Clog⁡p/n asks for and what Lemma 28.5.2 delivers. At that order, and with p>n, pμ≍plog⁡pn ≥ nlog⁡pn=nlog⁡p → ∞. The ℓ1 norm of the error is spread over p coordinates, and each application of K trades a factor μ against a factor p; with p larger than n, p wins.

  • (d) Then Θ^Σ^=I, so K=0 and 𝛃𝛃T(b)=β+w/n=β^ for every b, by Exercise (A1). The iteration converges in one step because the correction is exact.

The moral is that Eq. 28.5.1 is not the first step of a convergent scheme. It is the one step whose error the design can be made to control, and the noise term of (a) also changes as soon as k>1, since ∑i(−K)iw no longer has the covariance σ2ωj2 that the interval uses.

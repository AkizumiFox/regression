28.1  Minimum-norm interpolation

Gene expression studies measure tens of thousands of genes on a few hundred patients, and a model with many splines, interactions or random features easily has more columns than rows. This section asks what least squares does when p>n. It does not fail, but it no longer picks out one answer, and we study the answer that the SVD and the common algorithms return.

28.1.1. Least squares when p exceeds n

Suppose rank(X)=n<p, the usual case (a matrix with continuously distributed entries has full rank with probability one). Three consequences follow from Parts I and II.

First, C(X)=Rn, so M=I: every least squares fit reproduces the data, the residuals are zero, R2=1, and the estimator s2 of Theorem 5.6.1 has n−r=0 in its denominator. The data contain no internal information about σ2.

Second, N(X) has dimension p−n≥1, and the least squares estimates form the affine set X+y+N(X) (Theorem 10.4.1). Coefficient vectors that differ by an element of N(X) give the same mean, so the data cannot tell them apart.

Third, 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β is estimable iff 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤) (Theorem 8.2.2), and a coordinate vector ej lies in the n-dimensional row space only if it is orthogonal to N(X), which fails for generic designs. For a generic X with p>n, no single coefficient is estimable. Any method that answers questions about individual coefficients is adding information, explicitly or through its algorithm.

Example (Two interpolators)

Take n=4 observations on p=6 regressors, with the design of the listing below and y=(1,2,0,−1)⊤. The minimum-norm solution 𝛃β^+=X+y (Proposition 1.4.3, Proposition 6.10.1) has length 1.322. Adding twice a unit vector of N(X) gives a second solution of length 2.398. Both reproduce y exactly. They disagree about the coefficients: the first, for instance, is −0.002 in one and −0.059 in the other. They also disagree about predictions at a point that was not observed: at x0=1 they predict 2.131 and 2.588. The data give no reason to prefer either. The minimum-norm solution is singled out by a choice, not by the data.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2801)
n, p = 4, 6
X = np.round(rng.normal(size=(n, p)), 1)            # a 4 x 6 design, rank 4
y = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 0.0, -1.0])

b_plus = X.T @ np.linalg.solve(X @ X.T, y)          # X^T (X X^T)^{-1} y
print("min-norm solution ", np.round(b_plus, 3))
print("residual norm     ", np.linalg.norm(y - X @ b_plus))

# another interpolator: add a null-space vector of X
_, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X)
b_other = b_plus + 2.0 * Vt[-1]                     # Vt[-1] spans part of N(X)
print("other solution    ", np.round(b_other, 3))
print("residual norm     ", np.linalg.norm(y - X @ b_other))
print("norms", np.linalg.norm(b_plus), np.linalg.norm(b_other))

x0 = np.ones(p)                                      # a new point
print("predictions at x0:", x0 @ b_plus, x0 @ b_other)

28.1.2. The minimum-norm solution

In this chapter the singular values of X are written d1≥⋯≥dr>0, so that σ stays free for the error standard deviation. By Theorem 1.8.1, X=∑i=1rdiuivi⊤,X+=∑i=1rdi−1viui⊤, with orthonormal u1,…,ur∈Rn and v1,…,vr∈Rp. The matrix

P=X+X=∑i=1rvivi⊤
(28.1.1)

is the orthogonal projection onto the row space C(X⊤)=span(v1,…,vr). The next theorem collects what is known about 𝛃β^+=X+y when p>n. Part (a) restates earlier results in the form needed here. Parts (b) and (c) show that 𝛃β^+ is what two common procedures produce when they are pushed to the limit. Part (d) gives its bias and variance.

Theorem 28.1.1 (The minimum-norm interpolator)

Let X be n×p with rank r and singular values d1≥⋯≥dr>0, and let 𝛃β^+=X+y.

  • (a) (Characterization.) The least squares estimates are the vectors 𝛃β^++v with v∈N(X). Among them 𝛃β^+ is the only one in C(X⊤), and it has the smallest Euclidean norm. If r=n, then 𝛃β^+=X⊤(XX⊤)−1y and every least squares estimate interpolates: 𝛃Xβ^+=y.

  • (b) (Ridgeless limit.) For λ>0 let 𝛃β^λ=(X⊤X+λI)−1X⊤y be the ridge estimate. Then 𝛃𝛃𝛃‖β^λ−β^+‖≤(λ/dr2)‖β^+‖, so 𝛃𝛃β^λ→β^+ as λ↓0.

  • (c) (Gradient descent.) Let b0∈Rp, 0<η<2/d12 and bt+1=bt+ηX⊤(y−Xbt). Then 𝛃bt→β^++(I−P)b0. In particular, gradient descent started at b0=0 converges to 𝛃β^+.

  • (d) (Bias and variance.) If 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ and Cov(Y)=σ2I, then 𝛃E(X+Y)=Pβ and Cov(X+Y)=σ2∑i≤rdi−2vivi⊤. For every a∈Rp,

    𝛃𝛃E(a⊤X+Y−a⊤β)2=(a⊤(I−P)β)2+σ2∑i=1r(a⊤vi)2di2,
    (28.1.2)

    and 𝛃𝛃E‖X+Y−β‖2=‖(I−P)β‖2+σ2∑i≤rdi−2.

Proof.

  • (a) The first two statements are Theorem 10.4.1, and Proposition 1.4.3 applied to the normal equations of Theorem 6.4.2; 𝛃β^+=∑i(ui⊤y/di)vi lies in span(v1,…,vr)=C(X⊤). If r=n, then XX⊤ is a nonsingular n×n matrix, and X⊤(XX⊤)−1 satisfies the four conditions of Definition 1.3.2, so it equals X+. Moreover C(X)=Rn, so the projection onto C(X) is I and every least squares fit equals y.

  • (b) Write ci=ui⊤y. By Theorem 27.2.2(a), 𝛃β^λ=∑i≤rdicivi/(di2+λ), and Theorem 27.2.2(d) gives the limit 𝛃β^+=∑i≤r(ci/di)vi; only the rate is new. Subtracting, 𝛃𝛃β^+−β^λ=∑i≤r{λ/(di2+λ)}(ci/di)vi, so by orthonormality 𝛃𝛃𝛃‖β^+−β^λ‖2≤(λ/dr2)2∑i(ci/di)2=(λ/dr2)2‖β^+‖2.

  • (c) Complete v1,…,vr to an orthonormal basis of Rp with vectors vr+1,…,vp spanning N(X). Since X⊤(y−Xb)=∑i≤rdi(ci−divi⊤b)vi, the coordinates of bt evolve separately: vi⊤bt+1=(1−ηdi2)vi⊤bt+ηdici(i≤r),vi⊤bt+1=vi⊤bt(i>r). For i≤r, subtracting the fixed point ci/di gives vi⊤bt−ci/di=(1−ηdi2)t(vi⊤b0−ci/di)→0, because 0<ηdi2<2. The coordinates with i>r never move. So 𝛃bt→∑i≤r(ci/di)vi+∑i>r(vi⊤b0)vi=β^++(I−P)b0.

  • (d) 𝛃𝛃E(X+Y)=X+Xβ=Pβ, and Cov(X+Y)=σ2X+(X+)⊤=σ2∑idi−2vivi⊤ (Theorem 2.2.1). The mean squared error of a⊤X+Y is its squared bias 𝛃(a⊤(P−I)β)2 plus its variance, which is Eq. 28.1.2. Summing Eq. 28.1.2 over a=e1,…,ep gives the last formula.

Parts (b) and (c) explain why 𝛃β^+ matters although nobody chooses it on purpose. Gradient iterates started at zero never leave C(X⊤), because every gradient X⊤(y−Xb) lies in it. Algorithms for large models are typically started near zero and run until the training error vanishes, and in the linear case they return the minimum-norm interpolator. This is called the implicit regularization of the algorithm.

for lam in [1.0, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    b_ridge = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + lam * np.eye(p), X.T @ y)
    print(f"lambda = {lam:6.3f}   distance to min-norm {np.linalg.norm(b_ridge - b_plus):.2e}")

# gradient descent on ||y - X b||^2 / 2 started at zero
step = 1.0 / np.linalg.norm(X, 2) ** 2
b = np.zeros(p)
for t in range(5000):
    b = b + step * X.T @ (y - X @ b)
print("gradient descent: distance to min-norm", np.linalg.norm(b - b_plus))

For the design of Example (Two interpolators) the ridge estimate at λ=0.01 is within 0.0132 of 𝛃β^+, and the distance shrinks in proportion to λ, as part (b) predicts.

In part (d), the bias 𝛃(I−P)β is the component of 𝛃β in the null space, which the data cannot see and the minimum-norm solution sets to zero. The variance is the familiar σ2/di2 of Theorem 10.4.1, summed over the directions the data do see. So 𝛃a⊤β^+ is unbiased for all 𝛃β exactly when 𝛃a⊤β is estimable (Exercise (A2)). For any other a, its bias depends on the invisible part of 𝛃β and can be anything.

28.1.3. The risk with random regressors

To say whether interpolation predicts well, the design must be random, since prediction is at new regressor values. The cleanest case has independent standard normal regressors, where both terms of part (d) can be averaged exactly.

Proposition 28.1.2 (Risk under isotropic regressors)

Let the rows of X be independent Np(0,Ip) vectors, let 𝛃𝛆Y=Xβ+ε with 𝛆ε∼Nn(0,σ2I) independent of X, and let x0∼Np(0,Ip) be independent of both. The prediction risk 𝛃R=E(x0⊤X+Y−x0⊤β)2 is

𝛃R={σ2pn−p−1,p≤n−2,‖β‖2(1−np)+σ2np−n−1,p≥n+2.
(28.1.3)

Proof. Given X and Y, the vector 𝛃β^+=X+Y is fixed and 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E{(x0⊤(β^+−β))2∣X,Y}=‖β^+−β‖2, because Cov(x0)=I. So 𝛃𝛃R=E‖β^+−β‖2, and by Theorem 28.1.1(d) applied conditionally on X, 𝛃R=E‖(I−P)β‖2+σ2E∑i=1rdi−2.

Case p≤n−2. X⊤X=∑ix(i)x(i)⊤ has the Wishart law Wp(n,I) (Definition 14.2.1), so by Lemma 14.2.4 it is nonsingular with probability one and E(X⊤X)−1=I/(n−p−1). Then r=p, P=I, the bias term vanishes, and ∑idi−2=tr(X⊤X)−1 has mean p/(n−p−1).

Case p≥n+2. The p columns of X are independent Nn(0,In) vectors, so XX⊤∼Wn(p,In). By Lemma 14.2.4 again, XX⊤ is nonsingular with probability one, so r=n. The numbers di2 are also the eigenvalues of XX⊤, so ∑idi−2=tr(XX⊤)−1, with mean n/(p−n−1). For the bias, let Q be any orthogonal p×p matrix. The rows of XQ are independent Np(0,Q⊤Q)=Np(0,I) vectors (Theorem 3.3.1), so XQ has the same law as X. One checks from Definition 1.3.2 that (XQ)+=Q⊤X+, so the row-space projection of XQ is Q⊤PQ. Hence P¯=EP satisfies P¯=Q⊤P¯Q for every orthogonal Q. Then u⊤P¯u takes the same value c at every unit vector u, since any unit vector is Qe1 for some Q. A symmetric matrix whose quadratic form is constant on the unit sphere is cI, by Theorem 1.7.7. Taking traces, pc=EtrP=Er=n. Therefore 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E‖(I−P)β‖2=β⊤(I−EP)β=‖β‖2(1−n/p).

Below p=n the fit is unbiased and its variance grows like p/(n−p−1), which explodes as p approaches n. Above p=n the variance falls again, like n/(p−n−1). The fit has to interpolate n noisy values, and with many more directions available the interpolator can spread the noise over them thinly. The price is the bias: the fit recovers only the fraction n/p of 𝛃‖β‖2. The formulas do not cover |p−n|≤1.

28.1.4. Double descent

In applications p is usually a choice, such as the number of basis functions. To compare choices of p on one problem, let the response depend on a pool of D features and let the fit use only the first p. The features left out act as extra noise.

Corollary 28.1.3 (Fitting the first p of D features)

Let x∼ND(0,ID), 𝛃Y=x⊤β+ε with ε∼N(0,σ2) independent of x, and let n independent copies be observed. For p≤D, split 𝛃𝛃𝛃β=(β(1)⊤,β(2)⊤)⊤ into its first p and last D−p entries, write 𝛃ω2=‖β(2)‖2, fit the minimum-norm least squares estimate 𝛃β^+ (Proposition 6.10.1) to the first p features, and predict with 𝛃(β^+⊤,0⊤)⊤. With σp2=σ2+ω2, the excess prediction risk at an independent new x0 is ω2 plus the right-hand side of Eq. 28.1.3 with 𝛃β(1) in place of 𝛃β and σp2 in place of σ2.

Proof. Split X=[X1,X2] in the same way. Then 𝛃𝛃𝛆Y=X1β(1)+(X2β(2)+ε). The entries of X2 are independent of X1, so the bracket is a Nn(0,σp2I) vector independent of X1. Proposition 28.1.2 applies with X1, 𝛃β(1) and σp2, and gives 𝛃𝛃E‖β^+−β(1)‖2. At the new point, the prediction error 𝛃𝛃𝛃x0,1⊤(β^+−β(1))−x0,2⊤β(2) is a sum of two uncorrelated terms with mean zero, so its mean square is 𝛃𝛃E‖β^+−β(1)‖2+ω2.

Example (Two shapes of signal)

Take n=50 observations, a pool of D=200 features, σ2=0.25 and 𝛃‖β‖2=1. Figure 28.1.1 plots the exact risk of Corollary 28.1.3 against p for two coefficient vectors, with simulated values as a check. It also shows the risk of ridge regression (Theorem 27.2.2) with the best penalty for each p.

In panel (a) all 200 coefficients are equal, so each feature left out adds to the noise. No model with p<n beats predicting zero, which has risk 1.000. The risk climbs to 12.31 at p=45, is 12.94 at p=55, and then falls. The overall minimum is at p=200: 0.834. Here the best prediction comes from interpolating with every feature.

In panel (b) the coefficients decay geometrically, and the classical picture holds: a small model with p=9 has risk 0.078, and nothing to the right of the peak comes close. At p=D the risk is 0.834 again. When all features are used, Eq. 28.1.3 depends on 𝛃β only through 𝛃‖β‖.

A risk curve that falls, peaks at p=n and falls again is called double descent. Three cautions keep it in proportion.

  • The peak belongs to the estimator, not to the problem. It comes from the variance term σ2∑idi−2, large when the smallest nonzero singular value is near zero, as for p≈n. Ridge with the best penalty has no peak, and its best values, 0.811 and 0.073, are slightly below the best minimum-norm values, as Theorem 28.1.1(b) requires.
  • The second descent need not win. That depends on how the signal is spread, as the panels show.
  • Isotropic features are a special case. Hastie, Montanari, Rosset and Tibshirani (2022) treat general covariances as n,p→∞ with p/n fixed, and Bartlett, Long, Lugosi and Tsigler (2020) find when interpolation is benign, with risk still tending to that of the best predictor.
import numpy as np

def exact_risk(p, n, beta, sigma2):
    """Excess prediction risk of min-norm least squares on the first p of D isotropic features."""
    kept, left = np.sum(beta[:p] ** 2), np.sum(beta[p:] ** 2)
    noise = sigma2 + left                           # omitted features act as extra noise
    if p == 0:
        return left
    if p <= n - 2:
        return left + noise * p / (n - p - 1)
    if p >= n + 2:
        return left + kept * (1 - n / p) + noise * n / (p - n - 1)
    return np.inf                                   # |p - n| <= 1: not covered by the formula

def simulated_risk(p, n, beta, sigma2, reps, rng, lams=()):
    """Average over designs of the conditional risk given X; also ridge for each penalty."""
    bS, left = beta[:p], np.sum(beta[p:] ** 2)
    noise = sigma2 + left
    out, ridge = [], np.zeros(len(lams))
    for _ in range(reps):
        X = rng.normal(size=(n, p))
        _, d, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
        c = Vt @ bS                                 # coordinates of beta_S in the row space
        bias2 = bS @ bS - c @ c                     # ||(I - P) beta_S||^2
        out.append(left + bias2 + noise * np.sum(1 / d ** 2))
        if len(lams):
            shrink = d ** 2 / (d ** 2 + np.asarray(lams)[:, None])     # one row per penalty
            ridge += left + bias2 + np.sum(((1 - shrink) * c) ** 2, axis=1) \
                + noise * np.sum(shrink ** 2 / d ** 2, axis=1)
    return np.mean(out), np.std(out) / np.sqrt(reps), ridge / reps

n, D, sigma2 = 20, 100, 0.25
beta = np.full(D, 1 / np.sqrt(D))                  # signal spread evenly, ||beta||^2 = 1
rng = np.random.default_rng(2802)
for p in [5, 10, 15, 25, 40, 100]:
    sim, se, _ = simulated_risk(p, n, beta, sigma2, reps=200, rng=rng)
    print(f"p = {p:3d}: formula {exact_risk(p, n, beta, sigma2):.3f}   simulation {sim:.3f}")

For the rest of the chapter we take a different route. Instead of letting the algorithm decide which of the many solutions to report, we assume that the truth is simple: only a few coefficients are nonzero. That assumption is strong, but it is explicit, and it can be exploited through the lasso.

28.1.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let X be n×p with an intercept column and rank(X)=n<p. Show that every least squares fit has R2=1, and explain why neither the F test of Theorem 11.1.1 nor the residual-based diagnostics of Chapter 20 can be used. What are the leverages hii?

Exercise (A2)

In the setting of Theorem 28.1.1(d), show that a⊤X+Y is unbiased for 𝛃a⊤β for every 𝛃β iff a∈C(X⊤). Show also that for such a its variance is σ2a⊤(X⊤X)+a, and that it equals the least squares estimator 𝛃a⊤β^ of Theorem 6.4.4 for any least squares estimate 𝛃β^.

Solution. The bias is 𝛃a⊤(P−I)β, which vanishes for all 𝛃β iff (I−P)a=0, that is, iff a∈C(P)=C(X⊤). The variance is σ2∑i(a⊤vi)2/di2, and ∑idi−2vivi⊤ is the Moore–Penrose inverse of X⊤X=∑idi2vivi⊤ (Theorem 1.8.1). Finally, any least squares estimate is 𝛃β^++v with v∈N(X), and a⊤v=0 because a∈C(X⊤)=N(X)⊥, so 𝛃𝛃a⊤β^=a⊤β^+.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show directly from the update in Theorem 28.1.1(c) that (I−P)bt=(I−P)b0 for all t. Deduce that gradient descent started at b0 returns the least squares estimate closest to b0.

Exercise (B2)

Fix X of rank r and let 𝛃𝛃m(λ)=E‖β^λ−β‖2 for the ridge estimate, with m(0) the risk of 𝛃β^+ in Theorem 28.1.1(d). Show that 𝛃𝛃m(λ)=‖(I−P)β‖2+∑i=1r[(λdi2+λ)2(vi⊤β)2+σ2di2(di2+λ)2], and that m′(0)<0 whenever σ>0. So some ridge estimate beats the minimum-norm interpolator for every 𝛃β. Compare Theorem 27.2.3.

Solution. The linear representation in Theorem 27.2.2(a) holds for any rank r. Applied to 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ, whose coordinates are 𝛃𝛃ui⊤Xβ=divi⊤β, it gives 𝛃𝛃Eβ^λ=∑i≤r{di2/(di2+λ)}(vi⊤β)vi, and the covariance is σ2∑i≤rdi2(di2+λ)−2vivi⊤, as in Theorem 27.2.2(c). What r<p adds is the bias component 𝛃−(I−P)β in the null space, orthogonal to the rest of the bias 𝛃−∑i{λ/(di2+λ)}(vi⊤β)vi; it contributes 𝛃‖(I−P)β‖2. Adding the squared bias and the trace of the covariance gives m(λ). Differentiating term by term, the first bracket has derivative 𝛃2λdi2(vi⊤β)2/(di2+λ)3, which is zero at λ=0. The second has derivative −2σ2di2/(di2+λ)3, which equals −2σ2/di4<0 at λ=0. Hence m′(0)=−2σ2∑idi−4<0, and m(λ)<m(0) for small λ>0.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Check Eq. 28.1.3 for n=1 directly. With one observation (x,y), show that 𝛃β^+=xy/‖x‖2, and use E(1/χ2(p))=1/(p−2) for p≥3 to show that 𝛃R=‖β‖2(1−1/p)+σ2/(p−2).

Solution. Here X=x⊤ and XX⊤=‖x‖2, so 𝛃β^+=xy/‖x‖2 and 𝛃𝛃𝛃β^+−β=−(I−xx⊤/‖x‖2)β+xε/‖x‖2, a sum of orthogonal vectors. So 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃‖β^+−β‖2=‖β‖2−(x⊤β)2‖x‖2+ε2‖x‖2. The matrix P=xx⊤/‖x‖2 is the row-space projection, and the rotation argument in the proof of Proposition 28.1.2 gives EP=I/p, so 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E(x⊤β)2/‖x‖2=β⊤(EP)β=‖β‖2/p. And ‖x‖2∼χ2(p) is independent of ε, so Eε2/‖x‖2=σ2/(p−2). This agrees with Eq. 28.1.3 at n=1.

Exercise (C2)

In Proposition 28.1.2 let n,p→∞ with n/p→γ∈(0,1) and 𝛃‖β‖2 fixed. Show that 𝛃R→‖β‖2(1−γ)+σ2γ/(1−γ), and that this is below the risk 𝛃‖β‖2 of predicting zero iff 𝛃‖β‖2/σ2>1/(1−γ). What happens to the limit as γ→0?

26.1  What collinearity does to variance

The columns of a model matrix are collinear when a nontrivial linear combination of them is zero, and nearly collinear when a combination with coefficients of reasonable size is a short vector. The exact case is rank deficiency, settled in Chapter 8. Here X has full column rank and every linear function of 𝛃β is estimable, yet some are estimated so poorly that in practice they have not been estimated at all; “collinearity” below means this near case.

Section 19.6 wrote the variance of 𝛃a⊤β^ as a sum over the eigenvectors of X⊤X (Proposition 19.6.1). This section says which functions collinearity damages, links them to estimability, and separates the statistical problem from the numerical one of Chapter 10.

26.1.1. Canonical coordinates

Throughout the section X is n×p of rank p, 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ and Cov(Y)=σ2I. Write the spectral decomposition (Theorem 1.7.2) as X⊤X=∑ℓ=1pλℓvℓvℓ⊤,λ1≥λ2≥⋯≥λp>0, with orthonormal eigenvectors v1,…,vp, and put 𝛃γℓ=vℓ⊤β. The numbers γℓ are the coordinates of 𝛃β in the eigenvector basis, the canonical coordinates of the regression. Since Xvℓ has squared length λℓ, the mean vector is

𝛃Xβ=∑ℓ=1pγℓXvℓ=∑ℓ=1pλℓγℓuℓ,uℓ=Xvℓ/λℓ,
(26.1.1)

and the vectors uℓ are the left singular vectors of X (Theorem 1.8.1). A small λℓ means that a unit change in γℓ moves the mean vector only by λℓ. The data then carry little information about γℓ, and the following theorem makes this precise.

Theorem 26.1.1 (Variances in canonical coordinates)

Under the assumptions above, let 𝛃γ^ℓ=vℓ⊤β^.

  • (a) γ^ℓ=uℓ⊤Y/λℓ, and 𝛃β^=∑ℓγ^ℓvℓ. The γ^ℓ are uncorrelated, with Var(γ^ℓ)=σ2/λℓ.

  • (b) For every nonzero a∈Rp, 𝛃σ2‖a‖2λ1≤Var(a⊤β^)≤σ2‖a‖2λp, with equality on the left at a=v1 and on the right at a=vp. The ratio of the largest to the smallest variance of 𝛃a⊤β^ over unit vectors a is κ(X)2.

  • (c) If a∈span{v1,…,vk}, then 𝛃Var(a⊤β^)≤σ2‖a‖2/λk: the eigenvalues λk+1,…,λp do not enter. For every a, 𝛃Var(a⊤β^)≥σ2(a⊤vp)2/λp.

  • (d) 𝛃𝛃E‖β^−β‖2=σ2∑ℓ1/λℓ≥σ2/λp, while, by Proposition 19.6.1(b), 𝛃𝛃E‖Xβ^−Xβ‖2=σ2p whatever the eigenvalues.

  • (e) For each column, ‖(I−M(j))xj‖2≥λp, where M(j) projects onto the span of the other columns. Hence Var(β^j)≤σ2/λp.

Proof.

  • (a) By Theorem 5.5.1, 𝛃β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤Y with covariance σ2(X⊤X)−1=σ2∑ℓλℓ−1vℓvℓ⊤. Hence 𝛃vℓ⊤β^=λℓ−1(Xvℓ)⊤Y=uℓ⊤Y/λℓ, and 𝛃𝛃Cov(vℓ⊤β^,vm⊤β^)=σ2vℓ⊤(X⊤X)−1vm, which is σ2/λℓ if ℓ=m and zero otherwise. The expansion of 𝛃β^ holds because the vℓ form an orthonormal basis.

  • (b) Write cℓ=a⊤vℓ, so that ∑ℓcℓ2=‖a‖2. By Proposition 19.6.1(a), 𝛃Var(a⊤β^)=σ2∑ℓcℓ2/λℓ, which lies between σ2‖a‖2/λ1 and σ2‖a‖2/λp, with the stated equality cases. The singular values of X are λℓ (Theorem 1.8.1), so the ratio of the extremes is λ1/λp=κ(X)2 by Definition 1.8.1.

  • (c) If a∈span{v1,…,vk}, then cℓ=0 for ℓ>k and ∑ℓ≤kcℓ2/λℓ≤‖a‖2/λk. The lower bound keeps only the term ℓ=p.

  • (d) 𝛃𝛃𝛃E‖β^−β‖2=trCov(β^)=σ2tr(X⊤X)−1, and the eigenvalues of (X⊤X)−1 are the 1/λℓ. The statement about fitted values is Proposition 19.6.1(b) multiplied by n.

  • (e) (I−M(j))xj=xj−X(j)c for some c, which is Xb for a vector b with bj=1. By Theorem 1.7.7, ‖Xb‖2≥λp‖b‖2≥λp. The variance bound follows from Proposition 7.2.4.

Part (b) is the precise sense in which κ(X)2 measures collinearity: it is the ratio of the worst to the best precision among linear functions of the same length. Part (e) is the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell picture of Theorem 6.6.2: a coefficient is imprecise when its column’s part orthogonal to the others is short, and no such part is shorter than λp.

Idea. Collinearity does not make least squares imprecise in general. It makes it imprecise in a few directions of coefficient space, those of the eigenvectors with small eigenvalues, and precise in the others. Whether it matters depends on whether the question asked points along a weak direction.

Example (Two regressors that move together)

Draw n=30 values xi1 from a standard normal distribution and set xi2=xi1+0.15zi with independent standard normal zi. The sample correlation is 0.9951. Centre both regressors and fit E(Y)=β0+β1x1+β2x2, so that the intercept is orthogonal to the slopes and the slope block of X⊤X carries all the collinearity. Its eigenvalues are 70.05 and 0.1718. The weak eigenvector is close to (1,−1)⊤/2 (its first coordinate is 0.718 in absolute value), so the weak canonical coordinate is essentially the difference β1−β2. In units of σ, the standard deviations are

Function Standard deviation
β^1 1.734
β^2 1.682
β^1+β^2 0.177
β^1−β^2 3.412

Had x1 been orthogonal to x2 with the same spread, the first entry would have been 0.171, smaller by the factor 10.1=102.4, the root of the variance inflation factor of Section 26.2. The sum of the slopes is estimated about as well as a slope in an orthogonal design, the difference worst of all. The ratio of the largest to the smallest variance over unit vectors is 407.8, the squared condition number of the centred slope columns (Theorem 26.1.1(b)).

Figure 26.1.1 shows what this means for a single data set. With the design fixed and true slopes β1=β2=1, the estimates from 2000 replicate responses lie along the line β1+β2=2. The estimate of β2 ranges from −4.535 to 7.359 and is negative in 26.1 per cent of the replicates, while β^1+β^2 stays between 1.440 and 2.639. Every data set says clearly that the two regressors together matter, yet a quarter of them give β2 the wrong sign, and 54.1 per cent give at least one of the two slopes the wrong sign.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(2601)
n, sigma = 30, 1.0
x1 = rng.normal(size=n)
x2 = x1 + 0.15 * rng.normal(size=n)                  # x2 nearly repeats x1
x1c, x2c = x1 - x1.mean(), x2 - x2.mean()             # centring makes the intercept orthogonal
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1c, x2c])
G = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)                            # Cov(beta_hat) / sigma^2

lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(X[:, 1:].T @ X[:, 1:])        # canonical coordinates of the slopes
print("correlation of x1 and x2:", round(np.corrcoef(x1, x2)[0, 1], 4))
print("eigenvalues:", np.round(lam, 3), " weak direction:", np.round(V[:, 0], 3))

def sd(a):
    """Standard deviation of a' beta_hat, in units of sigma."""
    return np.sqrt(a @ G @ a)

print("sd of b1, b2   :", round(sd(np.array([0, 1, 0])), 3), round(sd(np.array([0, 0, 1])), 3))
print("sd of b1 + b2  :", round(sd(np.array([0, 1, 1])), 3))
print("sd of b1 - b2  :", round(sd(np.array([0, 1, -1])), 3))
beta = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 1.0])                      # equal true slopes
reps = 2000
Y = X @ beta + sigma * rng.normal(size=(reps, n))     # 2000 replicate responses, same design
B = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ Y.T).T             # one row of estimates per replicate
print("share of replicates with b2 < 0:", np.mean(B[:, 2] < 0))
print("share with b1 < 0 or b2 < 0:", np.mean((B[:, 1:] < 0).any(axis=1)))
print("range of b1 + b2:", np.round([B[:, 1:].sum(1).min(), B[:, 1:].sum(1).max()], 3))

26.1.2. The symptoms

The classical symptoms all follow from Theorem 26.1.1: large standard errors for single coefficients alongside small ones for some combinations; coefficients of implausible size or sign; instability under deletion of a few cases; and a significant F test for a group in which no single t test is significant, because the group statistic is governed by the best combination of its members (Proposition 11.6.2). None of them means the model is wrong.

26.1.3. Near-estimability

When the near-dependence is a small perturbation of an exact one, the functions that stay precise are exactly those estimable in the limiting, rank-deficient design.

Proposition 26.1.2 (Near-estimability)

Let X1 be n×(p−1) of full column rank, c∈Rp−1, and z a unit vector orthogonal to C(X1). For δ>0 let Xδ=[X1, X1c+δz], with least squares estimate 𝛃𝛃β^δ=(β^1⊤,β^2)⊤, and let X0=[X1, X1c], which has rank p−1. Write a=(a1⊤,a2)⊤.

  • (a) 𝛃Var(a⊤β^δ)=σ2a1⊤(X1⊤X1)−1a1+σ2(a2−c⊤a1)2/δ2.

  • (b) 𝛃a⊤β is estimable in the model with matrix X0 iff a2=c⊤a1. For such a, 𝛃Var(a⊤β^δ) does not depend on δ and equals the variance of the least squares estimator of 𝛃a⊤β in the limiting model. For every other a it grows like δ−2 as δ→0.

Proof.

  • (a) Apply Theorem 6.6.2 with X2=X1 and the last column in the role of X1 there. The last column residualized on C(X1) is (I−M1)(X1c+δz)=δz, so β^2=z⊤Y/δ. By part (c) of the same theorem and X1⊤z=0, 𝛃𝛃𝛃β^1=(X1⊤X1)−1X1⊤(Y−(X1c+δz)β^2)=β^1S−cβ^2,β^1S=(X1⊤X1)−1X1⊤Y. Hence 𝛃𝛃a⊤β^δ=a1⊤β^1S+(a2−c⊤a1)β^2. The two terms are uncorrelated, because 𝛃Cov(β^1S,β^2)=σ2(X1⊤X1)−1X1⊤z/δ=0, and Var(β^2)=σ2/δ2.

  • (b) By Theorem 8.3.1, 𝛃a⊤β is estimable under X0 iff a∈C(X0⊤). Since X1 has full column rank, C(X1⊤)=Rp−1, so C(X0⊤)={(a1⊤,c⊤a1)⊤:a1∈Rp−1}. For such a the second term in (a) vanishes. In the limiting model 𝛃𝛃X0β=X1(β1+cβ2), so 𝛃𝛃a⊤β=a1⊤(β1+cβ2) is estimated by 𝛃a1⊤β^1S, with variance σ2a1⊤(X1⊤X1)−1a1. If a2≠c⊤a1, the second term is a positive multiple of δ−2.

The orthogonality of z to C(X1) matters only for the size of the bounded term. A general perturbation w splits as M1w+(I−M1)w, and the first part, being X1d for some d, can be absorbed into c. The dichotomy survives: a function estimable in the limit keeps a variance that does not depend on δ, and every other function’s variance grows like δ−2. But the bounded value can exceed the limiting-model variance (Exercise (B2)). Figure 8.3.1 showed the δ−2 growth numerically; the proposition identifies the rate and the exact set of functions that escape it.

26.1.4. Statistical, not numerical

Chapter 10 met ill-conditioning as a numerical problem: a large κ(X) amplifies rounding errors (Theorem 10.5.1). The statistical cost is different. It is σ2/λℓ along each weak direction, so it involves the error variance and the absolute size of the eigenvalues, not only their ratio. Rescaling a column changes κ(X) (Exercise (B2)) but no estimand’s variance, so diagnostics must be computed for a stated scaling (Section 26.3) or be scale-free (Section 26.2). And the eigenvalues grow with n: if the rows are drawn independently from a distribution with nonsingular second-moment matrix Σ, then λp/n→λmin(Σ) by the strong law of large numbers and Proposition 10.4.2, so collinearity in the population is paid for by a larger n. Longley’s regression shows how far apart the two problems can be: QR computes its coefficients to about eleven digits (Example (Longley’s data, exactly)), yet its variance inflation factors reach 1788.5 (Example (Longley’s regression)).

Measurement error in the regressors sits between the two. It perturbs X as rounding does, by an amount that more digits cannot reduce, and a perturbation comparable to λp can turn the weak eigenvector and change which combinations appear well estimated (Theorem 10.5.1, with κ(X) as the amplifier). The directions that collinearity leaves ill-determined are thus the least robust to errors in X; Chapter 24 treats the resulting bias (Theorem 24.1.1).

26.1.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In Example (Two regressors that move together), the difference β1−β2 corresponds to a=(0,1,−1)⊤, of squared length 2. Check that the variance quoted for β^1−β^2 lies between the two bounds of Theorem 26.1.1(b), applied to the slope block, and explain why it is close to the upper one.

Solution. Computed without rounding, the variance is 11.640σ2, and the bounds are 2σ2/70.05=0.0286σ2 and 2σ2/λ2=11.643σ2. (The check needs more digits than the table gives: 3.4122 and 2/0.1718 agree to four figures.) The variance is σ2∑ℓ(a⊤vℓ)2/λℓ, and (a⊤v2)2=1.9995 of the total ‖a‖2=2, because a points almost exactly along the weak eigenvector. Nearly all of its weight sits on the term with the small eigenvalue.

Exercise (A2)

Under normal errors, β^2∼N(β2,Var(β^2)). With β2=1 and the standard deviation of Example (Two regressors that move together), compute Pr(β^2<0) and compare it with the share of replicates in which β^2 was negative.

Exercise (A3)

Let X=[x1,x2] have orthogonal columns with ‖x1‖=1 and ‖x2‖=10. Find the canonical coordinates and the weak direction. Now measure the second regressor in units ten times larger, so that the column becomes x2/10. What happens to the weak direction and to κ(X)? Which variances of estimable quantities have changed?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that maxjVar(β^j)≥σ2/(pλp), so that a small λp always hurts at least one coefficient. Give an example with p=3 in which λp is tiny but only one coefficient has a large variance.

Solution. By Theorem 26.1.1(d), ∑jVar(β^j)=σ2∑ℓ1/λℓ≥σ2/λp, and the largest of p numbers is at least their average. For the example take X with orthonormal columns and multiply the third column by ϵ. Then X⊤X=diag(1,1,ϵ2), λp=ϵ2, and only Var(β^3)=σ2/ϵ2 is large: a short column, not a near-dependence, which is why Section 26.3 scales the columns.

Exercise (B2)

In Proposition 26.1.2, replace z by an arbitrary vector w∉C(X1). Write w=X1d+‖(I−M1)w‖z with z a unit vector orthogonal to C(X1), and show that 𝛃Var(a⊤β^δ)=σ2a1⊤(X1⊤X1)−1a1+σ2(a2−(c+δd)⊤a1)2δ2‖(I−M1)w‖2. Deduce that if a2=c⊤a1 the variance does not depend on δ and equals the limiting-model variance plus σ2(d⊤a1)2/‖(I−M1)w‖2, which vanishes iff d⊤a1=0, and that for every other a it still grows like δ−2.

Solution. X1c+δw=X1(c+δd)+δ‖(I−M1)w‖z, which is the setting of the proposition with c replaced by c+δd and δ by δ‖(I−M1)w‖. Part (a) gives the formula. If a2=c⊤a1, the numerator of the second term is δ2(d⊤a1)2, the δ2 cancels, and the second term is the constant σ2(d⊤a1)2/‖(I−M1)w‖2; the first is the limiting-model variance, by part (b) of the proposition. Otherwise the numerator tends to (a2−c⊤a1)2>0 and the term grows like δ−2.

Exercise (B3)

Section 8.3 used twelve equally spaced ti on [−1,1], X1=[1,t], c=(0,1)⊤ and w=(cos⁡πti), and reported Var(β^1+β^2)=0.212σ2 whatever δ. Use Exercise (B2) to explain the number: show that d=(d1,0)⊤ because w is even and t is odd, and that the variance equals σ2/∑iti2.

Solution. Here a=(0,1,1)⊤, so a1=(0,1)⊤ and a2=c⊤a1=1: the function is estimable in the limit. The coefficients d of the projection of w on C(1,t) are d1=w¯ and d2=t⊤w/t⊤t=0, because the ti are symmetric about zero and cos is even. So d⊤a1=d2=0 and the second term vanishes. The first is σ2[(X1⊤X1)−1]22=σ2/∑iti2, since 1⊥t. With ti=−1+2i/11, i=0,…,11, ∑iti2=(4/121)∑i=011(i−5.5)2=(4/121)⋅143=4.727, and 1/4.727=0.2115, which rounds to 0.212.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

A near-dependence need not show up in any pair of columns. Let X have p≥3 centred columns of unit length with all pairwise correlations equal to ρ, so that X⊤X=R=(1−ρ)I+ρ11⊤.

  • (a) Show that the eigenvalues of R are 1+(p−1)ρ, with eigenvector 1, and 1−ρ with multiplicity p−1; hence R is positive definite exactly when −1/(p−1)<ρ<1.

  • (b) Show that at ρ=−1/(p−1) the columns satisfy X1=0 exactly. With p=10 every pairwise correlation is then −19 in absolute value, a number no inspection of the correlation matrix would remark on, and the design is singular.

  • (c) Put ρ=−1/(p−1)+ϵ with ϵ>0 small. Find λp, find 𝛃Var(1⊤β^), and show that both are governed by ϵ alone.

  • (d) Verify that R−1=11−ρ{I−ρ1+(p−1)ρ11⊤}, so that Var(β^j)/σ2=(R−1)jj, the variance inflation factor of Definition 26.2.1, is (1−ρ)−1{1−ρ/(1+(p−1)ρ)}. Evaluate it at p=10 and ρ=−19+ϵ and show that it grows like 1/(90ϵ).

What does the exercise say about diagnosing collinearity from a table of correlations?

Solution.

  • (a) R1=(1−ρ)1+ρp1={1+(p−1)ρ}1; and if v⊥1 then Rv=(1−ρ)v. These account for all p eigenvalues, and R is positive definite iff both are positive.

  • (b) At ρ=−1/(p−1) the first eigenvalue is 1−1=0, so R1=0, and ‖X1‖2=1⊤X⊤X1=0, whence X1=0: the columns sum to the zero vector. With p=10, ρ=−19=−0.111.

  • (c) The eigenvalue on 1 becomes 1+(p−1){−1/(p−1)+ϵ}=(p−1)ϵ, which for small ϵ is the smallest, so λp=(p−1)ϵ and vp=1/p. By Theorem 26.1.1(a) applied to that eigenvector, 𝛃𝛃Var(1⊤β^)=pVar(vp⊤β^)=pσ2/{(p−1)ϵ}, which is also the upper bound of Theorem 26.1.1(b) at a=1, attained because 1 points exactly along the weak eigenvector.

  • (d) Multiply out: with c=ρ/{1+(p−1)ρ}, {(1−ρ)I+ρ11⊤}(I−c11⊤)=(1−ρ)I+11⊤{ρ−c(1−ρ+ρp)}=(1−ρ)I, since 1−ρ+ρp=1+(p−1)ρ. Hence the stated inverse, and (R−1)jj=(1−ρ)−1(1−c). At p=10 and ρ=−19+ϵ, 1+9ρ=9ϵ and 1−ρ=109−ϵ, so c=ρ/(9ϵ)→−1/(81ϵ) and (R−1)jj≈910{1+1/(81ϵ)}=910+1/(90ϵ).

A table of pairwise correlations answers the question “is any one column nearly a multiple of another?” The question that matters is “is any combination of the columns nearly zero?”, and the two are different as soon as p>2: here ten columns that are nearly mutually orthogonal in pairs are nevertheless nearly linearly dependent as a set. The eigenvalues of Theorem 26.1.1 and the variance inflation factors see it; the correlation matrix, read entry by entry, does not.

Exercise (C2)

Collinearity does not merely spread the same total precision differently: it can put more precision on a chosen direction than an orthogonal design of the same column lengths.

  • (a) Let p=2 with centred columns of length L and correlation ρ∈(−1,1). Show that Var(β^1+β^2)=2σ2L2(1+ρ), which decreases as ρ increases, and is half its orthogonal value as ρ→1.

  • (b) For p equicorrelated columns of length L, show that 𝛃Var(1⊤β^)=pσ2/[L2{1+(p−1)ρ}], so that the sum of the coefficients is estimated p times better than in an orthogonal design as ρ→1, and infinitely worse as ρ→−1/(p−1), the design of Exercise (C1).

  • (c) Reconcile this with Theorem 26.1.1(b) and (d).

Solution.

  • (a) X⊤X=L2(1ρρ1), whose inverse is {L2(1−ρ2)}−1(1−ρ−ρ1). With a=(1,1)⊤, a⊤(X⊤X)−1a=(2−2ρ)/{L2(1−ρ2)}=2/{L2(1+ρ)}. At ρ=0 this is 2/L2 and at ρ→1 it is 1/L2.

  • (b) X⊤X=L2R with R as in Exercise (C1), and R−11=1/{1+(p−1)ρ} because 1 is an eigenvector. So 1⊤(X⊤X)−11=p/[L2{1+(p−1)ρ}]. At ρ=0 this is p/L2; as ρ→1 it is 1/L2, smaller by the factor p; as ρ→−1/(p−1) it diverges.

  • (c) There is no contradiction. Part (b) of the theorem bounds 𝛃Var(a⊤β^) by σ2‖a‖2/λp and below by σ2‖a‖2/λ1, and here a=1 has ‖a‖2=p and points exactly along the strong eigenvector when ρ>0, so the lower bound pσ2/λ1 is attained: collinearity has made λ1=L2{1+(p−1)ρ} as large as it can be, and the sum is the direction that benefits. Part (d) records the price: 𝛃𝛃E‖β^−β‖2=σ2tr(X⊤X)−1 contains the p−1 terms σ2/{L2(1−ρ)}, which diverge as ρ→1. Total precision is not conserved either; what is true is that the design decides how much of it each direction receives, and a design chosen with one estimand in mind can be better than an orthogonal one for that estimand and far worse for every other.

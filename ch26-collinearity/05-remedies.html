26.5  What to do about collinearity

Refitting the same data in another way cannot supply information the design lacks, and every genuine remedy does one of four things: it gets more information of the right kind, changes the question to one the data can answer, adds information from outside the data, or accepts bias in exchange for variance. Before choosing among them, decide whether anything needs to be done at all.

26.5.1. Does it matter?

By Theorem 26.1.1(c) and Proposition 26.4.1, collinearity is harmless for quantities with no component along the weak directions: fitted values, predictions that follow the pattern of the data, and combinations such as the sum in Example (Two regressors that move together). If those are the targets, report them and stop.

26.5.2. Collect better data

The only remedy that keeps the question is more information along the weak directions. The next proposition shows how much a single well-placed observation can supply.

Proposition 26.5.1 (One new observation)

Let A=X⊤X be positive definite with eigenvalues λ1≥⋯≥λp and orthonormal eigenvectors v1,…,vp, p≥2, and let a case with regressor vector x be added, so that A becomes A+xx⊤. Write G=A−1.

  • (a) For every a, the variance of 𝛃a⊤β^ falls by σ2(a⊤Gx)2/(1+x⊤Gx); in particular it never increases.

  • (b) If ‖x‖≤c, then λmin(A+xx⊤)≤min(λp+c2, λp−1), with equality when x=cvp.

  • (c) Among all x with ‖x‖=c, the choice x=cvp minimizes the largest variance of 𝛃a⊤β^ over unit vectors a.

Proof.

  • (a) By Eq. 1.6.7, (A+xx⊤)−1=G−Gxx⊤G/(1+x⊤Gx).

  • (b) By Theorem 1.7.7, λmin(A+xx⊤)≤vp⊤(A+xx⊤)vp=λp+(x⊤vp)2≤λp+c2. For the second bound, the plane span{vp−1,vp} contains a unit vector b orthogonal to x, since the linear map b↦x⊤b from the plane to R has a nontrivial null space. Then λmin(A+xx⊤)≤b⊤Ab≤λp−1. If x=cvp, then A+xx⊤ has the same eigenvectors as A and eigenvalues λ1,…,λp−1,λp+c2, whose minimum is min(λp+c2,λp−1).

  • (c) By Theorem 26.1.1(b), the largest variance over unit vectors is σ2/λmin, and (b) says that x=cvp maximizes λmin.

The new case should lie along the weakest direction, where the existing data are thin. Such a point breaks the pattern of the regressors, and in observational data it may not exist; in an experiment, or when the sample can be chosen, the proposition says what to look for.

Example (The value of one observation)

In the design of Example (Two regressors that move together), add one case at the centred values (x1,x2)=(1.077,−1.044), the point at distance 1.5 from the centroid along the weak slope direction. Each coordinate is well inside the range of its regressor; only the combination is new. The slope estimates are governed by the slope block with the intercept partialled out (Theorem 6.6.2), and the new case, which has an intercept entry, changes that block from S to S+nn+1xx⊤, where x holds its two slope entries: the columns must be centred again about the new mean, and that costs the factor n/(n+1). Applied to this block, Proposition 26.5.1(b) predicts that the smaller eigenvalue rises from 0.172 to 0.172+1.52⋅30/31=2.349, which is what the script finds. The standard deviation of β^1 falls from 1.734σ to 0.476σ. To achieve the same reduction with more data of the old pattern, the whole sample would have to be repeated 13.3 times, about 370 further cases in place of one. The script also checks, on the centred block, that no other direction does better.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(2601)                     # the design of Section 26.1
n = 30
x1 = rng.normal(size=n)
x2 = x1 + 0.15 * rng.normal(size=n)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1 - x1.mean(), x2 - x2.mean()])
G = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(X.T @ X)                      # ascending eigenvalues
v_weak = V[:, 0]                                      # the weak direction (it has no intercept part)
c = 1.5
x_new = np.r_[1.0, c * v_weak[1:] / np.linalg.norm(v_weak[1:])]   # breaks the pattern
print("new point (centred x1, x2):", np.round(x_new[1:], 3))
G_new = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X + np.outer(x_new, x_new))
print("sd of b1 before / after:", round(np.sqrt(G[1, 1]), 3), round(np.sqrt(G_new[1, 1]), 3))
S_old = X[:, 1:].T @ X[:, 1:]                         # slope block: the intercept is orthogonal
xs = x_new[1:]
S_new = S_old + n / (n + 1) * np.outer(xs, xs)        # centred slope block after recentring
print("smallest slope eigenvalue before / after:", round(np.linalg.eigvalsh(S_old)[0], 3),
      round(np.linalg.eigvalsh(S_new)[0], 3))
# repeating the old pattern k times multiplies X'X by k: Var(b1) falls by the factor 1/k
k_equiv = G[1, 1] / G_new[1, 1]
print("equivalent number of copies of the whole data set:", round(k_equiv, 1))

26.5.3. Change the question

A reparameterization 𝛃𝛃Xβ=(XT)(T−1β) leaves the least squares estimate of every linear function of 𝛃β unchanged (Theorem 6.7.1), so it cannot make β1−β2 better determined. It can make the reported parameters the well-determined ones. Replacing x1 and x2 by their average and their difference gives a coefficient for the average that estimates β1+β2 precisely, and leaves all the imprecision in the coefficient of the difference. Centring polynomial terms (Example (A quadratic far from the origin)) is the same move.

Two common changes of model are really linear restrictions on 𝛃β, and should be judged as such. Dropping a regressor imposes β2=0. By Theorem 19.1.2 the short slope estimates β1+πβ2, where π is the slope of x2 on x1; in Example (Two regressors that move together) π=1.0261, and the short slope has standard deviation 0.171σ against 1.734σ. It is precise because it answers a different question, giving x1 the credit for both regressors: with true slopes (1,1) it centres at about 2.03. Theorem 19.1.5 gives the condition under which it has the smaller mean squared error. Combining regressors into an index imposes a restriction such as β1=β2. It concerns the weak direction β1−β2, whose estimate has standard deviation 3.412σ in the example, so an F test of it has almost no power: the restriction is cheap in variance and nearly untestable, for the same reason.

26.5.4. Add information from outside the data

An exact restriction known from theory is fitted by restricted least squares (Proposition 11.3.3). An approximate one can be written as extra observations 𝛃r=Lβ+u and combined with the data by generalized least squares, the mixed estimation of Theil and Goldberger (1961) (Exercise (B1)). The Bayesian version is a prior: with the conjugate prior of Theorem 7.6.2, under which 𝛃β given σ2 has covariance σ2V0, the posterior mean solves equations with matrix V0−1+X⊤X. A prior informative along the weak eigenvector raises the smallest eigenvalue as a new observation does, and the answer along that direction then comes mostly from the prior.

26.5.5. Accept bias

The last option gives up unbiasedness. Principal component regression drops the canonical coordinates with small eigenvalues, the truncated SVD estimator of Proposition 10.4.3; ridge regression shrinks every canonical coordinate towards zero, the weak ones most. Chapter 27 develops both (Theorem 27.2.2 and Definition 27.3.1). Neither creates information: they trade variance along the weak directions for bias along the same directions.

26.5.6. What not to do

  • Do not drop regressors because their variance inflation factors exceed a threshold; the fit may hardly change while the meaning of every remaining coefficient does.
  • Do not expect centring or rescaling to cure collinearity that is not created by the parameterization.
  • Do not let an automatic search choose among collinear regressors: noise decides which enters (Chapter 29).

26.5.7. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Explain, using Theorem 26.1.1, why no reparameterization of Example (Two regressors that move together) can estimate β1−β2 with a standard deviation smaller than 3.412σ, although the coefficient of the new difference regressor may have any standard deviation you like.

Exercise (A2)

In Example (Two regressors that move together), what does the short regression of y on x1 alone estimate if the true slopes are (2,0)? If they are (0,2)? What does this say about using the short regression to decide which regressor matters?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Suppose that, besides 𝛃𝛆Y=Xβ+ε with 𝛆Cov(ε)=σ2I, there is independent information 𝛃r=Lβ+u with E(u)=0 and Cov(u)=σ2Ω, Ω known and positive definite. Show that the generalized least squares estimator from the stacked data is 𝛃β~=(X⊤X+L⊤Ω−1L)−1(X⊤Y+L⊤Ω−1r), that it is unbiased with covariance σ2(X⊤X+L⊤Ω−1L)−1, and that it equals the posterior mean of Theorem 7.6.2 for a suitable prior. With L=vp⊤, how does the smallest eigenvalue change?

Solution. Stack 𝛃𝛆(Y⊤,r⊤)⊤=(X⊤,L⊤)⊤β+(ε⊤,u⊤)⊤, with error covariance σ2diag(I,Ω). Generalized least squares (Corollary 7.2.3) gives the stated estimator, and it is unbiased with covariance σ2 times the inverse of the weighted cross-product matrix. If L is square and nonsingular, the prior 𝛃β∼N(L−1r,σ2L−1Ω(L−1)⊤) has precision L⊤Ω−1L/σ2 and mean L−1r, and the posterior mean of Theorem 7.6.2 is the same expression. With L=vp⊤ and Ω=ω2, the matrix becomes X⊤X+vpvp⊤/ω2, whose smallest eigenvalue is min(λp+1/ω2,λp−1): exactly the effect of a new observation of length 1/ω along vp (Proposition 26.5.1(b)).

Exercise (B2)

Let x1,x2 be centred with ‖x1‖2=S1, ‖x2‖2=S2 and x1⊤x2=S12, and fit the intercept and the single composite s=x1+x2. Show that its coefficient has expectation β1+(β2−β1)(S2+S12)/(S1+S2+2S12). When is this (β1+β2)/2?

Solution. The coefficient is s⊤Y/s⊤s, because s is centred and so orthogonal to 1. Its expectation is s⊤(x1β1+x2β2)/s⊤s, with s⊤x1=S1+S12, s⊤x2=S2+S12 and s⊤s=S1+S2+2S12. Writing β1(S1+S12)=β1(s⊤s)−β1(S2+S12) gives the formula. It is (β1+β2)/2 for all 𝛃β iff S2+S12=(S1+S2+2S12)/2, that is, iff S1=S2: the composite estimates the average slope when the two regressors have equal spread, whatever their correlation.

Exercise (B3)

For p=2 and A=diag(λ1,λ2) with λ1>λ2, let x=c(cos⁡θ,sin⁡θ)⊤. Compute λmin(A+xx⊤) and show directly that it is maximized at θ=π/2.

Solution. The trace is T=λ1+λ2+c2, independent of θ, and the determinant is D=(λ1+c2cos2⁡θ)(λ2+c2sin2⁡θ)−c4cos2⁡θsin2⁡θ=λ1λ2+c2(λ1sin2⁡θ+λ2cos2⁡θ). The smaller eigenvalue is (T−T2−4D)/2, which increases with D for fixed T. Since λ1>λ2, D is largest when sin2⁡θ=1.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Proposition 26.5.1(c) says where to put one new case. Repeating that advice for two cases is not optimal. Let p=2, A=diag(λ1,λ2) with λ1>λ2>0, and add two rows x and x~, each of length c, so that A becomes A+xx⊤+x~x~⊤.

  • (a) Show that both cases along the weak direction give λmin=min(λ1, λ2+2c2), and that one along each eigenvector gives λ2+c2.

  • (b) Show that for every such pair, λmin≤min{λ2+2c2, 12(λ1+λ2)+c2}, using v2⊤(⋅)v2 for the first bound and the trace for the second.

  • (c) Show that the bound is attained. When 2c2≤λ1−λ2 it is attained by (a)’s first choice. When 2c2>λ1−λ2 it is attained by x=(a1/2, a2/2)⊤,x~=(a1/2, −a2/2)⊤,a1,2=c2∓12(λ1−λ2), neither of which lies along an eigenvector.

  • (d) Take λ1=2, λ2=1 and c=3/2. Compute λmin for the three designs of (a) and (c), and compare.

Solution.

  • (a) If x=x~=cv2, the sum A+2c2v2v2⊤ is diagonal with entries λ1 and λ2+2c2. If x=cv1 and x~=cv2, it is diagonal with entries λ1+c2 and λ2+c2, whose minimum is λ2+c2 because λ1>λ2.

  • (b) Write M=xx⊤+x~x~⊤, which is nonnegative definite with trM=2c2. By Theorem 1.7.7, λmin(A+M)≤v2⊤(A+M)v2=λ2+v2⊤Mv2≤λ2+trM=λ2+2c2, since the eigenvalues of M are nonnegative and sum to trM. And the smaller of two eigenvalues is at most their average, so λmin(A+M)≤12tr(A+M)=12(λ1+λ2)+c2.

  • (c) For the first case the design of (a) gives exactly λ2+2c2, which is then the smaller of the two bounds and is attained. For the second, a1 and a2 are nonnegative because 2c2>λ1−λ2, they sum to 2c2, and xx⊤+x~x~⊤=diag(a1,a2), the off-diagonal terms cancelling. Also ‖x‖2=‖x~‖2=(a1+a2)/2=c2. Then A+M=diag(λ1+a1, λ2+a2), and both entries equal 12(λ1+λ2)+c2, so λmin attains the second bound.

  • (d) Here c2=9/4 and λ1−λ2=1<2c2. Both cases along v2 give min(2, 1+92)=2, no better than doing nothing to the strong direction. One along each eigenvector gives 1+94=134=3.25. The optimal pair, with a1=74 and a2=114, gives 32+94=154=3.75.

The greedy rule fails because after the first case the weakest direction is no longer v2, and a plan made for two cases should anticipate that. Choosing several cases at once is a design problem — here the criterion λmin, called E-optimality — and one-at-a-time optimization solves it only when the budget is too small to level the two eigenvalues.

Exercise (C2)

Outside information helps only if it is good enough. In the setting of Exercise (B1) take L=vp⊤ and Ω=ω2, and suppose the source is wrong: E(r)=γp+δ with 𝛃γp=vp⊤β and δ≠0, while Var(r)=σ2ω2 as claimed, and r is independent of Y. Write κ=1/ω2.

  • (a) Show that 𝛃vℓ⊤β~=γ^ℓ for ℓ≠p, while γ~p=(λpγ^p+κr)/(λp+κ). Only the weak coordinate moves.

  • (b) Show that γ~p has bias κδ/(λp+κ) and variance σ2/(λp+κ), so that E(γ~p−γp)2=σ2λp+κ+κ2δ2(λp+κ)2.

  • (c) Deduce that the outside information lowers the mean squared error if and only if δ2<σ2ω2+σ2λp=Var(r)+Var(γ^p): its error must be smaller than the two standard errors combined in quadrature.

  • (d) Interpret: what happens when the source is honest, in the sense that δ is itself drawn with mean zero and variance σ2ω2? What happens when it is confident and wrong, that is when ω2 is small and δ is not?

Solution.

  • (a) By Exercise (B1), 𝛃β~=(X⊤X+κvpvp⊤)−1(X⊤Y+κvpr). The matrix has the eigenvectors of X⊤X, with eigenvalues λℓ for ℓ≠p and λp+κ, so its inverse is ∑ℓ≠pλℓ−1vℓvℓ⊤+(λp+κ)−1vpvp⊤. Since vℓ⊤X⊤Y=λℓγ^ℓ (Theorem 26.1.1(a)) and vℓ⊤vp=0 for ℓ≠p, the stated coordinates follow.

  • (b) Eγ~p=(λpγp+κ(γp+δ))/(λp+κ), whose difference from γp is κδ/(λp+κ). Since r and γ^p are independent with variances σ2/κ and σ2/λp (Theorem 26.1.1(a)), Var(γ~p)=λp2σ2/λp+κ2σ2/κ(λp+κ)2=σ2λp+κ. Add the squared bias.

  • (c) The inequality E(γ~p−γp)2<σ2/λp reads κ2δ2(λp+κ)2<σ2(1λp−1λp+κ)=σ2κλp(λp+κ). Multiply by (λp+κ)2/κ and divide by κ: δ2<σ2(λp+κ)/(κλp)=σ2/κ+σ2/λp, which is the stated bound because σ2/κ=σ2ω2=Var(r).

  • (d) If the source is honest then Eδ2=σ2ω2, which is strictly less than the bound, so on average the outside information helps, and it helps most where the data are weakest, λp small. If the source is confident and wrong — ω2 small, so that κ is large and the answer along vp comes almost entirely from outside — the bound σ2ω2+σ2/λp is itself small, and a modest δ makes the mean squared error worse than plain least squares, which at least was unbiased. A stated prior variance is a claim about δ, and the inequality says the claim has to be roughly right, not merely convenient.

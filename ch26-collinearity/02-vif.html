26.2  Variance inflation factors

Analysts usually want something coarser than the eigenvalues: one number per coefficient saying how much its precision has suffered. Chapter 6 supplied that number as a by-product of the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem. This section states what it measures and what it does not, and extends it to a group of columns such as the indicators of a factor.

26.2.1. Definition and meaning

Throughout, the model contains an intercept, xj is one of the other columns, and X(j) is X with xj removed, with projection M(j). Write Sjj=‖xj−x¯j1‖2 for the centred sum of squares of xj.

Definition 26.2.1 (Variance inflation factor)

Let Rj2 be the coefficient of determination of the least squares regression of xj on the columns of X(j), which include 1, and suppose Rj2<1. The variance inflation factor of xj is VIFj=11−Rj2, and TOLj=1−Rj2=1/VIFj is its tolerance.

The identity Eq. 6.6.2, proved with the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem, reads Var(β^j)=σ2VIFj/Sjj. The next proposition collects what follows from it.

Proposition 26.2.1 (What a variance inflation factor measures)

  • (a) ‖(I−M(j))xj‖2=Sjj/VIFj, and VIFj is the ratio of Var(β^j) to σ2/Sjj, the variance β^j would have if the centred xj were orthogonal to every other centred column. By Exercise (A2), VIFj≥1.

  • (b) VIFj depends on X only through xj and C(X(j)). It is unchanged when xj is replaced by axj+b1 with a≠0, and when X(j) is replaced by any matrix with the same column space.

  • (c) If R is the correlation matrix of the non-intercept columns, then VIFj=[R−1]jj.

  • (d) The t interval for βj at any level is VIFj times as long, on average, as in the orthogonal design of (a), and the noncentrality of the F test of βj=0 (the square of the t statistic’s) is divided by VIFj: βj2Var(β^j)=βj2Sjjσ2⋅1VIFj.

Proof.

  • (a) Since 1∈C(X(j)), the vector (I−M(j))xj is the residual of the regression of xj on X(j), and its squared length is Sjj(1−Rj2) by the definition of R2 (Exercise (B1)). The variance ratio is Eq. 6.6.2 divided by σ2/Sjj, which is the variance in the orthogonal case by Proposition 7.2.4 applied to the model with centred columns.

  • (b) By (a), 1/VIFj=‖(I−M(j))xj‖2/Sjj, and M(j) depends only on C(X(j)). Replacing xj by axj+b1 multiplies the numerator by a2, because (I−M(j))1=0, and multiplies Sjj by a2.

  • (c) This is Exercise (A1).

  • (d) The t interval has half-length tα/2(n−p)sVIFj/Sjj, and s has the same distribution in both designs. The noncentrality is βj2/Var(β^j) with the variance from (a) (Theorem 11.6.1).

By (b) the factor is scale-free: a property of the angle between the centred xj and the space of the other centred columns, whose squared cosine is Rj2 (Theorem 6.7.2). By (a), with the spread per observation fixed, β^j is as precise as in an orthogonal design with n/VIFj observations.

Idea. A variance inflation factor of 10 costs β^j as much precision as discarding nine observations in ten from an orthogonal design with the same spread.

26.2.2. Reading variance inflation factors

Rules of thumb flag a regressor when VIFj exceeds 10, or 5, or 4. The thresholds are arbitrary, and they measure the wrong thing. The factor compares the design with a hypothetical orthogonal design of the same size; it says nothing about whether the actual standard error is small enough for the purpose, which depends also on σ2 and on Sjj, hence on n. As n grows with the regressors drawn from a fixed population, VIFj settles to a population value while Sjj grows like n, so a factor that is ruinous at n=20 can be irrelevant at n=20,000. O’Brien (2007) makes this case in detail. A large factor is a reason to look at the interval, never a verdict by itself.

Example (Large factors, precise estimate)

The quarterly US macroeconomic series of Example (A macroeconomic design) run from 1959 to 2009, n=203. Regress real personal consumption on real disposable income, population, the consumer price index, the money stock M1, the three-month Treasury bill rate and the unemployment rate, with an intercept. The variance inflation factors are

Regressor income population CPI M1 T-bill unemployment
VIFj 154.5 183.7 140.4 97.0 2.9 1.3

By every rule of thumb, four regressors are badly collinear. Yet the coefficient of income is 1.0659 with standard error 0.0210, a t statistic of 50.9: the standard error is 154.5≈12.4 times what an orthogonal design would give, and still small, because income varies widely and consumption tracks it closely. (The errors are autocorrelated, so all the standard errors are too small, Chapter 21; the factors depend on X alone.)

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

def vifs(Z):
    """VIFs of the columns of Z (an intercept is always included): diagonal of R^{-1}."""
    return np.diag(np.linalg.inv(np.corrcoef(Z, rowvar=False)))

macro = sm.datasets.macrodata.load_pandas().data
names = ["realdpi", "pop", "cpi", "m1", "tbilrate", "unemp"]
Z = macro[names].to_numpy()
fit = sm.OLS(macro["realcons"].to_numpy(), sm.add_constant(Z)).fit()
for name, v, b, se in zip(names, vifs(Z), fit.params[1:], fit.bse[1:]):
    print(f"{name:9s} VIF {v:7.1f}   estimate {b:9.4f}   se {se:7.4f}   t {b / se:6.1f}")

26.2.3. Collinearity that a reparameterization removes

Some large factors are produced by the parameterization, not by the data. Polynomial and interaction terms built from regressors whose origin lies far from the data are the standard case. Such collinearity is sometimes called nonessential.

Example (A quadratic far from the origin)

Take n=21 equally spaced values xi=10,10.5,…,20 and the quadratic model E(Y)=β0+β1x+β2x2. Over this range x and x2 are almost perfectly correlated: R2=0.9920, and both have VIF=124.6. Rewrite the model in the centred regressors x−15 and (x−15)2. The fitted values are identical, and both variance inflation factors are now exactly 1, because the design is symmetric about 15 and an odd function of x−15 is orthogonal to an even one once both are centred.

What changed is the question. In the raw coding β1 is the slope of the mean function at x=0, ten units below the data, with standard deviation 0.804σ. In the centred coding the coefficient of x−15 is the slope at x=15, with standard deviation 0.0721σ, and the raw coding estimates the same slope, β1+30β2, with exactly the same standard deviation. The large factors described the difficulty of a question, the slope at the origin, that the analyst probably did not mean to ask (Exercise (B2) derives 124.6 by hand).

x = np.linspace(10, 20, 21)                           # a regressor far from its origin

raw = np.column_stack([x, x**2])
cen = np.column_stack([x - x.mean(), (x - x.mean()) ** 2])
print("VIFs, raw     :", np.round(vifs(raw), 1))
print("VIFs, centred :", np.round(vifs(cen), 3))

X_raw = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), raw])
X_cen = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), cen])
G_raw, G_cen = np.linalg.inv(X_raw.T @ X_raw), np.linalg.inv(X_cen.T @ X_cen)
a_mean = np.array([0, 1, 2 * x.mean()])               # slope at x = 15 in the raw coefficients
print("sd of the slope at 0 (raw b1)       :", round(np.sqrt(G_raw[1, 1]), 3))
print("sd of the slope at 15, raw coding   :", round(np.sqrt(a_mean @ G_raw @ a_mean), 4))
print("sd of the slope at 15, centred (b1) :", round(np.sqrt(G_cen[1, 1]), 4))

26.2.4. Groups of columns

A factor enters the model through several indicator columns, a polynomial through several powers. The individual coefficients of such a block depend on the coding (Definition 8.5.1), and so do their variance inflation factors. What is intrinsic is the joint precision of the block, which the next definition, due to Fox and Monette (1992), measures.

For the rest of the section, split the non-intercept columns of X into Z1 (n×q) and Z2 (n×(k−q)), let Z~1 and Z~2 be the centred versions, and let M~2 project onto C(Z~2). Assume X=[1,Z1,Z2] has full column rank.

Definition 26.2.2 (Generalized variance inflation factor)

The generalized variance inflation factor of the block Z1 is GVIF1=det(Z~1⊤Z~1)det(Z~1⊤(I−M~2)Z~1).

The canonical correlations between the two blocks are defined through orthonormal bases Q1 of C(Z~1) and Q2 of C(Z~2): they are the singular values ρ1≥ρ2≥⋯≥ρm, m=min(q,k−q), of the q×(k−q) matrix Q1⊤Q2. The largest, ρ1, is the largest correlation between a linear combination of the centred columns of Z1 and a linear combination of the centred columns of Z2 (Exercise (C1)).

Proposition 26.2.2 (Properties of the generalized factor)

Let 𝛃β1 be the coefficients of Z1.

  • (a) 𝛃detCov(β^1)=GVIF1⋅detCov⊥, where Cov⊥=σ2(Z~1⊤Z~1)−1 is the covariance 𝛃β^1 would have if C(Z~1) were orthogonal to C(Z~2). So GVIF1 is the squared ratio of the volumes of the joint confidence ellipsoids for 𝛃β1 in the two designs.

  • (b) GVIF1=∏i=1m1/(1−ρi2).

  • (c) GVIF1 is unchanged when Z1 is replaced by Z1A+1f⊤ and Z2 by Z2B+1g⊤, for nonsingular A and B and any vectors f and g. In particular it does not depend on the coding of a factor. It is symmetric: GVIF1=GVIF2.

  • (d) If q=1, GVIF1 is the variance inflation factor of Definition 26.2.1. In general, with R the correlation matrix of [Z1,Z2] partitioned conformably, GVIF1=detR11detR22/detR.

Proof.

  • (a) Since C(1,Z2)=C(1)⊕C(Z~2) with orthogonal summands, the projection onto C(1,Z2) is n−111⊤+M~2 (Theorem 6.3.7), and the residual of Z1 after it is (I−M~2)Z~1. By Theorem 6.6.2 and Eq. 6.6.1, 𝛃Cov(β^1)=σ2(Z~1⊤(I−M~2)Z~1)−1. If C(Z~1)⊥C(Z~2), then M~2Z~1=0 and the covariance is Cov⊥. Take the ratio of determinants. By Theorem 12.2.1(b), the volume of the confidence ellipsoid is proportional to (detΣ)1/2, where Σ is the covariance matrix, with the same constant in both designs.

  • (b) Centring annihilates 1f⊤ and commutes with multiplication on the right, so replacing Z1 by Z1A+1f⊤ replaces Z~1 by Z~1A, and both determinants in Definition 26.2.2 are multiplied by det(A)2. For the same reason Z2↦Z2B+1g⊤ replaces Z~2 by Z~2B, which leaves C(Z~2), and so M~2, unchanged. We may therefore take Z~1=Q1. Then M~2=Q2Q2⊤, and with C=Q1⊤Q2, GVIF1=det(Q1⊤Q1)det(Q1⊤Q1−CC⊤)=1det(Iq−CC⊤). The eigenvalues of CC⊤ are ρ12,…,ρm2 and q−m zeros, so the determinant is ∏i(1−ρi2).

  • (c) The invariance was shown in the proof of (b). Symmetry follows from (b), because Q1⊤Q2 and Q2⊤Q1 have the same singular values.

  • (d) For q=1 the ratio is Sjj/‖(I−M(j))xj‖2=VIFj by Proposition 26.2.1(a). For the determinant form, let D be the diagonal matrix with R=D[Z~1,Z~2]⊤[Z~1,Z~2]D, with diagonal blocks D1,D2. By Theorem 1.6.3, detR=detR22det(R11−R12R22−1R21), and the Schur complement is D1Z~1⊤(I−M~2)Z~1D1. Since R11=D1Z~1⊤Z~1D1, the factors det(D1)2 cancel in detR11detR22/detR, which leaves GVIF1.

A generalized factor inflates a q-dimensional volume, so for comparison with single columns Fox and Monette suggest GVIF11/(2q), the geometric mean of the factors by which the axes of the confidence ellipsoid lengthen; for q=1 it is VIFj.

Example (A factor that explains a regressor)

Grunfeld’s panel (Example (Grunfeld’s investment data)) has 220 firm-years of gross investment, market value and capital stock for 11 firms. Regress investment on value, capital and the firm, entered as 10 indicator columns with the first firm as reference. The generalized factors are

Term columns q GVIF GVIF1/(2q)
value 1 18.32 4.280
capital 1 2.04
firm 10 23.61 1.171

The canonical correlations between the firm block and the pair (value, capital) are 0.9694 and 0.5459, and 1/((1−0.96942)(1−0.54592))≈23.6 as Proposition 26.2.2(b) requires. Market value is largely a characteristic of the firm. With the firm indicators in the model, the coefficient of value is identified by movements within firms (the within estimator of Section 6.6), and the part of value that neither firm nor capital explains is 1/18.32, about 5.5 per cent, of its variation.

The ordinary factors of the ten indicator columns range from 1.8 to 16.8; with the last firm as reference the largest is 12.7. The generalized factors are the same under every coding, including sum-to-zero coding (Proposition 26.2.2(c)).

import pandas as pd
grun = sm.datasets.grunfeld.load_pandas().data
D = pd.get_dummies(grun["firm"], drop_first=True).to_numpy(dtype=float)   # 10 indicator columns
Zg = np.column_stack([grun["value"], grun["capital"], D])

def gvif(Z, block):
    """det(R_11) det(R_22) / det(R) for the columns in block against the rest."""
    R = np.corrcoef(Z, rowvar=False)
    rest = [k for k in range(Z.shape[1]) if k not in block]
    return (np.linalg.det(R[np.ix_(block, block)]) * np.linalg.det(R[np.ix_(rest, rest)])
            / np.linalg.det(R))

firm = list(range(2, Zg.shape[1]))
print("GVIF value  :", round(gvif(Zg, [0]), 2))
print("GVIF capital:", round(gvif(Zg, [1]), 2))
print("GVIF firm   :", round(gvif(Zg, firm), 2), " per dimension:", round(gvif(Zg, firm) ** (1 / 20), 3))
print("VIFs of the firm indicators:", np.round(vifs(Zg)[2:], 1))

26.2.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Which values of Rj2 correspond to variance inflation factors of 4, 5 and 10? By what factor does each multiply the length of a confidence interval for βj, relative to the orthogonal design of Proposition 26.2.1(a)?

Exercise (A2)

For a model with an intercept and two regressors with sample correlation r, show that VIF1=VIF2=1/(1−r2). What does Proposition 26.2.1(a) say about the design of Example (Two regressors that move together)?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that appending a column w∉C(X) to X cannot decrease the variance inflation factor of any column already in the model, and that it leaves VIFj unchanged iff the residual of w on X(j) is orthogonal to the residual of xj on X(j). Is Var(β^j) also nondecreasing?

Solution. Appending a column enlarges C(X(j)), so ‖(I−M(j))xj‖2 cannot increase, and by Proposition 26.2.1(a) VIFj cannot decrease. Let w~≠0 be the residual of w on X(j). The enlarged space is C(X(j))⊕C(w~) with orthogonal summands, so the squared residual of xj drops by (w~⊤xj)2/‖w~‖2, and w~⊤xj=w~⊤(I−M(j))xj. So VIFj is unchanged iff the two residuals are orthogonal. Since Sjj does not change, Var(β^j)=σ2VIFj/Sjj is nondecreasing too. This is the variance half of Theorem 19.1.3.

Exercise (B2)

Let xi=m+ui, where the ui are symmetric about zero. Write v for the mean of the ui2 and w for the variance of the ui2 (both with divisor n). Show that the variance inflation factor of x and of x2 in the quadratic model is VIF=1+4m2vw. Check the value in Example (A quadratic far from the origin).

Solution. With one other regressor, VIF=1/(1−r2), where r is the correlation of x and x2 (Exercise (A2)). Since x2=m2+2mu+u2, the covariance of x and x2 is that of u and 2mu+u2, which is 2mv because the third moment of u vanishes. The variance of x2 is 4m2v+w, since u and u2 are uncorrelated. So r2=4m2v2/(v(4m2v+w))=4m2v/(4m2v+w) and 1/(1−r2)=1+4m2v/w. In the example, m=15 and u runs over −5,−4.5,…,5: v=192.5/21≈9.167, the mean of u4 is 3166.6/21≈150.79, so w≈150.79−84.03=66.76 and VIF≈1+900⋅9.167/66.76≈124.6.

Exercise (B3)

Let xj be a column of the block Z1. Show that GVIF1≥1/(1−Rj∣22), where Rj∣22 is the coefficient of determination of xj regressed on 1 and Z2 alone. Why is there no such inequality between GVIF1 and the ordinary VIFj computed in the full model?

Solution. Rj∣2 is the correlation between the centred xj and its projection on C(Z~2), which is a correlation between a vector Q1s of C(Z~1) and a vector Q2t of C(Z~2), with s and t unit vectors. That correlation is s⊤Q1⊤Q2t≤ρ1 (Proposition 1.8.2; this is the first part of Exercise (C1)). Hence GVIF1≥1/(1−ρ12)≥1/(1−Rj∣22) by Proposition 26.2.2(b). The ordinary VIFj regresses xj also on the other columns of its own block, which can explain it well even when Z2 cannot. The indicators of a factor with a small reference level are an example: each is nearly determined by the others.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that ρ1=max{cos⁡∠(u,v):u∈C(Z~1), v∈C(Z~2), u,v≠0}. More generally, show that the generalized eigenvalues of 𝛃Cov(β^1) relative to Cov⊥, the roots θ of 𝛃det(Cov(β^1)−θCov⊥)=0, are 1/(1−ρi2), i≤m, together with q−m ones. Deduce that for every a≠0 the ratio 𝛃Var(a⊤β^1)/a⊤Cov⊥a lies between 1 and 1/(1−ρ12).

Solution. Write u=Q1s and v=Q2t with unit s,t. Then cos⁡∠(u,v)=s⊤Q1⊤Q2t, whose maximum over unit vectors is the largest singular value of Q1⊤Q2 (Proposition 1.8.2). For the second part, take Z~1=Q1 by Proposition 26.2.2(c), which changes both matrices by the same congruence and so leaves the generalized eigenvalues unchanged. Then Cov⊥=σ2I and 𝛃Cov(β^1)=σ2(I−CC⊤)−1, whose eigenvalues are the stated ones. The ratio in the last part is a ratio of quadratic forms, bounded by Corollary 1.7.8, so it lies between the smallest and the largest generalized eigenvalue.

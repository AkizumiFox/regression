26.6  Summary and Notes

26.6.1. Summary

Idea.

  1. Collinearity breaks no assumption. It inflates the variance of 𝛃a⊤β^ when a points along an eigenvector of X⊤X with a small eigenvalue; the worst-to-best ratio is κ(X)2, and fitted values keep total variance σ2p (Theorem 26.1.1).

  2. Near a rank-deficient design, the functions with bounded variance are those estimable in the limit (Proposition 26.1.2).

  3. The variance inflation factor is the variance ratio to an orthogonal design of the same spread: scale-free, but a ratio, not a verdict (Definition 26.2.1, Proposition 26.2.1). Large factors that vanish under centring belong to questions about the origin (Example (A quadratic far from the origin)).

  4. For a block of columns, the generalized factor ∏i1/(1−ρi2) measures the inflation of the joint confidence ellipsoid and does not depend on coding (Definition 26.2.2, Proposition 26.2.2).

  5. Condition indices of the unit-length scaled design and the variance-decomposition proportions locate near-dependencies (Definition 26.3.1, Theorem 26.3.2). Centring can only hide them, namely those involving the intercept (Proposition 26.3.1), and only high-leverage cases can mask them (Proposition 26.3.3).

  6. Predictions inside the convex hull, or along the pattern of the regressors, are unharmed (Proposition 26.4.1).

  7. Remedies: data along the weak direction (Proposition 26.5.1), a question the data can answer, outside information, or biased estimation (Chapter 27).

26.6.2. Notes and sources

The problem. Frisch introduced the word “multicollinearity” in the 1930s. The statistical treatment rests on the observation that the eigenvectors of X⊤X sort linear functions of 𝛃β into well and badly estimated ones; Silvey (1969) set this out, including the advice to take new observations along the weak eigenvectors behind Proposition 26.5.1. Goldberger (1991) insisted that collinearity is a shortage of data, not a disease of the model. Stewart (1987) gave a numerical analyst’s account.

Diagnostics. The name “variance inflation factor” is usually traced to Marquardt (1970). The generalized factor and its 1/(2q) power are due to Fox and Monette (1992); the canonical-correlation form of Proposition 26.2.2(b) is implicit there. O’Brien (2007) examined the rules of thumb. Condition indices and variance-decomposition proportions, with the working rule of indices above about 30 and two or more proportions above one half, are due to Belsley, Kuh and Welsch (1980, chapter 3); Belsley (1991) is a book-length development, and Belsley (1984), with its discussion, argues for scaling without centring. The bounds Theorem 26.3.2(c) and Proposition 26.3.3 are stated in the form this book needs and follow from standard eigenvalue inequalities. Among the textbooks used for coverage, Seber and Lee (2003, sections 9.7 and 10.7) treat variance inflation factors, eigenvalues, centring and influential cases; Christensen (2020, sections 13.1–13.2) develops collinearity through ill-defined directions and tolerances; and Sen and Srivastava (1990, chapter 10) give detailed variance-decomposition examples.

Data and remedies. The US macroeconomic series and Grunfeld’s panel are public-domain data distributed with statsmodels; Longley (1967) built his data to test regression programs. Mixed estimation is due to Theil and Goldberger (1961). Shrinkage is the subject of Chapter 27, and selection among collinear regressors of Chapter 29.

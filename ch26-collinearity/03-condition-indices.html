26.3  Condition indices and variance decomposition

A variance inflation factor says how much one coefficient has suffered, but not which other regressors are responsible, how many near-dependencies there are, or whether the intercept is involved. The eigenvalue analysis of Section 26.1 answers those questions but depends on the units of the columns. This section fixes a scaling and derives the variance-decomposition proportions of Belsley, Kuh and Welsch (1980).

26.3.1. Scaling the columns

The canonical directions of X⊤X change when a column is rescaled (Exercise (A3)), and a merely short column produces a small eigenvalue without any near-dependence (Exercise (B1)). The diagnostics are therefore computed for the design with every column, including 1, scaled to unit length: X~=XD0,D0=diag(1/‖x1‖,…,1/‖xp‖). By Proposition 10.5.2 this is within a factor p of the best diagonal scaling for the condition number. The coefficients of X~ are β~j=‖xj‖βj.

Definition 26.3.1 (Condition indices)

Let X~ have singular values μ1≥μ2≥⋯≥μp>0. The condition indices of X are ηk=μ1μk,k=1,…,p. The largest, ηp=κ(X~), is the scaled condition number.

Whether to centre before scaling is a real choice. Let R be the correlation matrix of the non-intercept columns, the cross-product matrix of the centred and scaled design, and write sj2=Sjj/n and CVj=sj/|x¯j| for the coefficient of variation of a column with nonzero mean.

Proposition 26.3.1 (Scaled condition numbers)

Let X contain the column 1 and have full column rank, and let R be nonsingular.

  • (a) ∑kμk2=p, so 1≤μ12≤p. Every diagonal entry of (X~⊤X~)−1 is at most 1/μp2≤ηp2.

  • (b) λmin(R)≥μp2. Hence ηp2≥1/λmin(R)≥maxjVIFj: every near-dependency among the centred regressors is at least as severe in the uncentred scaled design.

  • (c) If a non-intercept column xj has x¯j≠0, then μp2≤CVj2/(CVj2+2), so that ηp≥(CVj2+2)/CVj2>2/CVj.

Proof.

  • (a) ∑kμk2=‖X~‖F2=p (Proposition 1.8.2), since each column has unit length. The largest of p numbers with sum p is between 1 and p. The jth diagonal entry of (X~⊤X~)−1 is a Rayleigh quotient of a matrix whose largest eigenvalue is 1/μp2 (Theorem 1.7.7), and 1/μp2=ηp2/μ12≤ηp2.

  • (b) Number the columns so that x0=1 and x1,…,xk are the others. The centred and scaled columns are zj=(xj−x¯j1)/(nsj), so that R=Z⊤Z with Z=[z1,…,zk]. Since 1=nx~0 and xj=‖xj‖x~j, where x~j are the columns of X~, Zc=∑j=1kcj‖xj‖x~j−nx¯jx~0nsj=X~b, where bj=cj‖xj‖nsj(j≥1),b0=−∑j=1kcjx¯jsj. Because ‖xj‖2=n(sj2+x¯j2)≥nsj2, |bj|≥|cj|, so ‖b‖≥‖c‖. For a unit vector c, c⊤Rc=‖X~b‖2≥μp2‖b‖2≥μp2 by Theorem 1.7.7; minimizing over c gives λmin(R)≥μp2. Then ηp2≥1/μp2≥1/λmin(R) by (a), and 1/λmin(R)≥VIFj by Proposition 19.6.1(c).

  • (c) Take b with bj=‖xj‖, b0=−nx¯j and all other entries zero. Then X~b=xj−x¯j1, with squared length nsj2, while ‖b‖2=n(sj2+x¯j2)+nx¯j2. So μp2≤nsj2/(n(sj2+2x¯j2))=CVj2/(CVj2+2), and ηp≥1/μp by (a).

Part (b) is the formal content of the argument of Belsley (1984) for not centring: centring can only hide near-dependencies, never create them. Part (c) shows what it hides. A regressor that varies little relative to its mean is nearly a multiple of the column of ones, and that near-dependency with the intercept is invisible in R and in every variance inflation factor. The design of Example (A macroeconomic design) shows the size of the effect: scaled to unit length, its condition number is 383; centred and scaled, 124.

Whether the hidden dependency matters depends on the question, as in Example (A quadratic far from the origin). It inflates the variance of the intercept, the mean response at the origin, and of the coefficients whose columns take part in it. If the origin is far from the data, it concerns a question nobody asked; if the origin is meaningful, the uncentred diagnostic is the honest one. This chapter uses the uncentred scaled design, as Belsley, Kuh and Welsch do, and reports the centred condition number alongside.

26.3.2. The variance-decomposition proportions

Let the singular value decomposition of the scaled design be X~=∑k=1pμkuktk⊤, with orthonormal right singular vectors tk=(t1k,…,tpk)⊤. The p×p matrix with columns tk is orthogonal, so its rows also have unit length: ∑ktjk2=1 for every j.

Theorem 26.3.2 (Variance-decomposition proportions)

Let X have rank p, Cov(Y)=σ2I, and let ϕjk=tjk2/μk2.

  • (a) Var(β^j)=σ2‖xj‖2∑k=1pϕjk. The variance-decomposition proportions πkj=ϕjk∑l=1pϕjl satisfy ∑kπkj=1, do not depend on σ2, and vanish iff tjk=0. The term πkj is the share of Var(β^j) contributed by the canonical coordinate with condition index ηk.

  • (b) For each k, ‖∑jtjkx~j‖=μk while ‖tk‖=1: the vector tk holds the coefficients of a near-dependency among the scaled columns, of strength μk.

  • (c) Let S be a nonempty proper subset of {1,…,p} such that μk≤μS for k∈S and μk≥μL for k∉S, with μS<μL. Let wj=∑k∈Stjk2 measure the participation of column j in the near-dependencies indexed by S. Then ∑k∈Sπkj ≥ wjwj+(1−wj)μS2/μL2,Var(β^j) ≥ σ2wj‖xj‖2μS2.

Proof.

  • (a) Since 𝛃𝛃Xβ=X~D0−1β, the least squares coefficients of X~ are β~^j=‖xj‖β^j, and by Theorem 26.1.1(a) applied to X~, whose cross-product matrix has eigenvalues μk2 and eigenvectors tk, 𝛃Cov(β~^)=σ2∑ktktk⊤/μk2. Its jth diagonal entry is σ2∑kϕjk. The statements about the proportions follow from the definition, since μk>0.

  • (b) ∑jtjkx~j=X~tk=μkuk.

  • (c) Put A=∑k∈Sϕjk and B=∑k∉Sϕjk. Then A≥wj/μS2 and, since ∑k∉Stjk2=1−wj, B≤(1−wj)/μL2. The sum of the proportions over S is A/(A+B), which increases with A and decreases with B, so it is at least (wj/μS2)/(wj/μS2+(1−wj)/μL2), which is the stated bound. The variance bound is Var(β^j)≥σ2A/‖xj‖2 together with A≥wj/μS2.

Part (c) is why the proportions locate near-dependencies. If the condition indices in S are at least ten times those outside it, μS2/μL2≤0.01, then any column with even a tenth of its unit row inside S, wj≥0.1, has at least 0.1/(0.1+0.9×0.01)≈0.92 of its variance attributed to S, and its variance is inflated accordingly. A column that does not take part has proportions near zero there. When several small singular values are close together, the individual tk are poorly determined, and a column’s proportions can be split between their rows in unpredictable ratios. Part (c) bounds the sum over the group, which is stable, and that is what should be read (Exercise (C1)).

Idea. Belsley, Kuh and Welsch’s reading of the table: scale the columns to unit length; flag condition indices of about 30 or more (indices near 10 signal weak dependencies); in each flagged row, or group of rows with similar indices, look for two or more coefficients with large proportions, conventionally above 0.5. Those columns form a near-dependency. Confirm it by regressing one of them on the others.

Example (A consumption function)

Return to the regression of Example (Large factors, precise estimate), with an intercept and six regressors. The scaled design has condition indices and proportions

Figure 26.3.1 shows the table graphically. Three indices exceed 30, and the table separates them.

  • Index 270.9: intercept, income and population (proportions 0.985, 0.720, 0.999). The singular vector has entries −0.522, −0.236 and 0.817 on these columns and small ones elsewhere: population, which grew steadily with income, is nearly an affine function of it (R2=0.9885). Its small coefficient of variation, 0.156, alone forces an index of at least 9.1 by Proposition 26.3.1(c); the shared trend does the rest.
  • Index 74.3: the price level and money. CPI has proportion 0.995 and M1 0.569. Regressing CPI on income and M1 gives R2=0.9868.
  • Index 38.9: a weaker dependency involving M1 and income. Taken with the previous row, M1 has 0.290+0.569=0.859 of its variance in the two rows, and income has 0.149+0.130+0.720=0.999 in the three rows above 30. Income takes part in all three near-dependencies.

A proportion is a share, not a size: the 0.307 of the T-bill rate at index 270.9 is a share of a variance that collinearity has barely inflated (its variance inflation factor is 2.9). Centred and scaled, the design has condition number 37.9 against 270.9 uncentred: centring removes the intercept from the largest near-dependency, and what is left is the relation between income and population that the variance inflation factors 154.5 and 183.7 already showed.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
macro = sm.datasets.macrodata.load_pandas().data
names = ["const", "realdpi", "pop", "cpi", "m1", "tbilrate", "unemp"]
X = sm.add_constant(macro[names[1:]].to_numpy())
X_s = X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=0)                   # unit-length columns, not centred

U, mu, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X_s, full_matrices=False)   # X_s = U diag(mu) V'
eta = mu[0] / mu                                      # condition indices
phi = Vt.T ** 2 / mu**2                               # phi[j, k] = v_jk^2 / mu_k^2
prop = (phi / phi.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)).T       # row k: proportions for index eta_k

print("index   " + " ".join(f"{nm[:7]:>7s}" for nm in names))
for k in range(len(mu)):
    print(f"{eta[k]:7.1f} " + " ".join(f"{p:7.3f}" for p in prop[k]))
Z = X[:, 1:] - X[:, 1:].mean(axis=0)
Z_s = Z / np.linalg.norm(Z, axis=0)                   # centred, then unit length
mu_c = np.linalg.svd(Z_s, compute_uv=False)
print("scaled condition number, not centred:", round(eta[-1], 1))
print("scaled condition number, centred    :", round(mu_c[0] / mu_c[-1], 1))
cv = X[:, 1:].std(axis=0) / X[:, 1:].mean(axis=0)
print("coefficients of variation:", dict(zip(names[1:], np.round(cv, 3))))
def r_squared(target, others):
    fit = sm.OLS(macro[target], sm.add_constant(macro[others])).fit()
    return fit.rsquared

print("pop on realdpi     : R^2 =", round(r_squared("pop", ["realdpi"]), 4))
print("cpi on realdpi, m1 : R^2 =", round(r_squared("cpi", ["realdpi", "m1"]), 4))

The script behind the example checks the bound of Theorem 26.3.2(c) for the groups formed by the last one, two and three indices, and the inequalities of Proposition 26.3.1.

26.3.3. Single cases

Collinearity diagnostics can be dominated by a few cases: one far out along a near-dependency can create it, one lying across it can hide it. The deletion formulas of Chapter 20 show that only cases of high leverage can have much effect.

Proposition 26.3.3 (Deleting a case)

Let X have rank p, let x(i) be its ith row with leverage hii<1, let X[i] be X without that row (Theorem 20.3.1 writes X(i); square brackets here avoid a clash with X(j), the matrix without column j), and write G=(X⊤X)−1, G[i]=(X[i]⊤X[i])−1.

  • (a) a⊤G[i]a=a⊤Ga+(a⊤Gx(i))2/(1−hii) for every a.

  • (b) a⊤Ga≤a⊤G[i]a≤a⊤Ga/(1−hii): deleting case i multiplies the variance of every linear function of 𝛃β^ by a factor between 1 and 1/(1−hii).

  • (c) (1−hii)λmin(X⊤X)≤λmin(X[i]⊤X[i])≤λmin(X⊤X).

Proof.

  • (a) This is the rank-one deletion formula G[i]=G+Gx(i)x(i)⊤G/(1−hii) of Example (Deleting an observation), on which Theorem 20.3.1 rests, sandwiched between a⊤ and a.
  • (b) The lower bound is clear from (a). For the upper, apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the inner product ⟨a,b⟩=a⊤Gb, which is legitimate because G is positive definite: (a⊤Gx(i))2≤(a⊤Ga)hii. Then a⊤G[i]a≤a⊤Ga(1+hii/(1−hii)).
  • (c) For a positive definite matrix, 1/λmin is the largest eigenvalue of the inverse, which is max‖a‖=1a⊤(⋅)−1a (Theorem 1.7.7). Taking the maximum in (b) over unit vectors gives the two inequalities.

So removing a case of small leverage changes the smallest eigenvalue by at most the factor 1−hii, and no variance by more than 1/(1−hii). In the consumption regression the largest leverage is 0.161, at 2009Q3, so by (c) no single quarter can reduce the smallest eigenvalue of the scaled cross-product matrix by more than the factor 1.192, with the column scaling of the full data held fixed. The largest reduction actually achieved by deleting one of the 203 quarters is by the factor 1.055. The near-dependencies of this design are carried by the whole sample, not by a few quarters.

Adding a case never increases a variance, by (b) read the other way round, but it can increase every variance inflation factor and the condition number (Exercise (B2)).

26.3.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In the table of Example (A consumption function), why is the intercept’s variance almost entirely attributed to the largest index, although the intercept is not an economic variable? Which coefficients would you expect to change most if a few more years of data were added in which population grew faster than income?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let X=[1,x] with x¯≠0. Show that the scaled design has μ1,22=1±cos⁡θ, where cos⁡θ=1/1+CV2 is the cosine of the angle between 1 and x, and hence η22=(1+cos⁡θ)/(1−cos⁡θ). Compare with the bound of Proposition 26.3.1(c). What is the variance inflation factor of x?

Solution. The scaled columns are unit vectors at angle θ, so X~⊤X~=(1cc1) with c=cos⁡θ, whose eigenvalues are 1±c (Exercise (B1)). Now c=1⊤x/(n‖x‖)=nx¯/n⋅n(s2+x¯2)=1/1+CV2 (taking x¯>0). So η22=(1+c)/(1−c). The bound of the proposition gives μ22≤CV2/(CV2+2). For small CV, 1−c≈CV2/2, so μ22=1−c≈CV2/2, and the bound is attained to first order. With a single regressor Rj2=0 and VIF=1: the variance inflation factor cannot see this near-dependency at all.

Exercise (B2)

Take n=20 cases with (x1,x2) at the points of a 4×5 grid in [−1,1]2, so that the regressors are uncorrelated, and add a twenty-first case at (t,t). Show that the correlation of the two regressors tends to one as t→∞, so that both variance inflation factors grow without bound, while no variance of any linear function of 𝛃β^ increases. Reconcile the two statements.

Solution. By Proposition 26.3.3(b) read backwards, adding a case can only decrease a⊤Ga for every a. The sample covariance of x1,x2 in the enlarged data is t2(1−1/21)/21 (the grid contributes nothing, being centred and uncorrelated), and the sample variance of xj is (Sj+t2(1−1/21))/21, where Sj is the grid’s sum of squares for xj. As t→∞ the correlation tends to one and VIFj=1/(1−r2)→∞. But Var(β^j)=σ2VIFj/Sjj, and Sjj grows like t2 as fast as VIFj does, so the variances converge to finite limits no larger than before. The variance inflation factor compares with an orthogonal design with the new spread, which the added point has made enormous; the point added information along (1,1) and none across it.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Suppose μk=μ for all k∈S. Show that the singular vectors tk, k∈S, are determined only up to an orthogonal transformation of their span, that the individual proportions πkj, k∈S, depend on the choice, and that ∑k∈Sπkj does not.

Solution. If TS has columns tk, k∈S, and O is orthogonal, then X~⊤X~TSO=μ2TSO, so the columns of TSO are equally valid singular vectors, and the individual tjk2 change with O. But ∑k∈Sϕjk=μ−2∑k∈Stjk2=μ−2ej⊤TSTS⊤ej, and TSTS⊤ is the projection onto the span, which does not depend on O. The denominator ∑lϕjl is a diagonal entry of (X~⊤X~)−1 and does not depend on the choice either.

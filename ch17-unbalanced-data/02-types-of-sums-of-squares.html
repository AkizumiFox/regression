17.2  Types of sums of squares and what they test

Section 9.3 defined the Type I, II and III sums of squares by the subspaces they project onto (Definition 9.3.1, Definition 9.3.2). It also found the cell-mean hypotheses of three of them in the two-way layout: equal weighted row means for a factor entered first, equal unweighted row means for Type III, and a single column of the table for last-in sums of squares under reference coding (Theorem 9.3.2). This section completes the list with one lemma, and then shows that the hypothesis of a main effect’s last-in sum of squares is decided by the coding of the other factor.

17.2.1. One lemma for every sum of squares

Every term of a factorial model has columns that are constant within cells, so every model matrix we meet has the form WA for a matrix A with p rows, one for each cell. On the cell scale, projections become weighted by the counts: write HA=A(A⊤DA)−A⊤D, the operator that fits a table of cell means by the columns of A with weights nω.

Lemma 17.2.1 (The hypothesis of an extra sum of squares)

Let X1=WA1 and X2=WA2, let P1 project onto C(X1), and let Q be the projection onto C([X1,X2]) minus P1, so that SS(X2∣X1)=y⊤Qy. For every table of cell means 𝛍μ:

  • (a) 𝛍𝛍P1Wμ=WHA1μ;

  • (b) 𝛍QWμ=0 iff 𝛍A2⊤D(I−HA1)μ=0;

  • (c) in the cell-means model, SS(X2∣X1) is the sum of squares of the hypothesis in (b), and its F ratio with the cell-means error mean square is the F statistic Eq. 17.1.1 of that hypothesis.

Proof.

  • (a) X1⊤X1=A1⊤DA1, so by Theorem 6.3.6 𝛍𝛍𝛍P1Wμ=WA1(A1⊤DA1)−A1⊤W⊤Wμ=WHA1μ.
  • (b) By Lemma 9.2.1, Q projects onto C(T) with T=(I−P1)X2. A projection annihilates a vector iff the vector is orthogonal to its range, so 𝛍QWμ=0 iff 𝛍T⊤Wμ=0. By (a), 𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍T⊤Wμ=X2⊤(I−P1)Wμ=A2⊤W⊤W(I−HA1)μ=A2⊤D(I−HA1)μ.
  • (c) Let 𝛍𝛍S0={Wμ:QWμ=0}, the mean vectors allowed by the hypothesis. Since C(Q)⊆C(W), the orthogonal complement of S0 within C(W) is C(Q), and y⊤Qy is the drop in residual sum of squares from S0 to C(W) (Eq. 6.5.2). That is the sum of squares of the hypothesis (Theorem 11.3.2(b)), whose F statistic is Eq. 17.1.1 by Proposition 17.1.1(d).

Part (b) says that the extra sum of squares of X2 tests whether the residual of the true cell means, after the terms already fitted and weighted by the counts, is orthogonal to the new term. Unless D is a multiple of the identity, the counts enter the hypothesis. That is the root of every difference between the types.

17.2.2. The complete list for two factors

Let the a×b layout have all nij≥1, with row totals ni⋅, column totals n⋅j and n=∑ijnij. Besides the weighted row means μ¯iw of Section 17.1, write μ¯⋅jw=1n⋅j∑inijμij,μ¯w=1n∑i,jnijμij for the weighted column means and the weighted grand mean. As in Chapter 9, SS(B∣μ,A) denotes the extra sum of squares of the columns of B given the intercept and A.

Theorem 17.2.2 (The cell-mean hypotheses of the types of sums of squares)

In the two-way layout with all cells occupied:

  • (a) SS(A∣μ), the Type I sum of squares of A entered first, addresses μ¯1w=⋯=μ¯aw;

  • (b) SS(B∣μ,A), which is both the Type I sum of squares of B entered second and the Type II sum of squares of B, addresses

    ∑inij(μij−μ¯iw)=0for j=1,…,b;
    (17.2.1)

  • (c) SS(A∣μ,B), the Type II sum of squares of A, addresses

    ∑jnij(μij−μ¯⋅jw)=0for i=1,…,a;
    (17.2.2)

  • (d) the Type III sum of squares of A addresses μ¯1⋅=⋯=μ¯a⋅;

  • (e) SS(AB∣μ,A,B), the interaction entry of every type, addresses additivity: μij−μil−μkj+μkl=0 for all i,k,j,l.

The hypotheses have a−1, b−1, a−1, a−1 and (a−1)(b−1) degrees of freedom, and each sum of squares is the sum of squares Eq. 17.1.1 of its hypothesis. The statements for B follow by exchanging the factors.

Proof. Parts (a) and (d) are Theorem 9.3.2(a) and (b). By Lemma 17.2.1(c), each sum of squares is the sum of squares of the hypothesis given by Lemma 17.2.1(b), and it remains to identify that hypothesis and count its degrees of freedom.

  • (b) Take X1=[1,ZA] and X2=ZB. The columns of X1 span the vectors that are constant within rows, so P1 replaces each entry of a vector by the average of its row. Applied to 𝛍Wμ, this gives μ¯iw in row i. The entry j of 𝛍ZB⊤(I−P1)Wμ sums μij−μ¯iw over the observations in column j, which is the left side of Eq. 17.2.1. Part (c) is the same argument with the factors exchanged.

For part (e), the projection is Q=M−PA+B, where PA+B projects onto the additive model space, so 𝛍QWμ=0 iff 𝛍Wμ lies in that space, that is, iff μij=αi+βj for some α,β. Additive tables have vanishing tetrads. Conversely, if all tetrads vanish, then μij=μi1+μ1j−μ11, which is additive.

Degrees of freedom. A complete layout is connected, so the additive model matrix has rank a+b−1 (Theorem 8.6.1(a)). The degrees of freedom of the extra sums of squares are therefore a−1 for (a), (a+b−1)−a=b−1 for (b), a−1 for (c) and ab−(a+b−1) for (e). In each case this is rank(Q), the number of independent linear restrictions that the hypothesis places on 𝛍μ.

In the Type II hypothesis Eq. 17.2.2, row i is compared cell by cell with the weighted means μ¯⋅jw of its columns, with the row’s own counts as weights. The a equations sum to zero (Exercise (B1)). With two rows the hypothesis is ∑jhj(μ1j−μ2j)=0 with hj=n1jn2j/n⋅j (Exercise (B2)), which favours columns where both rows are well replicated. That is the most precise comparison when the row difference is the same in every column. When it is not, the weights are set by the design rather than by anyone’s question.

Under additivity, μij=αi+βj, the hypotheses separate cleanly. The Type II and Type III hypotheses both reduce to α1=⋯=αa: Type III because unweighted row means are αi+β¯, and Type II by Theorem 9.3.2(d). The Type I hypothesis becomes αi+∑jnijni⋅βj  the same for all i, which involves the column effects unless the counts are proportional (Theorem 9.4.3). A factor entered first is credited with the effects of the factors it is confounded with.

In Figure 17.2.1 the Type I coefficients (V1’s weighted mean minus the weighted grand mean) do not sum to zero within columns, so that hypothesis compares sites as well as varieties. The Type II and Type III tables have zero column sums and compare varieties within sites. Type II gives site j the weight n1j(n⋅j−n1j)/n⋅j, which is large only where V1 and the other varieties are both well replicated. Type III weights the sites equally.

17.2.3. Why Type III needs contrast codings

Definition 9.3.2 defines Type III through sum-to-zero coding, and Theorem 9.3.2(c) showed that reference coding gives a different hypothesis. The next result settles which codings give Type III and why.

Proposition 17.2.3 (Which coding a last-in sum of squares depends on)

In the two-way layout with all cells occupied, code A by an a×(a−1) coding matrix CA and B by a b×(b−1) coding matrix CB, with [1a,CA] and [1b,CB] nonsingular (Definition 8.5.1). Let the interaction columns be the products of the coded columns of A and B, and let w≠0 span the line C(CB)⊥⊆Rb. Then the partial sum of squares of the columns of A, with the intercept, B and the interaction in the model, addresses

∑jwjμij  is the same for all i.
(17.2.3)

The hypothesis depends neither on CA nor on the counts. It is the Type III hypothesis μ¯1⋅=⋯=μ¯a⋅ iff every column of CB sums to zero.

Proof. Put KA=[1a,CA] and KB=[1b,CB]. For an observation in cell (i,j), the product of the kth coded column of A and the lth coded column of B takes the value (CA)ik(CB)jl, the entry in row (i,j) of the corresponding column of CA⊗CB. So the full model matrix is W times the columns of KA⊗KB, namely 1a⊗1b, 1a⊗CB, CA⊗1b and CA⊗CB. The matrix KA⊗KB is nonsingular (Proposition 1.10.1(b)), so the full model space is C(W) and its ab columns are linearly independent. Deleting the a−1 columns CA⊗1b leaves the reduced model WF with F=[1a⊗1b, 1a⊗CB, CA⊗CB],rank(F)=ab−(a−1). Let 𝛍𝛍V={μ:(u⊗w)⊤μ=0 for all u∈1a⊥}. Each column of F lies in V: by the mixed-product rule, (u⊤⊗w⊤)(1a⊗v)=(u⊤1a)(w⊤v)=0 for any v, and (u⊤⊗w⊤)(CA⊗CB)=(u⊤CA)⊗(w⊤CB)=0 because w⊥C(CB). The functionals u⊗w, u∈1a⊥, form a space of dimension a−1, since u↦u⊗w is injective. So dim⁡V=ab−(a−1)=rank(F), and C(F)=V. The partial sum of squares is the drop in residual sum of squares from C(WF) to C(W), so it addresses the hypothesis 𝛍μ∈V. Now 𝛍(u⊗w)⊤μ=∑iui∑jwjμij vanishes for every contrast u iff ∑jwjμij does not depend on i, which is Eq. 17.2.3. Finally, w∝1b iff 1b⊥C(CB), that is, iff 1b⊤CB=0.

Sum-to-zero, Helmert and orthogonal polynomial codings of B all give the Type III sum of squares for A, whatever coding A receives. Reference coding of B at level r gives w=er and the simple effect of Theorem 9.3.2(c). With more factors (Exercise (C2)), the last-in sum of squares of a main effect is the Type III one when every other factor is coded by contrasts. In statsmodels, write C(x, Sum) for every factor.

17.2.4. The variety trial, five ways

Example (Five tables for one data set)

The table has the layout of Example (Party, education and political self-placement) in Chapter 9, and the listing computes its entries the same way, as squared lengths of projections. For the trial of Example (Three varieties at three sites):

Only the Type I columns add up to the model sum of squares 33.892; the Type II and Type III entries add up to 39.506 and 34.738. The verdicts on varieties differ. Entered first, F=0.91 and p=0.414: the raw averages 6.423, 6.583 and 6.275 mix each variety with the sites it was grown at. After sites (Type II), F=9.86 and p=0.001. Type III gives F=7.90 and p=0.002, the test of Example (Three varieties at three sites). The last column compares the varieties at S1 only (F=5.74, p=0.008).

The script checks every entry against statsmodels’ anova_lm (types 1, 2 and 3, with sum-to-zero and Helmert coding for type 3), and recovers each entry from the matrix L of its part of Theorem 17.2.2. It also confirms Proposition 17.2.3: reference coding of the varieties with contrast coding of the sites reproduces 4.958, and a random coding of the sites gives the sum of squares of Eq. 17.2.3.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
counts = np.array([[7, 4, 2],        # rows: varieties V1-V3; columns: sites S1-S3
                   [3, 5, 4],
                   [2, 3, 7]])
true_means = np.array([[5.8, 7.2, 7.6],
                       [5.0, 6.5, 7.5],
                       [4.4, 5.9, 7.2]])
rng = np.random.default_rng(20172)
rows = [(f"V{i + 1}", f"S{j + 1}", round(true_means[i, j] + rng.normal(0, 0.5), 1))
        for i in range(3) for j in range(3) for _ in range(counts[i, j])]
trial = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["variety", "site", "y"])

def proj(*blocks):
    """Orthogonal projection onto the span of the given column blocks (any rank)."""
    Z = np.column_stack(blocks)
    U, s, _ = np.linalg.svd(Z, full_matrices=False)
    U = U[:, s > 1e-10 * s[0]]
    return U @ U.T

y = trial["y"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)
one = np.ones((n, 1))
ZA = pd.get_dummies(trial["variety"]).to_numpy(float)     # variety indicators
ZB = pd.get_dummies(trial["site"]).to_numpy(float)        # site indicators
W = np.column_stack([ZA[:, [i]] * ZB for i in range(3)])  # the nine cell indicators

def ss(P):
    return y @ P @ y

P0, PA, PB, PAB, M = proj(one), proj(one, ZA), proj(one, ZB), proj(one, ZA, ZB), proj(W)
typeI_AB = {"A": ss(PA - P0), "B": ss(PAB - PA), "AB": ss(M - PAB)}   # variety first
typeI_BA = {"B": ss(PB - P0), "A": ss(PAB - PB), "AB": ss(M - PAB)}   # site first
typeII = {"A": ss(PAB - PB), "B": ss(PAB - PA), "AB": ss(M - PAB)}
def coding(labels, kind):
    """Columns coding a factor: sum-to-zero, or reference level (treatment)."""
    D = pd.get_dummies(labels).to_numpy(float)
    g = D.shape[1]
    C = np.vstack([np.eye(g - 1), -np.ones(g - 1)]) if kind == "sum" else np.eye(g)[:, 1:]
    return D @ C

def last_in(CA, CB):
    """Partial (last-in) sums of squares of A and B with interaction columns = products."""
    CAB = np.column_stack([CA[:, [i]] * CB for i in range(CA.shape[1])])
    full = proj(one, CA, CB, CAB)
    assert np.allclose(full, M)                  # every coding spans the cell-means space
    return {"A": ss(full - proj(one, CB, CAB)), "B": ss(full - proj(one, CA, CAB))}

typeIII = last_in(coding(trial["variety"], "sum"), coding(trial["site"], "sum"))
reference = last_in(coding(trial["variety"], "treatment"), coding(trial["site"], "treatment"))
sse = ss(np.eye(n) - M)
for name, t in [("Type I, variety first", typeI_AB), ("Type I, site first", typeI_BA),
                ("Type II", typeII), ("Type III (sum)", typeIII), ("reference coding", reference)]:
    print(f"{name:22s}", {k: round(float(v), 3) for k, v in t.items()})

The next cell builds the Type II hypothesis Eq. 17.2.2 and the Type I hypothesis as matrices on the nine cell means, and Eq. 17.1.1 recovers their entries in the table.

cells = trial.groupby(["variety", "site"])["y"]
ybar = cells.mean().to_numpy()                        # nine cell means, row-major
N = cells.size().to_numpy().reshape(3, 3)             # counts n_ij
npi, npj = N.sum(axis=1), N.sum(axis=0)

def hypothesis_ss(L):
    """Sum of squares of L mu = 0 in the cell-means model (redundant rows allowed)."""
    u = L @ ybar
    return u @ np.linalg.pinv(L @ np.diag(1 / N.ravel()) @ L.T) @ u

# Type II for variety: row i has coefficient n_ij (1[i = k] - n_kj / n_.j) on cell (k, j)
L2A = np.array([[N[i, j] * ((i == k) - N[k, j] / npj[j]) for k in range(3) for j in range(3)]
                for i in range(3)])
# Type I, variety first: the count-weighted variety means are equal
wrow = np.array([[(i == k) * N[k, j] / npi[k] for k in range(3) for j in range(3)]
                 for i in range(3)])
L1 = wrow[[0]] - wrow[1:]
print(f"Type II hypothesis: SS = {hypothesis_ss(L2A):.3f};  Type I hypothesis: SS = {hypothesis_ss(L1):.3f}")

17.2.5. Choosing a sum of squares

The advice of Section 9.3 stands: decide the hypothesis first, use Type I only when the order of entry is part of the question, and without interaction prefer Type II. The cell-mean hypotheses sharpen it in three ways.

  1. Test the interaction first. It is the same in every type and tests additivity (Theorem 17.2.2(e)). Under additivity Type II and Type III test the same hypothesis, and Type II is never less powerful (Exercise (C1)).

  2. If the factors interact, the weights of a main-effect hypothesis decide the answer. Choose weights that describe a population (equal weights give Type III), or report simple effects within each column with multiplicity-adjusted intervals (Chapter 13).

  3. Report the hypothesis, not the type. Anyone can read 𝛍Lμ=0, and Eq. 17.1.1 tests it. With empty cells this is the only safe course (Section 17.4).

17.2.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In a 2×2 layout with counts n11=6, n12=2, n21=1, n22=3, write the hypothesis of the Type I sum of squares of A entered first as one linear equation in the four cell means. Show that for the additive table μij=βj, in which A has no effect, the hypothesis holds only if β1=β2. In particular it fails for β1=0, β2=1.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that the left sides of the a equations Eq. 17.2.2 add up to zero for every table 𝛍μ. Show also that, for a connected design, the equations have rank exactly a−1. Hint: count degrees of freedom.

Solution. Summing over i gives ∑ijnijμij−∑jn⋅jμ¯⋅jw=∑ijnijμij−∑j∑inijμij=0. So at most a−1 of the equations are independent. By Lemma 17.2.1(b) the equations are 𝛍ZA⊤(I−PB)Wμ=0, where PB projects onto C([1,ZB]). The number of independent restrictions they impose on 𝛍μ is the rank of the projection onto C((I−PB)ZA), which is rank[1,ZA,ZB]−rank[1,ZB]=(a+b−1)−b=a−1 for a connected design (Theorem 8.6.1(a)).

Exercise (B2)

Show that when a=2, the Type II hypothesis Eq. 17.2.2 for A is the single equation ∑jhj(μ1j−μ2j)=0 with hj=n1jn2j/(n1j+n2j). Show that σ2/hj is the variance of y¯1j−y¯2j, and conclude that under additivity the Type II hypothesis compares the rows with inverse-variance weights.

Solution. The first equation is ∑jn1j(μ1j−(n1jμ1j+n2jμ2j)/n⋅j)=∑j(n1jn2j/n⋅j)(μ1j−μ2j), and the second is its negative (Exercise (B1)). The variance of y¯1j−y¯2j is σ2(1/n1j+1/n2j)=σ2n⋅j/(n1jn2j)=σ2/hj. If the difference δ=μ1j−μ2j is the same in every column, the most precise combination of the column differences weights them in proportion to hj, and the hypothesis says that this combination is zero.

Exercise (B3)

Suppose the counts are proportional, nij=ni⋅n⋅j/n. Show that the hypotheses of Theorem 17.2.2(a) and (c) coincide, and that both say that the row means weighted by the column shares n⋅j/n are equal. Why does this agree with Theorem 9.4.3?

Solution. With proportional counts, nij/ni⋅=n⋅j/n=:πj for every i, so μ¯iw=∑jπjμij. The left side of Eq. 17.2.2 is ni⋅∑jπj(μij−μ¯⋅jw)=ni⋅(μ¯iw−∑jπjμ¯⋅jw), and ∑jπjμ¯⋅jw=μ¯w. So (c) says μ¯iw=μ¯w for all i, which is (a). In Theorem 9.4.3 the centred factor spaces are orthogonal, so SS(A∣μ,B)=SS(A∣μ) for every data set. Two sums of squares that agree for every y must address the same hypothesis.

Exercise (B4)

Use Proposition 17.2.3 to find the hypothesis of the last-in sum of squares of A when B (with b=3) is coded by (a) the Helmert columns (−1,1,0)⊤,(−1,−1,2)⊤; (b) the orthogonal polynomial columns (−1,0,1)⊤,(1,−2,1)⊤; (c) the columns (1,0,0)⊤,(0,0,1)⊤, reference coding with level 2 as reference; (d) the columns (1,0,0)⊤,(0,1,0)⊤ plus 12(1,1,1)⊤ added to both.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Suppose the cell means are additive. Let PII=PA+B−PB and let PIII be the projection of the Type III sum of squares of A. Both have rank a−1, and both are used with the cell-means error mean square. Show that the noncentrality parameters satisfy 𝛍𝛍‖PIIWμ‖2≥‖PIIIWμ‖2, so that the Type II test is at least as powerful. Hint: both are squared distances from 𝛍Wμ to a subspace.

Solution. Write 𝛍v=Wμ, which lies in the additive space C(PA+B). Then PIIv=v−PBv, so ‖PIIv‖2 is the squared distance from v to C([1,ZB]). Also PIII=M−PR, where PR projects onto the reduced space C([1,CB-columns,interaction columns]), and Mv=v. So ‖PIIIv‖2 is the squared distance from v to C(PR). This space contains C([1,ZB]), and the distance to a larger subspace is smaller. The two F statistics have the same degrees of freedom, and the power of an F test increases with its noncentrality (Theorem 11.5.1).

Exercise (C2)

In an a×b×d layout, where C has d levels and all cells are occupied, code the factors by CA,CB,CC, and let the model contain all main effects and all two- and three-factor interactions, formed as products of coded columns. Show that the last-in sum of squares of the columns of A addresses equality over i of ∑j,kvjwkμijk, where v spans C(CB)⊥ and w spans C(CC)⊥. When is this the unweighted hypothesis μ¯1⋅⋅=⋯=μ¯a⋅⋅?

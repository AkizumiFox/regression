44.3  Varying-coefficient models

The additive model of Definition 44.1.1 has no interactions: whatever z2 does, the contribution of z1 is the same curve. Throwing that restriction away brings back the curse of dimensionality, so relax it in one direction at a time. The most useful way keeps a linear effect and lets its slope move.

44.3.1. The model

Definition 44.3.1 (Varying-coefficient model)

Let w1,…,wn be the values of an interaction variable and z1,…,zn those of an effect modifier. A varying-coefficient term is

f(zi)wi,∑i=1nf(zi)=0,
(44.3.1)

and the varying-coefficient model is a structured additive model (Definition 44.1.1) in which one or more of the terms have this form. Combined with a parametric column w=(w1,…,wn)⊤ carrying a coefficient βw, the total effect of w on the response is

E(yi)=⋯+{βw+f(zi)}wi,
(44.3.2)

so that βw+f(z) is the coefficient function: the slope in w at effect modifier value z. With wi≡1 the term is an ordinary smooth main effect, and with f constrained to be constant it is an ordinary linear effect.

Three readings of the same object:

  • An interaction, the smooth analogue of the product term βuz of Section 5.7. Where a product term says the slope in w is linear in z, this one says only that it is smooth.
  • A factor-by-curve model, when w indicates a group: each group gets its own deviation curve in z.
  • A time-varying coefficient, when z is calendar time: the relationship has drifted, and the model does not say how.

44.3.2. Estimation is the same computation

Varying coefficients cost nothing new because the design of the term is a row-scaled basis.

Proposition 44.3.1 (A varying-coefficient term is an ordinary penalized term)

Let B1,…,Bd be a basis for the effect modifier and P0 the penalty that the corresponding smooth main effect would use. Put (Z)il=wiBl(zi).

  • (a) With f=∑lγlBl, the vector of the term’s contributions is 𝛄{f(zi)wi}i=Zγ, and the roughness of f is 𝛄𝛄γ⊤P0γ as before. The term is therefore the pair (Z,P0), and everything in Sections 44.1 and 44.2 applies unchanged.

  • (b) The constraint Eq. 44.3.1 is 𝛄c⊤γ=0 with c=(∑iB1(zi),…,∑iBd(zi))⊤ — the unweighted column sums of the basis, not of Z — and is absorbed by Proposition 44.1.1(c). Under it, βw in Eq. 44.3.2 is the average of the coefficient function over the design points, βw=n−1∑i{βw+f(zi)}.

  • (c) If wi≠0 for at least d design points with distinct zi and the basis is linearly independent there, Z has full column rank. Observations with wi=0 contribute nothing to the term: the coefficient function is estimated only where the interaction variable is nonzero.

Proof.

  • (a) The ith entry of 𝛄Zγ is ∑lγlwiBl(zi)=wif(zi). The penalty is a statement about f, so it is unchanged by multiplying the term by wi.
  • (b) 𝛄∑if(zi)=∑lγl∑iBl(zi)=c⊤γ, which is the stated constraint; Proposition 44.1.1(c) applies verbatim with c in place of Z⊤1. The averaging statement is then 𝛄n−1∑i{βw+f(zi)}=βw+n−1c⊤γ=βw.
  • (c) Suppose 𝛄Zγ=0. Then wif(zi)=0 for all i, so f(zi)=0 at the d design points with wi≠0; linear independence of the basis at those points forces 𝛄γ=0. The last sentence is the same computation read forwards: rows with wi=0 are zero rows of Z.

Warning. Constraining the weighted sum ∑iwif(zi)=0 instead — what centring the columns of Z does — also identifies the model, but βw is then not the average slope, and if w has been centred it means nothing at all, since ∑iwi=0 leaves the constraint silent about the level of f. Use (b).

44.3.3. An example, and how far it can be pushed

Example (Does poverty matter more in urban states?)

Example (Poverty and murder rates) fitted the murder rate of the 50 US states in 2009 on the poverty rate, the single-parent percentage and the urban percentage, reporting one coefficient for poverty. Here that coefficient may vary smoothly with how urban the state is: murderi=β0+β1singlei+{β2+f(urbani)}(povertyi−poverty―)+f0(urbani)+εi, with P-spline terms of 8 basis functions for f and f0 and smoothing parameters chosen by the restricted-likelihood updates of Section 44.5 (Proposition 44.5.3). The fit uses 6.83 effective degrees of freedom in all, of which 1.82 go to the smooth main effect of urbanization and 2.01 to the coefficient function.

The average poverty slope is 0.250. Keeping the smooth main effect of urbanization but forcing the poverty slope to be constant gives 0.231, and Example (Poverty and murder rates), in which urbanization entered linearly as well, gave 0.2538. The estimated coefficient function rises from −0.034 at 20% urban to 0.364 at 60% and falls back to 0.152 at 90%: on its face, a story in which poverty is associated with homicide in middling states and not in the most rural ones.

That story does not survive its standard errors. The band of ±2 standard errors in Figure 44.3.1 contains the constant coefficient over the whole range, and the contrast between 60% and 20% urban is 0.398 with a standard error of 0.234, a ratio of 1.70. Fifty states and a residual standard deviation of 1.418 murders per 100,000 cannot resolve a coefficient function; they can only say that one is not needed.

Fitting takes no new code, because Proposition 44.3.1(a) has made this an ordinary term: the design is the P-spline basis in the urban percentage scaled row by row by the centred poverty column, the penalty is the same second-difference matrix, and the same solver handles it. The band is read off the joint covariance of 𝛄(β2,γ) with β2+f(z) evaluated on a grid; the code is code/ch44/varying_coefficient.py.

Remark (What a coefficient function means). Example (Does poverty matter more in urban states?) is an observational comparison across states, and Chapter 25 applies to it word for word. A varying coefficient describes how an association changes with the modifier. Reading it as “raising poverty by one point in a 60%-urban state would raise the murder rate by 0.36” requires everything Theorem 25.6.2 requires, and requires it separately at each level of the modifier, since the confounding structure may differ across it. The extra flexibility buys a richer description, not weaker causal assumptions.

44.3.4. Where varying coefficients take you

Two extensions use nothing beyond Proposition 44.3.1. With z time and w a covariate observed repeatedly, f(ti)wi is a coefficient drifting over the study; for series data the band must then come from Part VII rather than from the independence assumption of Definition 44.1.1 (Exercise (B1)). With z a location and a spatial term from Section 44.4 in place of the P-spline, the coefficient varies over a map, which is disease mapping: the same code with a different penalty.

When neither covariate deserves to be the linear one, a genuine bivariate term f(z1,z2) is needed. The standard construction is a tensor-product basis Blm(z1,z2)=Bl(1)(z1)Bm(2)(z2) with the penalty

P=λ1(I⊗P(1))+λ2(P(2)⊗I),
(44.3.3)

penalizing roughness along each coordinate separately, and so invariant to the units of the two covariates. Everything in this chapter goes through for it, with two smoothing parameters instead of one. The book does not develop the construction further; how to scale the two penalties, how to separate the interaction from its main effects and how to display the surface belong to a book on smoothing, and Wood (2017) is where to find them. Exercise (C1) builds the smallest case by hand.

44.3.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

What does a varying-coefficient term become when (i) wi≡1; (ii) f is forced to be constant; (iii) f is forced to be linear in z? Which of the three has a familiar name from Part II?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

A varying-coefficient model is fitted to a time series, but the errors follow 𝛆Cov(ε)=σ2V with V≠I. Show that the penalized estimate 𝛄γ^=A−1Z⊤y is still unbiased for the penalized target A−1Z⊤E(Y), but that its covariance is σ2A−1Z⊤VZA−1, not σ2A−1Z⊤ZA−1. If V has positive autocorrelations, in which direction is the naive band wrong?

Solution. Linearity gives both at once: E(A−1Z⊤Y)=A−1Z⊤E(Y) and Cov(A−1Z⊤Y)=A−1Z⊤Cov(Y)ZA−1 (Theorem 2.2.1). Under positive autocorrelation Z⊤VZ−Z⊤Z is typically nonnegative definite for smooth columns, so the true variance exceeds the naive one and the band is too narrow, as Theorem 19.2.2 found for a linear model. The repair is Chapter 31, or a random-effect term for the serial structure as in Chapter 33.

Exercise (B2)

Show that a model containing both a smooth main effect f0(z) and a varying-coefficient term f(z)w has exact concurvity (Definition 44.2.1) as soon as wi is itself a function of zi, say wi=h(zi) with h in the span of the basis. What does this say about the model of Example (Does poverty matter more in urban states?)?

Solution. If wi=h(zi) with h in the span, the varying term contributes f(zi)h(zi), a function of zi alone; whenever fh is again in the span, the main-effect term realizes it too, and subtracting gives a nontrivial relation. In Example (Does poverty matter more in urban states?) the poverty rate is not a function of the urban percentage, so the concurvity is partial rather than exact; but the two are correlated across states, so the terms compete, which is part of why the bands are wide.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Build the smallest tensor-product term by hand: take d1=d2=3 basis functions in each coordinate and first-difference penalties P(1)=P(2). Write out the 9×9 penalty of Eq. 44.3.3 for λ1=λ2=1 and find its null space. Why does that null space make the term identifiable only after the main effects have been constrained away?

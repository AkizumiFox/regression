44.1  The additive model

Suppose a response depends on five continuous covariates and nothing is known about the shape of that dependence. The honest model is E(Y∣z)=f(z1,…,z5), f unknown and smooth. Chapter 43 estimated such an f from one covariate by averaging the responses near the point of interest; in five dimensions that fails, for a reason that has nothing to do with the estimator.

44.1.1. Why additivity

Take n points spread over the unit cube in q dimensions. To collect a fixed fraction c of them, a cube-shaped neighbourhood must have side c1/q: with c=0.05 that is 0.05 in one dimension, about 0.55 in five and about 0.74 in ten. An average over three quarters of the range of every covariate is not local, and no estimator repairs that: the data are not dense enough. This is the curse of dimensionality, and the rates make it quantitative. For a twice-differentiable curve Theorem 43.1.3 gives a mean squared error of order n−4/5; in q dimensions the same expansion gives n−4/(4+q), and no estimator does better for twice-differentiable regression functions (Stone, 1980, whose conditions and proof are not reproduced here). At q=5 a twelve-fold increase in sample size buys only what a four-fold increase buys in one dimension.

The response is to restrict the shape of f — not to a linear function, usually too severe a restriction, but to a sum of functions of one variable each.

Definition 44.1.1 (Additive model)

Let (yi,x(i),zi1,…,ziq), i=1,…,n, be observed, where x(i)⊤ is the ith row of an n×p matrix X whose first column is 1. The additive model is

𝛃yi=x(i)⊤β+∑j=1qfj(zij)+εi,E(εi)=0, Var(εi)=σ2,
(44.1.1)

with the εi uncorrelated, the fj unknown smooth functions, and the identifiability constraints

∑i=1nfj(zij)=0,j=1,…,q.
(44.1.2)

The vector 𝛈𝛃η=Xβ+∑jfj, where fj={fj(z1j),…,fj(znj)}⊤, is the additive predictor. If X=1 the model is purely additive; otherwise it is a partial linear or semiparametric model.

Two comments at once. The model contains no interactions: the effect of z1 is the same curve whatever z2 does, and Section 44.3 buys some of that flexibility back. And Eq. 44.1.2 is not a restriction on the model but a choice of representation, because the additive predictor fixes the pieces only up to constants.

Proposition 44.1.1 (Identifiability of the components)

Write Vj for the linear space of vectors {g(z1j),…,g(znj)}⊤ as g ranges over the functions the jth term is allowed to take, and assume 1∈Vj for each j.

  • (a) If 𝛃(β,f1,…,fq) and 𝛃(β~,f~1,…,f~q) give the same additive predictor and both satisfy Eq. 44.1.2, then every difference fj−f~j is orthogonal to 1, and so is 𝛃𝛃X(β−β~)=−∑j(fj−f~j). If in addition the centred spaces C(X)∩1⊥, V1∩1⊥,…,Vq∩1⊥ admit no nontrivial relation — no exact concurvity, the condition studied in Section 44.2 — then 𝛃𝛃Xβ=Xβ~ and fj=f~j for every j.

  • (b) Without Eq. 44.1.2 the components are determined only up to constants that sum to zero: replacing fj by fj+cj with ∑jcj=0 leaves 𝛈η unchanged.

  • (c) If 𝛄fj=Zjγj for a design block Zj, the constraint 𝛄1⊤Zjγj=0 is imposed exactly by writing 𝛄𝛂γj=Ujαj, where the columns of Uj are an orthonormal basis of 𝛄𝛄{γ:1⊤Zjγ=0}: minimizing any criterion over 𝛂αj with design ZjUj and penalty Uj⊤PjUj is the same as minimizing it over 𝛄γj subject to the constraint.

Proof.

  • (b) is immediate from ∑j(fj+cj)=∑jfj+∑jcj. For (a), equality of the predictors gives 𝛃𝛃X(β−β~)=−∑j(fj−f~j). Each difference fj−f~j lies in Vj∩1⊥, because both terms satisfy Eq. 44.1.2, so the left-hand side lies in C(X)∩1⊥ and the displayed equation is a relation among the centred spaces. That alone does not separate the pieces. Under the additional hypothesis the relation must be trivial, so 𝛃𝛃X(β−β~)=0 and every difference vanishes.

For (c), 𝛄𝛄{γ:1⊤Zjγ=0} is the null space of the row vector c⊤=1⊤Zj, of dimension dj−1 when c≠0, and Uj is a bijection from Rdj−1 onto it. At 𝛄𝛂γj=Ujαj the design term is 𝛂(ZjUj)αj and the penalty 𝛂𝛂αj⊤(Uj⊤PjUj)αj, so the two minimizations agree at corresponding points and the correspondence is onto the feasible set.

A QR factorization of Zj⊤1 produces Uj in one line: the first column of Q spans c, the rest its orthogonal complement. Absorbing the constraint this way rather than centring the columns of Zj matters, because a basis with the partition-of-unity property of Proposition 43.2.1 has Zj1=1: subtracting column means leaves Zj with a null vector and the penalized cross-product singular (Exercise (B1)).

44.1.2. Terms, designs and penalties

Every term in this chapter is represented in a basis. Choose dj basis functions for the jth component and write

𝛄fj(z)=∑l=1djγjlBjl(z),fj=Zjγj,(Zj)il=Bjl(zij),
(44.1.3)

after which the constraint of Proposition 44.1.1(c) has been absorbed, so that Zj has dj columns summing to zero. With the B-spline basis of Definition 43.2.1 and the difference penalty of Definition 43.3.1 this is a P-spline block, the default term throughout the chapter; but nothing below uses that particular basis. All that is used is that the roughness of the jth component is a quadratic form 𝛄𝛄γj⊤Pjγj with Pj symmetric nonnegative definite.

The estimate is then the minimizer of a penalized least squares criterion in the sense of Definition 30.1.1, with one penalty per term:

𝛃𝛄𝛄𝛃𝛄𝛄𝛄Q(β,γ1,…,γq)=‖y−Xβ−∑j=1qZjγj‖2+∑j=1qλjγj⊤Pjγj.
(44.1.4)

Collect the pieces: let Z=[X,Z1,…,Zq] with d=p+∑jdj columns, let 𝛄𝛃𝛄𝛄γ=(β⊤,γ1⊤,…,γq⊤)⊤, and let

P=(0λ1P1⋱λqPq)
(44.1.5)

be the block-diagonal penalty, with a zero block for the unpenalized columns. Then Eq. 44.1.4 is 𝛄𝛄𝛄‖y−Zγ‖2+γ⊤Pγ, a ridge-type criterion with a structured penalty, and differentiating gives the penalized normal equations

𝛄Aγ=Z⊤y,A=Z⊤Z+P.
(44.1.6)
Example (Two P-spline blocks)

Take q=2, a cubic B-spline basis of 15 functions on equally spaced knots for each covariate, and the second-difference penalty of Definition 43.3.1. Unconstrained, each block has 15 columns summing to 1 row by row and a penalty of rank 13; after Proposition 44.1.1(c) it has 14 columns summing to zero and a penalty of the same rank, so Z=[1,Z1,Z2] has 29 columns. Of the two unpenalized directions per block, constant and linear trend, the constraint removes one: the term still fits a straight line in zj free of charge, but not an arbitrary level.

44.1.3. The fit is a linear smoother

Because Eq. 44.1.4 is quadratic, the fit is linear in y — a linear smoother in the sense of Proposition 43.5.1 — and every quantity of interest is a trace, exactly as for ridge regression.

Proposition 44.1.2 (Smoother matrix and degrees of freedom)

Suppose A=Z⊤Z+P is nonsingular, and let C=A−1Z⊤, so that 𝛄γ^=Cy. Write Cj=ZjC[j], where C[j] holds the rows of C belonging to the jth block.

  • (a) The fitted vector is y^=Sy with S=ZA−1Z⊤, a symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues lie in [0,1]; the fitted jth component is f^j=Cjy, and S=XC[0]+∑jCj.

  • (b) The effective degrees of freedom of the fit and of its terms are

    df=trS=tr(A−1Z⊤Z),dfj=trCj=dj−λjtr{(A−1)jjPj},
    (44.1.7)

    and they add: df=p+∑jdfj. Each dfj decreases from dj at λj=0 towards dim⁡N(Pj) as λj→∞.

  • (c) If Cov(Y)=σ2I, then 𝛄Cov(γ^)=σ2A−1Z⊤ZA−1 and Cov(f^j)=σ2CjCj⊤.

Proof.

  • (a) Solving Eq. 44.1.6 gives 𝛄γ^=A−1Z⊤y and 𝛄y^=Zγ^=Sy. Symmetry is clear, and v⊤Sv≥0 because A−1 is positive definite. For the upper bound, fix v and put u=A−1Z⊤v, so that Au=Z⊤v. Then v⊤Sv=u⊤Au=‖Zu‖2+u⊤Pu ≥ ‖Zu‖2, while also v⊤Sv=v⊤(Zu)≤‖v‖‖Zu‖≤‖v‖v⊤Sv by Cauchy–Schwarz. Dividing by v⊤Sv when it is nonzero gives v⊤Sv≤‖v‖2, so the eigenvalues lie in [0,1]. Splitting 𝛄Zγ^ by blocks gives the last identity.
  • (b) trS=tr(ZA−1Z⊤)=tr(A−1Z⊤Z) by the cyclic property (Theorem 1.6.1), and A−1Z⊤Z=A−1(A−P)=I−A−1P. The jth diagonal block of A−1P is λj(A−1)jjPj, and the block belonging to the unpenalized columns is zero; taking traces block by block gives both formulas in Eq. 44.1.7 and their sum. Monotonicity in λj is Exercise (B2), and as λj→∞ the term is forced into N(Pj), which costs dim⁡N(Pj) dimensions.
  • (c) Both are immediate from Cov(MY)=MCov(Y)M⊤ (Theorem 2.2.1).

Part (b) is the additive-model version of Theorem 27.2.2(b): each term is charged for the dimensions it uses, and the charges add up. The split makes a printed summary readable — “living area: 5.3 degrees of freedom” says at a glance how far from a straight line the curve is — and it is what Chapter 29 needs, since Definition 29.3.2 and Proposition 29.2.4 apply verbatim with df in place of the number of parameters.

Warning. The degrees of freedom of a term are not the degrees of freedom of a test: they are a trace computed at a fixed 𝛌λ, and nothing so far says that dfj is the right divisor for a sum of squares or the right parameter for a reference distribution. Section 44.6 returns to it.

44.1.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Repeat the neighbourhood calculation of Section 44.1: what side length does a cube-shaped neighbourhood need in order to contain 1% of n points uniformly spread over the unit cube in q dimensions, for q=1,3,10? At what q does the required side exceed 0.5?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let Zj be a B-spline block with the partition-of-unity property, so Zj1=1, and let Pj be a difference penalty, so Pj1=0. Show that centring the columns, that is replacing Zj by (I−n−111⊤)Zj, makes Zj⊤Zj+λjPj singular, while the reparameterization of Proposition 44.1.1(c) does not.

Solution. Write Z~j=(I−n−111⊤)Zj. Then Z~j1=0, so 1⊤(Z~j⊤Z~j+λjPj)1=0 and the matrix is singular. Under (c) the matrix is Uj⊤(Zj⊤Zj+λjPj)Uj; if it killed some 𝛂α≠0, then 𝛄𝛂γ=Ujα would satisfy 𝛄Zjγ=0 and 𝛄Pjγ=0. The second puts 𝛄γ in the span of the constant, or of the constant and linear sequences for a second-difference penalty, and then 𝛄Zjγ=0 forces 𝛄γ=0, since the B-spline basis reproduces those functions exactly.

Exercise (B2)

Show that dfj in Eq. 44.1.7 is nonincreasing in λj. Hint: work with one term and no parametric part first, where df(λ)=∑kθk/(θk+λ) for the eigenvalues θk of Zj⊤Zj in the metric of Pj.

Solution. Simultaneously diagonalize Zj⊤Zj and Pj: some nonsingular T gives T⊤Zj⊤ZjT=I and T⊤PjT=diag(νk), νk≥0, by Corollary 1.7.8(b). Then df(λ)=∑k1/(1+λνk), decreasing in λ except where νk=0, which contributes dim⁡N(Pj) whatever λ is. With several terms, argue the same way on the jth block of A−1P, treating the others as part of Z⊤Z.

Exercise (B3)

In Definition 44.1.1 take q=1 and let X be a general parametric design. Show that the penalized estimate of 𝛃β is 𝛃β^={X⊤(I−S1)X}−1X⊤(I−S1)y where S1=Z1(Z1⊤Z1+λ1P1)−1Z1⊤ — the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell form of Theorem 6.6.2 with the smoother in place of a projection. What changes in the proof?

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

An additive model has no interactions. Show that if the true mean is f(z1,z2)=z1z2 on [0,1]2 with z1,z2 independent and uniform, the best additive approximation in mean square, argming1,g2E{(Z1Z2−g1(Z1)−g2(Z2))2} subject to Eg1(Z1)=Eg2(Z2)=0, is g1(z)=(z−12)/2, g2(z)=(z−12)/2 plus the constant 1/4, and compute the mean squared approximation error.

27.5  Stein’s paradox and James–Stein estimation

The shrinkage estimators so far beat least squares only for some parameter values, which is unavoidable for linear estimators (Exercise (B1)). Stein (1956) discovered that a nonlinear shrinkage can do better everywhere: when three or more means are estimated together under total squared error, the estimator that is at once least squares, maximum likelihood and best unbiased is inadmissible. James and Stein (1961) gave an explicit estimator that dominates it. This section proves their theorem and explains it as an empirical Bayes phenomenon.

27.5.1. The normal means problem

Let 𝛉Z∼Np(θ,σ2I) with σ2 known, and measure an estimator 𝛉θ^(Z) by its risk 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉R(θ,θ^)=E‖θ^−θ‖2, its total mean squared error. The estimator Z has risk pσ2 for every 𝛉θ. An estimator dominates another if its risk is never larger and is smaller for some 𝛉θ; an estimator dominated by none is admissible.

This is regression in canonical form. If X⊤X=I, then 𝛃𝛃β^∼Np(β,σ2I) (Theorem 7.5.1(a)). For a general full-rank design, 𝛃Z=(X⊤X)1/2β^ is 𝛉Np(θ,σ2I) with 𝛉𝛃θ=(X⊤X)1/2β, and for any estimator 𝛃β~ with 𝛉𝛃θ^=(X⊤X)1/2β~, 𝛉𝛉𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃‖θ^−θ‖2=(β~−β)⊤X⊤X(β~−β)=‖Xβ~−Xβ‖2, the prediction loss at the design points, with the square root of Theorem 1.7.6 as the change of coordinates.

27.5.2. Stein’s lemma

The proof rests on an integration-by-parts identity for the normal distribution.

Lemma 27.5.1 (Stein’s lemma)

  • (a) Let Z∼N(μ,σ2), and let g:R→R be absolutely continuous with E|g′(Z)|<∞. Then E|(Z−μ)g(Z)|<∞ and E[(Z−μ)g(Z)]=σ2Eg′(Z).

  • (b) Let 𝛉Z∼Np(θ,σ2I), and let g=(g1,…,gp):Rp→Rp be such that, for each i and almost every value of the other coordinates, gi is absolutely continuous in zi, with E|∂gi(Z)/∂zi|<∞ and E|(Zi−θi)gi(Z)|<∞. Then

    𝛉E[(Z−θ)⊤g(Z)]=σ2E∑i=1p∂gi∂zi(Z).
    (27.5.1)

Proof.

  • (a) Write Z=μ+σW with W∼N(0,1) and h(w)=g(μ+σw), so that h′(w)=σg′(μ+σw). The claim becomes E[Wh(W)]=Eh′(W), so we may take μ=0, σ=1, g=h. Let φ be the standard normal density. Since φ′(t)=−tφ(t), φ(w)=∫w∞tφ(t)dt(w≥0),φ(w)=−∫−∞wtφ(t)dt(w≤0). Hence Eh′(W)=∫0∞h′(w)∫w∞tφ(t)dtdw−∫−∞0h′(w)∫−∞wtφ(t)dtdw. The double integrals converge absolutely: replacing h′ by |h′| and t by |t| gives ∫|h′(w)|φ(w)dw=E|h′(W)|<∞. By Fubini’s theorem we may reverse the order of integration. The first term becomes ∫0∞tφ(t)∫0th′(w)dwdt=∫0∞tφ(t){h(t)−h(0)}dt, and the second −∫−∞0tφ(t)∫t0h′(w)dwdt=∫−∞0tφ(t){h(t)−h(0)}dt, using absolute continuity of h. Their sum is E[W{h(W)−h(0)}]=E[Wh(W)], since EW=0. The same bound shows E|W{h(W)−h(0)}|≤E|h′(W)|, so E|Wh(W)|<∞.
  • (b) The coordinates of Z are independent. Fix i and condition on the other coordinates Z−i. By Fubini, E[|∂gi/∂zi|∣Z−i]<∞ for almost every value of Z−i, and for those values part (a), applied to zi↦gi(Z) with Zi∼N(θi,σ2), gives E[(Zi−θi)gi(Z)∣Z−i]=σ2E[∂gi(Z)/∂zi∣Z−i]. Take expectations and sum over i.

The lemma turns an expectation involving the unknown 𝛉θ into the expectation of a function of Z alone. Stein (1981) built a theory of risk estimation on it (Exercise (C2)), and it computes covariance degrees of freedom (Exercise (C1)) for nonlinear fits such as the lasso (Exercise (C1)).

27.5.3. The James–Stein theorem

Theorem 27.5.2 (James–Stein)

Let 𝛉Z∼Np(θ,σ2I) with p≥3 and σ2 known, and for a constant c let 𝛉θ^c=(1−cσ2/‖Z‖2)Z. Then E‖Z‖−2<∞ and:

  • (a) 𝛉𝛉E‖θ^c−θ‖2=pσ2−c{2(p−2)−c}σ4E‖Z‖−2. This is below pσ2 for every 𝛉θ iff 0<c<2(p−2), and smallest at c=p−2. The James–Stein estimator is 𝛉θ^JS=(1−(p−2)σ2‖Z‖2)Z, with risk

    𝛉𝛉E‖θ^JS−θ‖2=pσ2−(p−2)2σ4E‖Z‖−2<pσ2.
    (27.5.2)

  • (b) With 𝛉γ=‖θ‖2/σ2 and K∼Poisson(γ/2), σ2E‖Z‖−2=E{1/(p−2+2K)}. The risk of 𝛉θ^JS depends on 𝛉θ only through γ, equals 2σ2 at 𝛉θ=0, increases strictly in γ, and tends to pσ2 as γ→∞.

Proof. Finiteness. The density of Z is at most (2πσ2)−p/2, so E‖Z‖−2≤(2πσ2)−p/2∫‖z‖≤1‖z‖−2dz+1=(2πσ2)−p/2Ap∫01rp−3dr+1<∞, where Ap is the surface area of the unit sphere in Rp and the integral is finite because p≥3. (a) Apply Eq. 27.5.1 to g(z)=z/‖z‖2. For p≥3 the other coordinates Z−i are nonzero with probability one, and then gi is a smooth function of zi with ∂gi∂zi=1‖z‖2−2zi2‖z‖4,|∂gi∂zi|≤3‖z‖2,∑i∂gi∂zi=p−2‖z‖2. The bound makes E|∂gi/∂zi| finite, and |(Zi−θi)gi(Z)|≤|Zi−θi|/‖Z‖ has finite expectation by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. So 𝛉E[(Z−θ)⊤Z/‖Z‖2]=(p−2)σ2E‖Z‖−2. Now expand 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉‖θ^c−θ‖2=‖Z−θ‖2−2cσ2(Z−θ)⊤Z‖Z‖2+c2σ4‖Z‖2 and take expectations: pσ2−2c(p−2)σ4E‖Z‖−2+c2σ4E‖Z‖−2. The factor c{2(p−2)−c} is positive iff 0<c<2(p−2) and largest at c=p−2, where it equals (p−2)2. (b) 𝛉Z/σ∼Np(θ/σ,I), so U=‖Z‖2/σ2∼χ2(p,γ) (Definition 4.1.1), and by Theorem 4.1.2(d), given K=k, U∼χ2(p+2k). For m≥3, E{1/χ2(m)}=1/(m−2) (Exercise (A1)), which gives the formula. At γ=0, K=0 and the risk is pσ2−(p−2)σ2=2σ2. Since E(1/U)=∫0∞Pr(U<1/s)ds and each Pr(U<1/s) decreases strictly in γ (Proposition 4.1.3), E(1/U) decreases strictly and the risk increases. As γ→∞, K→∞ in probability and 1/(p−2+2K)≤1/(p−2), so E{1/(p−2+2K)}→0 by dominated convergence.

The shrinkage factor is close to one when ‖Z‖2 is large compared with its null expectation pσ2, and small when the data are consistent with 𝛉θ≈0. Nothing is special about the origin: shrinking towards any point fixed in advance also dominates Z, with the largest gains near that point (Exercise (B1)).

The paradox is that the coordinates need not be related. If θ1 is a physical constant, θ2 a crop yield and θ3 a batting average, the James–Stein estimate of the crop yield depends on the other two measurements. The resolution is that the theorem concerns the total risk; a single coordinate’s risk can exceed σ2 (Exercise (B2)).

Example (The risk function for ten means)

For p=10 and σ=1, the exact risk from Theorem 27.5.2(b) is 2 at 𝛉θ=0, 4.784 at 𝛉‖θ‖2=5, 7.587 at 20 and 9.257 at 80, always below the constant risk 10 of Z (Figure 27.5.1). The fixed shrinkage Z/(1+0.5), which is ridge regression with λ=0.5 in an orthonormal design, has risk 𝛉(p+0.25‖θ‖2)/2.25 (Exercise (A2)). It beats James–Stein only on a middle range, 𝛉5.9<‖θ‖2<36.6. At the origin it is worse (10/2.25=4.44 against 2), it exceeds p once 𝛉‖θ‖2>5p=50, and it grows without bound. The dotted curve is the risk 𝛉𝛉p‖θ‖2/(p+‖θ‖2) of the best linear shrinkage fZ for each 𝛉θ, the oracle of Proposition 27.1.2(c) with a common factor; James–Stein stays within 2 of it without knowing 𝛉θ.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
p = 10

def js_risk(gamma, p=p):
    """Exact risk of (1 - (p-2)/||Z||^2) Z when Z ~ N_p(theta, I) and gamma = ||theta||^2."""
    k = np.arange(0, 400)
    w = stats.poisson.pmf(k, gamma / 2)            # ||Z||^2 ~ chi^2(p, gamma): Poisson(gamma/2) mixture
    return p - (p - 2) ** 2 * np.sum(w / (p - 2 + 2 * k))

def js(z, positive=False):
    factor = 1 - (len(z) - 2) / np.sum(z ** 2)
    return (max(factor, 0.0) if positive else factor) * z

rng = np.random.default_rng(27)
for gamma in [0.0, 5.0, 20.0, 80.0]:
    theta = np.sqrt(gamma / p) * np.ones(p)
    Zs = theta + rng.normal(size=(2_000, p))
    loss_js = np.mean([np.sum((js(z) - theta) ** 2) for z in Zs])
    loss_pp = np.mean([np.sum((js(z, True) - theta) ** 2) for z in Zs])
    print(f"||theta||^2 = {gamma:5.1f}: exact JS risk {js_risk(gamma):.3f}, "
          f"simulated {loss_js:.3f}, positive part {loss_pp:.3f}  (MLE: {p})")

27.5.4. The positive part

When ‖Z‖2<(p−2)σ2 the James–Stein factor is negative, and the estimator reverses the sign of every coordinate, which is absurd. Truncating the factor at zero helps.

Proposition 27.5.3 (Positive-part James–Stein)

In the setting of Theorem 27.5.2, the estimator 𝛉θ^JS+=(1−(p−2)σ2‖Z‖2)+Z has strictly smaller risk than 𝛉θ^JS for every 𝛉θ.

Proof. Write 𝛉θ^JS=hZ with h=1−(p−2)σ2/‖Z‖2, and let ψ=max(−h,0)≥0, a function of ‖Z‖. Where h≥0 the estimators agree. Where h<0, the positive part is 0, and 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉‖hZ−θ‖2−‖θ‖2=h2‖Z‖2−2hθ⊤Z=ψ2‖Z‖2+2ψθ⊤Z. So the difference of risks is 𝛉E[ψ2‖Z‖2]+2E[ψθ⊤Z]. The first term is positive, because Pr(h<0)=Pr{‖Z‖2<(p−2)σ2}>0. For the second, choose an orthogonal Q with 𝛉Qθ=me1, 𝛉m=‖θ‖. Then w=QZ∼Np(me1,σ2I), ‖w‖=‖Z‖ and 𝛉θ⊤Z=mw1. Given R=∑i≥2wi2, which is independent of w1∼N(m,σ2), E[ψw1∣R]=∫0∞ψ(t2+R)t{φσ(t−m)−φσ(t+m)}dt≥0, where φσ is the N(0,σ2) density: the integrand pairs t with −t, and (t−m)2≤(t+m)2 for t,m≥0. (The expectations are finite since ψ≤(p−2)σ2/‖Z‖2.) Hence 𝛉E[ψθ⊤Z]≥0 and the risk difference is positive.

The positive-part estimator is itself inadmissible, but improvements on it are tiny (Lehmann and Casella 1998, chapter 5). For p=10 its risk at the origin is E[{1−(p−2)/U}+2U]=1.258 with U∼χ2(10), against 2 for James–Stein, and the two curves in Figure 27.5.1 merge as negative factors become rare.

27.5.5. The empirical Bayes explanation

Efron and Morris (1973) explained the paradox by drawing the means themselves from a normal distribution.

Proposition 27.5.4 (James–Stein as empirical Bayes)

Let 𝛉θ∼Np(0,ω2I) and 𝛉𝛉Z∣θ∼Np(θ,σ2I), p≥3, and put B=σ2/(σ2+ω2).

  • (a) The posterior mean is 𝛉E(θ∣Z)=(1−B)Z, and its Bayes risk is 𝛉E‖(1−B)Z−θ‖2=pσ2(1−B).

  • (b) Marginally Z∼Np(0,(σ2+ω2)I), and (p−2)σ2/‖Z‖2 is an unbiased estimator of B.

  • (c) The Bayes risk of 𝛉θ^JS is pσ2(1−B)+2σ2B.

Proof.

  • (a) 𝛉(θ,Z) is jointly normal with 𝛉Cov(θ,Z)=ω2I and Cov(Z)=(σ2+ω2)I. By Theorem 3.5.2, 𝛉θ∣Z∼Np(ω2(σ2+ω2)−1Z, σ2ω2(σ2+ω2)−1I); the mean is (1−B)Z, and the Bayes risk is the expected trace of the posterior covariance, pσ2ω2/(σ2+ω2)=pσ2(1−B).
  • (b) 𝛉𝛉Z=θ+(Z−θ) is a sum of independent normal vectors. So ‖Z‖2/(σ2+ω2)∼χ2(p), and E{(p−2)σ2/‖Z‖2}=(p−2)σ2/{(σ2+ω2)(p−2)}=B by Exercise (A1).
  • (c) Average Eq. 27.5.2 over the prior: the Bayes risk is pσ2−(p−2)2σ4E‖Z‖−2 with the marginal expectation E‖Z‖−2=1/{(σ2+ω2)(p−2)}, which gives pσ2−(p−2)σ2B=pσ2(1−B)+2σ2B.

James–Stein plugs an unbiased estimate of B, based on the spread of the p observations, into the Bayes rule, and part (c) shows that not knowing ω2 costs at most 2σ2 in total. This is the empirical Bayes idea: the ensemble of parameters supplies its own prior. Theorem 27.5.2 says more, that the gain is guaranteed even if no such prior exists.

In an orthonormal design ridge regression is the Bayes rule Z/(1+λ) with ω2=σ2/λ (Theorem 27.2.2(e)). A fixed λ is a guess at the prior variance, and a bad guess is punished without limit, as the fixed-shrinkage curve in Figure 27.5.1 shows; choosing λ from the data is the regression version of James–Stein. With a general design the g-prior of Proposition 7.6.3(c) shrinks 𝛃β^ by the common factor g/(1+g), and estimating that factor gives the next corollary.

27.5.6. Shrinking a regression with unknown variance

In regression σ2 is unknown but estimated independently of 𝛃β^, which is enough.

Corollary 27.5.5 (James–Stein for regression)

Assume the normal linear model with X of full column rank p≥3, let SSE have m degrees of freedom (so SSE/σ2∼χ2(m), independent of 𝛃β^), and for c>0 let 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃β~c=(1−cSSEβ^⊤X⊤Xβ^)β^. Then, with 𝛃Z=(X⊤X)1/2β^, 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E[(β~c−β)⊤X⊤X(β~c−β)]=pσ2−cm{2(p−2)−c(m+2)}σ4E‖Z‖−2. So 𝛃β~c dominates least squares in this loss iff 0<c<2(p−2)/(m+2), and the best constant is c=(p−2)/(m+2). In terms of the F statistic 𝛃𝛃F=(β^⊤X⊤Xβ^/p)/(SSE/m) for the hypothesis 𝛃β=0, the shrinkage factor is 1−cm/(pF).

Proof. By Theorem 7.5.1, 𝛃𝛃β^∼Np(β,σ2(X⊤X)−1) and SSE is independent of it. So 𝛉Z∼Np(θ,σ2I) with 𝛉𝛃θ=(X⊤X)1/2β, and the loss is 𝛉‖(1−a/‖Z‖2)Z−θ‖2 with a=cSSE. Conditionally on SSE, a is a constant and the computation in the proof of Theorem 27.5.2(a), with cσ2 replaced by a, gives the conditional risk pσ2−{2a(p−2)σ2−a2}E‖Z‖−2. Now E(a)=cmσ2 and E(a2)=c2σ4E{χ2(m)2}=c2m(m+2)σ4 (Theorem 4.1.2(b)). Taking expectations gives the formula, and c{2(p−2)−c(m+2)} is positive iff 0<c<2(p−2)/(m+2) and largest at the midpoint. The last statement is 𝛃𝛃SSE/(β^⊤X⊤Xβ^)=m/(pF).

The factor 1−(p−2)m/{(m+2)pF} is close to 1−1/F when p and m are large: a regression that clearly fits is barely shrunk, and one indistinguishable from noise is shrunk hard. The loss concerns fitted values, not individual coefficients. Shrinking towards the fit of a reduced model, using its F statistic, works in the same way when the full model has at least three more parameters (Exercise (C1)).

Example (James–Stein for Longley’s regression)

For Longley’s six slopes there are m=9 residual degrees of freedom and the overall F statistic is 330.3. The James–Stein factor with c=(p−2)/(m+2) is 0.99835: the regression fits far too well for shrinkage towards zero to help. The script therefore also simulates responses at Longley’s design with the least squares estimates divided by 60 as the truth, a weak signal whose noncentrality 𝛃𝛃β⊤X⊤Xβ/σ2 is about 0.6, with σ equal to Longley’s s. Over 20000 simulated data sets the ratio of the James–Stein prediction risk to that of least squares is 0.501.

import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.longley.load_pandas().data
Zr = data.drop(columns="TOTEMP").to_numpy()
y = data["TOTEMP"].to_numpy()
n, k = Zr.shape
Xc = Zr - Zr.mean(axis=0)                              # centred slopes; the intercept is ybar
yc = y - y.mean()
b = np.linalg.lstsq(Xc, yc, rcond=None)[0]
m = n - k - 1                                          # residual degrees of freedom
sse = np.sum((yc - Xc @ b) ** 2)
fit_ss = b @ Xc.T @ Xc @ b                             # b^T X^T X b, the regression sum of squares
F = (fit_ss / k) / (sse / m)
factor = 1 - (k - 2) / (m + 2) * sse / fit_ss
print(f"F = {F:.1f}; James-Stein factor {factor:.5f} = 1 - (k-2) m / ((m+2) k F)")

27.5.7. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that E{1/χ2(m)}=1/(m−2) for m≥3 and that the expectation is infinite for m≤2. Deduce that the risk of the James–Stein estimator at 𝛉θ=0 is 2σ2 for every p≥3, however large p is.

Solution. With the χ2(m) density xm/2−1e−x/2/{2m/2Γ(m/2)}, E1χ2(m)=∫0∞xm/2−2e−x/2dx2m/2Γ(m/2)=2m/2−1Γ(m/2−1)2m/2Γ(m/2)=12(m/2−1)=1m−2, where the integral converges at zero iff m/2−2>−1, that is, m>2. At 𝛉θ=0, ‖Z‖2/σ2∼χ2(p), so Eq. 27.5.2 gives pσ2−(p−2)2σ2/(p−2)=2σ2.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)
  • (a) Show that for a fixed 𝛉θ0, the estimator 𝛉𝛉𝛉θ0+(1−(p−2)σ2/‖Z−θ0‖2)(Z−θ0) has risk 𝛉pσ2−(p−2)2σ4E‖Z−θ0‖−2. (b) Lindley’s estimator shrinks towards the grand mean: θ^i=Z¯+(1−(p−3)σ2/S)(Zi−Z¯) with S=∑i(Zi−Z¯)2. Show that it dominates Z when p≥4.

Solution.

  • (a) 𝛉𝛉𝛉Z−θ0∼Np(θ−θ0,σ2I), and the estimation error is that of the James–Stein estimator of 𝛉𝛉θ−θ0 from 𝛉Z−θ0; apply Eq. 27.5.2.
  • (b) Choose an orthogonal Q whose first row is 1⊤/p. In the coordinates w=QZ, the first coordinate is pZ¯ and the remaining p−1 are a Np−1 vector with squared length S. The estimator leaves the first coordinate alone and applies James–Stein in dimension p−1≥3 to the others, so its risk is σ2 plus less than (p−1)σ2 (Theorem 27.5.2).
Exercise (B2)

Let p be large and 𝛉θ=(pσ,0,…,0)⊤. Using the approximation 𝛉‖Z‖2≈‖θ‖2+pσ2, show that the James–Stein estimate of θ1 has mean squared error of roughly pσ2/4, far above the σ2 of Z1. Why does this not contradict Theorem 27.5.2?

Solution. With ‖Z‖2≈2pσ2, the factor is about 1−(p−2)/(2p)≈12, so θ^1≈Z1/2, with bias −θ1/2 and squared bias pσ2/4. Halving the other p−1 coordinates, whose means are zero, cuts each of their risks from σ2 to about σ2/4. The total is roughly pσ2/4+(p−1)σ2/4≈pσ2/2<pσ2. One coordinate pays heavily for the others’ gains; the theorem only controls the sum.

27.3  Principal component regression

Ridge regression keeps every principal direction and shrinks each by a factor between zero and one. Principal component regression makes the factors zero or one: it keeps the directions in which the regressors vary most and discards the rest. Principal components (Hotelling 1933) are an old remedy for collinearity (Massy 1965), and Proposition 10.4.3 computed the bias and variance of regression on the leading ones in uncentred form. This section proves when it beats least squares and makes precise the warning that goes with it.

27.3.1. Definition

Keep the conventions of Section 27.2: X is n×p, centred, of full column rank, with its columns on a common scale, and X=UDV⊤ with d1≥⋯≥dp>0.

Definition 27.3.1 (Principal component regression)

The principal components of the regressors are the vectors zj=Xvj=djuj, j=1,…,p. Their sample variances are dj2/(n−1), they are mutually orthogonal, and vj is the jth loading vector. For 0≤k≤p, the principal component regression estimate with k components is 𝛃𝛃β^kPC=∑j≤kα^jvj=VkVk⊤β^,Vk=[v1,…,vk], obtained by regressing y on z1,…,zk and transforming the coefficients back.

Because the zj are orthogonal, the coefficient of zj in the regression on z1,…,zk is zj⊤y/‖zj‖2=cj/dj=α^j, the same for every k≥j; the regression on the leading components is least squares with the trailing canonical coordinates set to zero. The shrinkage factors are fj=1 for j≤k and fj=0 for j>k.

Proposition 27.3.1 (Bias and variance of principal component regression)

Let 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ, Cov(Y)=σ2I, 𝛂𝛃α=V⊤β and τj2=dj2αj2/σ2.

  • (a) 𝛃β^kPC is the least squares estimate subject to the p−k restrictions vj⊤b=0, j>k. Its fitted vector is Mky, where Mk=∑j≤kujuj⊤ is the orthogonal projection onto span(z1,…,zk).

  • (b) 𝛃𝛃Eβ^kPC−β=−∑j>kαjvj and 𝛃Cov(β^kPC)=σ2∑j≤kdj−2vjvj⊤. The total mean squared error is ∑j>kαj2+σ2∑j≤kdj−2, and the mean squared error of the fitted values is 𝛃𝛃E‖Xβ^kPC−Xβ‖2=kσ2+∑j>kdj2αj2.

  • (c) 𝛃β^kPC is at least as good as 𝛃β^ in the matrix sense iff ∑j>kτj2≤1.

  • (d) Among all estimators 𝛃β~f with every fj∈{0,1}, both mean squared errors in

    • (b) are minimized by keeping exactly the directions with τj2>1 (ties may go either way). Principal component regression with k components is this best selection iff τj2≥1 for j≤k and τj2≤1 for j>k.

Proof.

  • (a) The restrictions say that b∈span(v1,…,vk), that is, b=Vkg. Then Xb=UkDkg with Uk,Dk the leading blocks, and ‖y−UkDkg‖2 is minimized at Dkg=Uk⊤y, so g=(α^1,…,α^k)⊤ and the fit is UkUk⊤y=Mky.
  • (b) The moments are Proposition 10.4.3, and the mean squared errors are Proposition 27.1.2(a) and (b) with the factors 0 and 1.
  • (c) By Theorem 27.1.1(c) with the saving ΔA=σ2∑j>kdj−2vjvj⊤, which is nonnegative definite with column space span(vk+1,…,vp), and bias 𝛅δ=−∑j>kαjvj, which lies in that column space. The Moore–Penrose inverse is ΔA+=σ−2∑j>kdj2vjvj⊤, so 𝛅𝛅δ⊤ΔA+δ=∑j>kdj2αj2/σ2.
  • (d) The losses are sums over directions, and by Proposition 27.1.2(d) the jth term is smaller with fj=0 than with fj=1 iff τj2<1.

By part (c), discarding directions improves the estimate of every linear combination exactly when the total signal they carry, in noise units, is at most one. Part (b) shows that the two losses charge a dropped direction differently: for 𝛃β it costs αj2 and saves σ2/dj2, which is large when dj is small; for the mean it costs dj2αj2 and saves only σ2. Collinearity is a problem for coefficients, not for fitted values (Proposition 26.4.1).

27.3.2. Low-variance components need not be irrelevant

Part (d) is the crux. The right directions to drop are those with small τj2=dj2αj2/σ2; principal component regression drops those with small dj2. The two orderings agree only if the αj do not grow too fast as dj shrinks, and nothing in the model says they should: the components are computed from X alone. Jolliffe (1982) gave published examples in which low-variance components were important predictors, and Hadi and Ling (1998) showed how a component with a tiny variance can carry most of a regression (Exercise (B2)).

The signal-to-noise ratios can be estimated: under normal errors cj2/σ2−1 is unbiased for τj2, and the component t statistics tj=cj/s estimate τj (Exercise (B1)). Part (d) then suggests keeping the components with tj2>2. Such a supervised selection is not a fixed projection, and the leave-one-out and standard-error formulas do not apply to it without adjustment (Chapter 29).

Example (Principal components of Longley’s regressors)

The six standardized Longley regressors have principal components whose shares of the total variance are 76.723%, 19.589%, 3.390%, 0.249%, 0.043% and 0.006%, so the usual rules of thumb keep two or three. The component t statistics are 42.66, 5.40, 10.66, −0.55, 3.96 and 1.72 (Figure 27.3.1(a)). The fifth component, with less than a two-thousandth of the variance, is nearly as strongly related to employment as the second, while the fourth, with more variance than the fifth and sixth together, is irrelevant.

Because principal component regression fits a projection (Proposition 27.3.1(a)), the leave-one-out prediction errors follow from Theorem 20.3.1 with the leverages 1/n+∑j≤kuij2. The root mean squared leave-one-out errors for k=0,1,…,6 are 3627, 1109, 1067, 506, 606, 435 and 425, in thousands. Adding the fourth component makes predictions worse, and adding the fifth makes them much better; the best number of leading components is 6, which is least squares itself. The rule tj2>2 keeps components 1,2,3,5,6. Applied once to all sixteen years and then treated as fixed, it gives 392, but that figure ignores the selection. Redoing the selection within each fold (nested leave-one-out) gives 444, worse than least squares. Searching all 64 subsets of components picks 1,2,3,5 with 389, optimistic for the same reason. With three leading components the coefficients are 1023, 1260, −1085, −417, 1070 and 966: all six regressors are retained, with the GNP and YEAR coefficients now positive and comparable.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.longley.load_pandas().data
names = ["GNPDEFL", "GNP", "UNEMP", "ARMED", "POP", "YEAR"]
Z = data[names].to_numpy()
y = data["TOTEMP"].to_numpy()
n, p = Z.shape
X = (Z - Z.mean(axis=0)) / Z.std(axis=0, ddof=1)
yc = y - y.mean()
U, d, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
c = U.T @ yc                                   # c_j = u_j^T y, the fitted coordinate of component j

def fit_components(keep):
    """Least squares on the chosen component scores: coefficients and leave-one-out error."""
    keep = list(keep)
    beta = Vt[keep].T @ (c[keep] / d[keep])
    resid = yc - X @ beta
    h = 1 / n + np.sum(U[:, keep] ** 2, axis=1)            # leverages, intercept included
    return beta, np.mean((resid / (1 - h)) ** 2)

resid_ls = yc - U @ c
s = np.sqrt(resid_ls @ resid_ls / (n - p - 1))
t = c / s                                        # t statistic of each component
share = d ** 2 / np.sum(d ** 2)                  # share of the regressors' variance
for k in range(p + 1):
    beta_k, loo_k = fit_components(range(k))
    print(f"k = {k}  LOO = {loo_k:9.0f}   coefficients", np.round(beta_k, 0))
print("variance shares:", np.round(share, 5))
print("component t statistics:", np.round(t, 2))

27.3.3. Ridge and principal components compared

Both methods order the directions by dj alone, ridge softly and principal components hard, and neither dominates. If the trailing canonical coefficients are zero, principal components with the right k is close to the oracle, while ridge must leave variance in the trailing directions or bias the leading ones; if all the αj2 are equal, ridge with λ=σ2/αj2 is the oracle and no zero–one choice matches it (Exercise (B3)). In the simulations of Frank and Friedman (1993) ridge was generally at least as good as principal components. For Longley’s data neither method gains over least squares (leading components never do, and tuned ridge has nested leave-one-out error 441 against 425), because the fifth direction matters and both treat it as close to noise. Partial least squares, which builds its directions from y as well as X, is one response (Exercise (C1)).

27.3.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that for k<p the estimate 𝛃β^kPC generally has no zero coordinates, so that principal component regression reduces dimension without selecting variables. When does it set the coefficient of regressor j to zero?

Exercise (A2)

Show that the fitted values of principal component regression with k components, with an unpenalized intercept, are (n−111⊤+Mk)y, that this matrix is an orthogonal projection of rank k+1, and that its leverages are 1/n+∑j≤kuij2. Why does Theorem 20.3.1 apply to principal component regression but not to ridge regression?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Under the normal linear model with rank(X)=p<n−1 (centred data), show that the cj=uj⊤Y are independent N(djαj,σ2), independent of the residual sum of squares, and that tj=cj/s has a noncentral t distribution with n−p−1 degrees of freedom and noncentrality τj. Show that cj2/σ2−1 is unbiased for τj2.

Solution. U⊤Y is normal with mean 𝛃𝛃𝛂U⊤Xβ=DV⊤β=Dα and covariance σ2U⊤U=σ2I, so the cj are independent with the stated laws. They are functions of the fitted values MY=UU⊤Y, which are independent of the residual sum of squares (Theorem 7.5.1(c)); with the intercept removed by centring the residual degrees of freedom are n−p−1. Hence tj=(cj/σ)/(s/σ) is a normal with mean τj (up to sign) divided by an independent χ2(n−p−1)/(n−p−1), which is the noncentral t (Definition 4.1.3). Finally E(cj2/σ2)=Var(cj/σ)+{E(cj/σ)}2=1+τj2.

Exercise (B2)

Let x1,x2 be standardized regressors with correlation ρ=0.99 and let E(Y)=x1−x2. Find the canonical coefficients and show that α1=0. Compute τ12 and τ22 with n=100, unit-variance columns and σ=1. Which principal component regression is best, and what does a one-component principal component regression estimate?

Solution. With X⊤X=(n−1)R, the eigenvectors are v1=(1,1)⊤/2 and v2=(1,−1)⊤/2, with d12=(n−1)(1+ρ) and d22=(n−1)(1−ρ). For 𝛃β=(1,−1)⊤, 𝛃α1=v1⊤β=0 and α2=2. So τ12=0 and τ22=(n−1)(1−ρ)⋅2/σ2=99×0.01×2=1.98. By Proposition 27.3.1(d), the best zero–one selection keeps only the second component. Principal component regression with one component keeps the first, estimates α1=0 and has expected value 0: it discards the whole regression.

Exercise (B3)

Consider a design with two principal directions. (i) If α2=0≠α1, show that the oracle factors of Proposition 27.1.2(c) are (τ12/(1+τ12),0), that no ridge estimate attains them, and that principal component regression with k=1 exceeds the oracle’s total mean squared error by only (σ2/d12)/(1+τ12). (ii) If α12=α22>0, show that ridge with λ=σ2/α12 attains the oracle, and that every principal component regression is strictly worse.

Solution. Part (i): τ22=0 gives f2∗=0. Ridge has f2(λ)=d22/(d22+λ)>0 for every finite λ, so it never attains f2∗=0. Principal components with k=1 has factors (1,0). By Proposition 27.1.2(a) its first term is σ2/d12 against the oracle’s (σ2/d12)τ12/(1+τ12), and the second terms are both zero; the difference is (σ2/d12)/(1+τ12). (ii) With α12=α22=a and λ=σ2/a, ridge has factors dj2/(dj2+σ2/a)=dj2a/(dj2a+σ2)=τj2/(1+τj2), the oracle. Each term of Proposition 27.1.2(a) is a strictly convex quadratic in fj, so the oracle is its unique minimizer, and since 0<τj2/(1+τj2)<1, every choice of factors in {0,1} is strictly worse.

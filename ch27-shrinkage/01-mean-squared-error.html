27.1  The bias–variance trade-off

Chapter 26 showed where collinearity does its damage (Theorem 26.1.1). The least squares estimate remains unbiased, but along a direction v in which the regressors barely vary, the variance of 𝛃v⊤β^ is σ2 divided by a tiny eigenvalue; for a single coefficient the same damage appears as the factor 1/(1−Rj2) of Eq. 6.6.2, the variance inflation factor of Definition 26.2.1. No assumption of Parts II and III is violated. The Gauss–Markov theorem (Theorem 7.2.1) concerns the class of linear unbiased estimators, within which least squares cannot be improved, so improving on it means leaving the class. This section sets up the criterion that makes biased and unbiased estimators comparable.

27.1.1. The mean squared error matrix

Definition 27.1.1 (Mean squared error)

Let 𝛉θ~ be an estimator of 𝛉θ∈Rq with finite second moments. Its mean squared error matrix is 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉MSE(θ~)=E[(θ~−θ)(θ~−θ)⊤], its bias is 𝛅𝛉𝛉δ=E(θ~)−θ, and its total mean squared error is 𝛉𝛉𝛉trMSE(θ~)=E‖θ~−θ‖2. For estimators 𝛉𝛉θ~1,θ~2 we say that 𝛉θ~1 is at least as good in the matrix sense if 𝛉𝛉MSE(θ~2)−MSE(θ~1) is nonnegative definite.

All three quantities depend on the unknown parameter, so one estimator may be better for some parameter values and worse for others. For unbiased estimators the mean squared error matrix is the covariance matrix, and the matrix sense is the ordering in which Gauss–Markov declares least squares the winner (Theorem 7.2.1(d)).

Theorem 27.1.1 (Bias–variance decomposition for linear estimators)

  • (a) For any estimator with finite second moments, 𝛉𝛉𝛅𝛅MSE(θ~)=Cov(θ~)+δδ⊤. For every a∈Rq, 𝛉𝛉𝛉E(a⊤θ~−a⊤θ)2=a⊤MSE(θ~)a, and for every nonnegative definite W, 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉E[(θ~−θ)⊤W(θ~−θ)]=tr(WMSE(θ~)).

  • (b) Suppose 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ and Cov(Y)=σ2I, and let 𝛃β~=AY be a linear estimator of 𝛃β. Then 𝛅𝛃δ=(AX−I)β and 𝛃𝛃𝛃MSE(β~)=σ2AA⊤+(AX−I)ββ⊤(AX−I)⊤.

  • (c) In the setting of (b), let X have full column rank, let 𝛃β^ be least squares, and put ΔA=σ2(X⊤X)−1−σ2AA⊤, the covariance that 𝛃β~ saves. The following are equivalent:

    • (i) 𝛃β~ is at least as good as 𝛃β^ in the matrix sense;

    • (ii) 𝛃𝛃𝛃E(a⊤β~−a⊤β)2≤Var(a⊤β^) for every a;

    • (iii) 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E[(β~−β)⊤W(β~−β)]≤E[(β^−β)⊤W(β^−β)] for every nonnegative definite W;

    • (iv) ΔA is nonnegative definite, 𝛅δ∈C(ΔA) and 𝛅𝛅δ⊤ΔA+δ≤1.

    When ΔA is positive definite, (iv) reads 𝛅𝛅δ⊤ΔA−1δ≤1.

Proof.

  • (a) Write 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛅θ~−θ=(θ~−Eθ~)+δ. The cross terms have expectation zero, which gives the decomposition. The scalar identities follow from E(a⊤w)2=a⊤E(ww⊤)a and w⊤Ww=tr(Www⊤) with 𝛉𝛉w=θ~−θ.
  • (b) 𝛃E(AY)=AXβ and Cov(AY)=σ2AA⊤ (Theorem 2.2.1); apply (a).
  • (c) By (a), 𝛃𝛃𝛅𝛅MSE(β^)−MSE(β~)=ΔA−δδ⊤, and (i) says this matrix is nonnegative definite. By (a), (ii) says 𝛅𝛅a⊤(ΔA−δδ⊤)a≥0 for all a, which is (i). Next, (i) implies (iii) because tr(WN)≥0 when W and N are both nonnegative definite: with W=∑kωkqkqk⊤ (Theorem 1.7.2), tr(WN)=∑kωkqk⊤Nqk≥0. And (iii) with W=aa⊤ is (ii). For (i)⇔(iv), put 𝛅𝛅N=ΔA−δδ⊤. If N is nonnegative definite, so is 𝛅𝛅ΔA=N+δδ⊤. If 𝛅δ∉C(ΔA), there is w∈N(ΔA) with 𝛅w⊤δ≠0 (Proposition 1.5.1), and then 𝛅w⊤Nw=−(w⊤δ)2<0. So (i) forces 𝛅δ=ΔAc for some c, and c⊤Nc=c⊤ΔAc(1−c⊤ΔAc)≥0 gives 𝛅𝛅δ⊤ΔA+δ=c⊤ΔAc≤1 (when c⊤ΔAc=0 there is nothing to prove). Here 𝛅𝛅δ⊤ΔA+δ=c⊤ΔAΔA+ΔAc=c⊤ΔAc by the first Penrose condition. Conversely, under (iv) the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the semi-inner product (w,z)↦w⊤ΔAz gives, for every w, 𝛅(w⊤δ)2=(w⊤ΔAc)2≤(w⊤ΔAw)(c⊤ΔAc)≤w⊤ΔAw, so w⊤Nw≥0. When ΔA is invertible, ΔA+=ΔA−1; this case is also Lemma 19.1.4.

Part (c) makes the trade-off precise: a biased linear estimator is better in every direction and under every quadratic loss exactly when its squared bias, measured in the metric of the covariance it saves, is at most one. Since 𝛅𝛃δ=(AX−I)β, condition (iv) holds when 𝛃β is close to what the estimator shrinks towards, and fails far from it. No linear estimator with AX≠I wins everywhere (Exercise (B1)). Theorem 19.1.5 was the first instance: the short regression is a biased linear estimator, and its winning region γ≤1 is condition (iv) for that choice of A.

27.1.2. What unbiasedness buys, and what it costs

Unbiasedness concerns an estimator’s centre, not its accuracy, and it does not survive transformation: 𝛃‖β^‖2 overestimates 𝛃‖β‖2 by σ2tr(X⊤X)−1 on average (Exercise (B3)), which under collinearity is enormous. Least squares estimates are systematically too long, and shrinking them is a correction, not a distortion. When many parameters are estimated at once, small biases spread over many coordinates can buy a large cut in total variance; Section 27.5 turns this into a theorem. The cost is in inference: the bias depends on the unknown 𝛃β, so an interval “estimate ± multiple of standard error” centred on a shrinkage estimate has no guaranteed coverage. Chapter 28 and Chapter 29 return to inference after shrinkage and selection.

27.1.3. Shrinkage in canonical coordinates

The collinearity problem has a natural coordinate system. Let X have full column rank and let X=UDV⊤ be its thin singular value decomposition (Theorem 1.8.1), with U=[u1,…,up] and V=[v1,…,vp] having orthonormal columns and D=diag(d1,…,dp), d1≥⋯≥dp>0. We write dj instead of the σj of Chapter 1 because σ is the error standard deviation. Put

𝛂𝛃𝛂𝛃α=V⊤β,α^=V⊤β^=D−1U⊤Y,cj=uj⊤Y=djα^j.
(27.1.1)

The αj are the canonical coefficients: the coordinates of 𝛃β along the eigenvectors vj of X⊤X, whose eigenvalues are dj2. By Theorem 10.4.1(d), the α^j are uncorrelated with E(α^j)=αj and Var(α^j)=σ2/dj2. A small dj is a direction vj along which the regressors hardly move, and along which the data therefore say little (Proposition 19.6.1).

Many shrinkage estimators act on the canonical coordinates one at a time. For numbers f1,…,fp, called shrinkage factors or filter factors, define 𝛃𝛂β~f=∑j=1pfjα^jvj=VFα^,F=diag(f1,…,fp). Least squares has every fj=1. Principal component regression (Section 27.3) has fj∈{0,1}, and ridge regression (Section 27.2) has fj=dj2/(dj2+λ). The signal-to-noise ratio of direction j is τj2=αj2Var(α^j)=dj2αj2σ2, the squared mean of the standardized coordinate α^j/Var(α^j). Under normal errors it is the noncentrality of cj2/σ2∼χ2(1,τj2).

Proposition 27.1.2 (Shrinking along the principal directions)

Let 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ, Cov(Y)=σ2I and rank(X)=p. For fixed factors fj:

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃E‖β~f−β‖2=∑j{fj2σ2/dj2+(1−fj)2αj2};

  • (b) 𝛃𝛃E‖Xβ~f−Xβ‖2=∑j{fj2σ2+(1−fj)2dj2αj2};

  • (c) both are minimized by the same factors fj∗=τj2/(1+τj2), and the minima are ∑j(σ2/dj2)τj2/(1+τj2) and σ2∑jτj2/(1+τj2);

  • (d) replacing fj=1 by some fj∈[0,1), with the other factors fixed, lowers both quantities iff τj2<(1+fj)/(1−fj). In particular dropping direction j (fj=0) helps iff τj2<1.

Proof. 𝛃𝛃𝛂𝛂β~f−β=V(Fα^−α) and V is orthogonal, so 𝛃𝛃‖β~f−β‖2=∑j(fjα^j−αj)2. Each term has expectation Var(fjα^j)+(fjαj−αj)2=fj2σ2/dj2+(1−fj)2αj2, which is (a). For (b), 𝛃𝛃𝛂𝛂X(β~f−β)=UD(Fα^−α) and U has orthonormal columns, so each term of (a) is multiplied by dj2. (c) Each term of (a) is a convex quadratic in fj with derivative 2fjσ2/dj2−2(1−fj)αj2, which vanishes at fj=αj2/(αj2+σ2/dj2)=τj2/(1+τj2). Terms of (b) are those of (a) times dj2, so they have the same minimizer; substituting gives the minima. (d) The jth term of (a) falls below its value σ2/dj2 at fj=1 iff (1−fj)2αj2<(1−fj2)σ2/dj2. Dividing by (1−fj)σ2/dj2>0 gives (1−fj)τj2<1+fj. The same holds for (b).

The ideal shrinkage depends on τj2, which combines the spread of the regressors in direction j and the size of the coefficient there, so a direction with tiny dj but large αj should be kept. The ideal factors are the same for estimating 𝛃β and 𝛃Xβ. The oracle factors depend on 𝛃β and σ2; each method of this chapter chooses factors from the data instead, ridge by one tuning constant, principal components by a cut-off, and James–Stein by an unbiased estimate of the risk. Part (d) with fj=0 is the criterion of Proposition 10.4.3.

Example (Two nearly collinear regressors)

Take n=50 observations of two centred regressors, scaled so that X⊤X=nR where R is a correlation matrix with off-diagonal entry 0.95. Let σ=1 and 𝛃β=(1,0.5)⊤. The eigenvalues of X⊤X are d12=n(1+0.95)=97.5 and d22=n(1−0.95)=2.5, with eigenvectors (1,1)⊤/2 and (1,−1)⊤/2. The canonical coefficients have sizes |α1|=1.0607 and |α2|=0.3536, and the signal-to-noise ratios are τ12=109.7 and τ22=0.3125. The first direction, the sum of the two regressors, is estimated precisely. The second, their difference, is not.

Estimator Factors (f1,f2) Total MSE
least squares (1,1) 0.4103
drop the second direction (1,0) 0.1353
halve the second direction (1,0.5) 0.1415
oracle fj∗ (0.991,0.238) 0.1054

Dropping the difference direction cuts the total mean squared error by two thirds, because τ22<1, and wins in the matrix sense too. Here ΔA=σ2v2v2⊤/d22 is singular, the bias is 𝛅δ=−α2v2∈C(ΔA), and 𝛅𝛅δ⊤ΔA+δ=α22d22/σ2=τ22=0.3125≤1. So by Theorem 27.1.1(c), every linear combination 𝛃a⊤β is estimated at least as accurately. Had the truth been 𝛃β=(1,−0.5)⊤, with the same length but pointing along the difference, then τ22=2.5×1.125=2.81 and dropping would have increased the total error.

import numpy as np
n, rho, sigma = 50, 0.95, 1.0
XtX = n * np.array([[1.0, rho], [rho, 1.0]])
beta = np.array([1.0, 0.5])

evals, V = np.linalg.eigh(XtX)
order = np.argsort(evals)[::-1]
d2, V = evals[order], V[:, order]          # d_1^2 >= d_2^2, eigenvectors v_1, v_2
alpha = V.T @ beta                         # canonical coefficients
tau2 = d2 * alpha ** 2 / sigma ** 2        # squared signal-to-noise ratio of each direction

def mse(f):
    """Total MSE of the estimator that multiplies alpha_hat_j by f_j."""
    f = np.asarray(f, dtype=float)
    return np.sum(f ** 2 * sigma ** 2 / d2 + (1 - f) ** 2 * alpha ** 2)

f_oracle = tau2 / (1 + tau2)
for label, f in [("least squares", [1, 1]), ("drop v_2", [1, 0]),
                 ("halve v_2", [1, 0.5]), ("oracle", f_oracle)]:
    print(f"{label:14s} f = {np.round(f, 3)}   MSE = {mse(f):.4f}")
print("tau^2 =", np.round(tau2, 3))

27.1.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let Y¯ be the mean of n independent observations with mean μ and variance σ2. For a constant c, find the mean squared error of cY¯ as an estimator of μ. Show that it is minimized at c∗=μ2/(μ2+σ2/n) and that cY¯ beats Y¯ iff (1−c)μ2<(1+c)σ2/n. Why is c∗ not an estimator?

Exercise (A2)

In Example (Two nearly collinear regressors), verify the least squares total mean squared error σ2(1/d12+1/d22)=0.4103 by hand, and check the entry for “drop the second direction” using Proposition 27.1.2(a).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let X have full column rank and let 𝛃β~=AY with AX≠I. Show that there is a 𝛃β for which 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E‖β~−β‖2>E‖β^−β‖2. So no biased linear estimator dominates least squares.

Solution. By Theorem 27.1.1(b), 𝛃𝛃𝛃E‖β~−β‖2=σ2tr(AA⊤)+‖(AX−I)β‖2. The first term does not depend on 𝛃β. Since AX−I≠0 there is a unit vector v with (AX−I)v≠0. Along 𝛃β=tv the second term is t2‖(AX−I)v‖2, which exceeds the constant σ2tr(X⊤X)−1 for large t.

Exercise (B2)

A linear smoother estimates the mean vector 𝛍μ=E(Y) by SY for a fixed n×n matrix S. Show that 𝛍𝛍E‖SY−μ‖2=σ2tr(SS⊤)+‖(I−S)μ‖2 when Cov(Y)=σ2I. For the projection S=M and 𝛍μ∈C(X), recover σ2r with r=rank(X).

Solution. 𝛍𝛍𝛍SY−μ=S(Y−μ)−(I−S)μ. The first term has mean zero and covariance σ2SS⊤, so its expected squared length is σ2tr(SS⊤) (Theorem 2.3.1). The second term is constant, and the cross term has mean zero. For S=M, tr(MM⊤)=trM=r and 𝛍(I−M)μ=0.

Exercise (B3)

Show that 𝛃𝛃E‖β^‖2=‖β‖2+σ2∑jdj−2. For which c does 𝛃cβ^ have smaller total mean squared error than 𝛃β^? Express the answer through 𝛃‖β‖2 and σ2tr(X⊤X)−1.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Least squares is equivariant under rescaling: replacing X by XG−1, with G diagonal and positive, turns the estimate into 𝛃Gβ^. By rescaling one column in Example (Two nearly collinear regressors) by 10, show that 𝛃β~f with f1=1, f2=0 is not equivariant. Why are shrinkage estimators usually applied to standardized regressors?

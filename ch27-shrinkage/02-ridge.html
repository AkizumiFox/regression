27.2  Ridge regression

The oldest remedy for collinearity is also the simplest: when X⊤X has eigenvalues close to zero, add a positive constant to its diagonal before inverting. Hoerl and Kennard (1970) proposed this in statistics; numerical analysts knew it as Tikhonov regularization. The constant bounds the variance at the price of bias towards the origin. Exercise (B1) and Exercise (C1) met the estimator briefly.

27.2.1. Centring, scaling and the intercept

A penalty should not touch the intercept, or shifting the response by a constant would change the fitted slopes. Leaving the intercept unpenalized is equivalent to centring, for every penalty.

Proposition 27.2.1 (An unpenalized intercept)

Let X0 be an n×p matrix of regressors with column means x¯0, let X=X0−1x¯0⊤ and y~=y−y¯1 be the centred regressors and response, and let P:Rp→[0,∞] be any function. Then (β0,b) minimizes ‖y−β01−X0b‖2+P(b) iff b minimizes ‖y~−Xb‖2+P(b) and β0=y¯−x¯0⊤b.

Proof. For fixed b, the first term is the residual sum of squares of regressing y−X0b on 1, minimized uniquely by the mean β0=y¯−x¯0⊤b, with minimum ‖(I−n−111⊤)(y−X0b)‖2=‖y~−Xb‖2. So the joint minimum is the minimum over b of ‖y~−Xb‖2+P(b), attained exactly at the pairs described.

From now on the regressors are centred and the response is centred, so X has no intercept column and 1⊤X=0⊤. A penalty also treats all coefficients as commensurable, so the regressors should be on a common scale (Exercise (C1)). In this chapter’s examples every column has unit standard deviation, so X⊤X=(n−1)R with R the correlation matrix, and a coefficient is the change in mean response per standard deviation of its regressor.

27.2.2. The estimator

Definition 27.2.1 (Ridge regression)

For λ≥0, a ridge estimate 𝛃β^λR minimizes

‖y−Xb‖2+λ‖b‖2.
(27.2.1)

The constant λ is the ridge parameter (or tuning parameter). The ridge trace is the curve 𝛃λ↦β^λR.

For λ=0 this is least squares. For λ>0 it is uniquely defined whatever the rank of X, and its behaviour is transparent in the coordinates of Eq. 27.1.1. Let X=∑j≤rdjujvj⊤ be the SVD of X, of rank r, and cj=uj⊤y.

Theorem 27.2.2 (Ridge regression)

Let λ>0.

  • (a) (Closed form.) The criterion Eq. 27.2.1 has the unique minimizer 𝛃β^λR=(X⊤X+λI)−1X⊤y=∑j≤rdjdj2+λcjvj. If r=p, then 𝛃β^λR=∑jfj(λ)α^jvj with shrinkage factors fj(λ)=dj2/(dj2+λ)∈(0,1), decreasing in λ and smallest for the smallest dj.

  • (b) (Fitted values.) 𝛃Xβ^λR=Hλy with Hλ=X(X⊤X+λI)−1X⊤=∑j≤rfj(λ)ujuj⊤. The effective degrees of freedom df(λ)=trHλ=∑j≤rfj(λ) decrease strictly from r (as λ→0) to 0 (as λ→∞).

  • (c) (Moments.) If 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ, Cov(Y)=σ2I and r=p, then 𝛃𝛃𝛃Eβ^λR−β=−λ(X⊤X+λI)−1β, 𝛃Cov(β^λR)=σ2∑jdj2(dj2+λ)2vjvj⊤, and, with 𝛂𝛃α=V⊤β, the total mean squared error is

    𝛃𝛃E‖β^λR−β‖2=σ2∑jdj2(dj2+λ)2+λ2∑jαj2(dj2+λ)2.
    (27.2.2)

  • (d) (The path.) Unless X⊤y=0, 𝛃‖β^λR‖ decreases strictly in λ, with limit 0 as λ→∞; and 𝛃β^λR→X+y, the least squares estimate of smallest norm, as λ→0.

  • (e) (Bayesian reading.) If 𝛃𝛃Y∣β∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I) with σ2 known and 𝛃β∼Np(0,(σ2/λ)I), then 𝛃𝛃β∣Y∼Np(β^λR,σ2(X⊤X+λI)−1). With σ2 unknown and the normal–inverse-gamma prior of Theorem 7.6.2 with m0=0 and V0=λ−1I, the posterior mean of 𝛃β is again 𝛃β^λR.

Proof.

  • (a) Uniqueness and the first formula are Exercise (A2): X⊤X+λI is positive definite, and Proposition 1.11.2 applies. Complete v1,…,vr to an orthonormal basis v1,…,vp of Rp. Then X⊤X+λI=∑j≤r(dj2+λ)vjvj⊤+∑j>rλvjvj⊤, whose inverse has the same eigenvectors and reciprocal eigenvalues (Theorem 1.7.2). Since X⊤y=∑j≤rdjcjvj, the second formula follows. When r=p, djcj/(dj2+λ)=fj(λ)cj/dj=fj(λ)α^j.
  • (b) Xvj=djuj, so 𝛃Xβ^λR=∑j≤rfj(λ)cjuj=∑jfj(λ)ujuj⊤y. The trace of ujuj⊤ is one. Each fj decreases strictly from 1 to 0.
  • (c) These are the formulas of Exercise (B1) in the present notation (its dj and 𝛄γ are our dj2 and 𝛂α); they are also Proposition 27.1.2(a) with the factors fj(λ).
  • (d) 𝛃‖β^λR‖2=∑j≤rdj2cj2/(dj2+λ)2. Every term is nonincreasing in λ and tends to zero, and a term with cj≠0 decreases strictly. Some cj≠0 iff X⊤y≠0. As λ→0, dj/(dj2+λ)→1/dj, and ∑j≤r(cj/dj)vj=X+y by Theorem 10.4.1(b).
  • (e) With σ2 known, the posterior density is proportional to 𝛃𝛃exp⁡{−(‖y−Xβ‖2+λ‖β‖2)/(2σ2)}. Completing the square as in the proof of Theorem 7.6.2, with P0=λI and m0=0, 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃‖y−Xβ‖2+λ‖β‖2=(β−β^λR)⊤(X⊤X+λI)(β−β^λR)+C, with C free of 𝛃β. This is the stated normal density. The last statement is Proposition 7.6.3(d).

Part (a) says what ridge does. It keeps the directions vj and shrinks the coordinate along each by fj(λ): directions with dj2≫λ are hardly touched, and those with dj2≪λ are shrunk almost to zero, whatever the size of the coefficients. Comparing with the oracle factors fj∗=dj2/(dj2+σ2/αj2) of Proposition 27.1.2(c), ridge is the oracle when all canonical coefficients have the same size, αj2=σ2/λ. Part (e) says the same in Bayesian terms: the prior N(0,(σ2/λ)I) treats every direction alike.

The effective degrees of freedom in (b) count each dimension by the fraction fj(λ) in which it is used; Chapter 29 uses them in model-selection criteria, and Exercise (C1) gives them a second meaning.

Remark (Penalized and constrained forms). For any penalty P≥0, a minimizer b^ of ‖y−Xb‖2+λP(b), with λ>0, also minimizes ‖y−Xb‖2 subject to P(b)≤t, where t=P(b^). Indeed, if P(b)≤t, then ‖y−Xb‖2≥‖y−Xb‖2+λ{P(b)−t}≥‖y−Xb^‖2+λ{P(b^)−t}=‖y−Xb^‖2. So ridge is least squares restricted to a ball, ‖b‖2≤t, whose radius is fixed by λ (Exercise (B3)). The lasso of Section 27.4 replaces the ball by a cross-polytope.

27.2.3. Ridge beats least squares

Exercise (B1) showed that the total mean squared error of ridge falls as λ leaves 0. Hoerl and Kennard made the range explicit, and Theobald (1974) added the matrix sense.

Theorem 27.2.3 (Hoerl–Kennard)

Let X have full column rank, 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ, Cov(Y)=σ2I with σ2>0, and let αmax2=maxjαj2 with 𝛂𝛃α=V⊤β. Let m(λ) be the total mean squared error Eq. 27.2.2, so that m(0)=σ2∑jdj−2 is that of least squares.

  • (a) m(λ)<m(0) for every 0<λ<2σ2/αmax2, and in particular for every 𝛃0<λ<2σ2/‖β‖2. If 𝛃β=0, for every λ>0.

  • (b) m is strictly decreasing on [0,σ2/αmax2].

  • (c) 𝛃β^λR is at least as good as 𝛃β^ in the matrix sense iff λσ2∑jdj2αj22dj2+λ≤1, which holds whenever 𝛃0<λ≤2σ2/‖β‖2.

Proof.

  • (a) Compare term by term. The jth term of Eq. 27.2.2 is below its value σ2/dj2 at λ=0 iff dj2(σ2dj2+λ2αj2)<σ2(dj2+λ)2, that is, iff λ2dj2αj2<2λσ2dj2+λ2σ2, or, dividing by λ>0, λ(dj2αj2−σ2)<2σ2dj2. If dj2αj2≤σ2, this holds for every λ>0. Otherwise it holds for λ<2σ2dj2/(dj2αj2−σ2), and the right side exceeds 2σ2dj2/(dj2αj2)=2σ2/αj2≥2σ2/αmax2. So for 0<λ<2σ2/αmax2 every term decreases, and so does the sum. Finally 𝛂𝛃αmax2≤‖α‖2=‖β‖2.
  • (b) By Exercise (B1), in the present notation, m′(λ)=2∑jdj2(λαj2−σ2)/(dj2+λ)3. For 0≤λ<σ2/αmax2 every numerator is negative, so m′<0 there, and m is strictly decreasing on the closed interval.
  • (c) Write S=X⊤X and A=(S+λI)−1X⊤. In the notation of Theorem 27.1.1(c), all matrices involved have the eigenvectors vj, with eigenvalues ΔA: σ2(1dj2−dj2(dj2+λ)2)=σ2λ(2dj2+λ)dj2(dj2+λ)2>0, and the bias is 𝛅δ=−∑jλαj(dj2+λ)−1vj. So ΔA is positive definite and 𝛅𝛅δ⊤ΔA−1δ=∑jλ2αj2(dj2+λ)−2⋅dj2(dj2+λ)2/{σ2λ(2dj2+λ)}, which is the stated sum. Each factor dj2/(2dj2+λ) is below 1/2, so the sum is at most 𝛂𝛃λ‖α‖2/(2σ2)=λ‖β‖2/(2σ2). Apply Theorem 27.1.1(c).

The theorem is an existence result. The good range of λ depends on the unknown 𝛃β and σ2, and it says nothing about a λ chosen from the data. Replacing 𝛃β by 𝛃β^ in the bound is misleading, because 𝛃‖β^‖2 overestimates 𝛃‖β‖2 by σ2∑jdj−2 on average (Exercise (B3)), which is large exactly when ridge is needed. What the theorem does establish is that least squares is never the best member of the ridge family.

27.2.4. Choosing the ridge parameter

Hoerl and Kennard suggested choosing the smallest λ at which the ridge trace “stabilizes”, a subjective judgement. Hoerl, Kennard and Baldwin (1975) proposed the plug-in 𝛃λ=pσ^2/‖β^‖2, the optimum of Exercise (A2) with estimates substituted; since 𝛃‖β^‖2 is too large on average, it tends to shrink too little under collinearity. A more defensible choice minimizes an estimate of prediction error. Because ridge is a linear smoother, y^=Hλy, the leave-one-out prediction errors need only one fit: the residual of case i divided by 1−hii(λ), with hii(λ) the ith diagonal entry of Hλ (Exercise (B1); add 1/n for an unpenalized intercept), the ridge version of Theorem 20.3.1. Generalized cross-validation replaces each hii(λ) by the average trHλ/n: GCV(λ)=n−1‖y−Hλy‖2(1−trHλ/n)2 (with an unpenalized intercept, trHλ becomes 1+df(λ)). Chapter 29 develops both criteria for general linear smoothers (Theorem 29.3.2 and Definition 29.3.2). A Bayesian alternative estimates the prior variance σ2/λ from the marginal likelihood, an empirical Bayes idea that returns in Section 27.5.

Example (Ridge regression for Longley’s data)

Longley’s regression (Example (Longley’s regression)) relates total employment, in thousands, to six economic series over n=16 years. With the regressors standardized, the eigenvalues dj2 of X⊤X run from 69.05 down to 0.0057. The least squares coefficients, in thousands of persons per standard deviation, are 163 for the GNP deflator, −3560 for GNP, −1888 for unemployment, −719 for the armed forces, −355 for population and 8709 for the year. The GNP and YEAR coefficients are large and of opposite sign: the two series rose together, and the data can hardly separate them.

Figure 27.2.1(a) shows the ridge trace against the effective degrees of freedom. As λ grows, the GNP and YEAR coefficients approach each other, and the GNP coefficient changes sign at λ=0.0110, where df=5.1. Leave-one-out cross-validation chooses λ=0.0026, with df=5.61, and GCV chooses λ=0.0030, with df=5.56. Both prefer little shrinkage. At the leave-one-out choice the coefficients are −6, −1809, −1645, −669, −825 and 7415. The largest changes are in the coefficients of GNP, YEAR and population, the three series that rose together, and the deflator coefficient is essentially zero. The minimized root mean squared leave-one-out error is 402, against 425 for least squares, but this minimum is optimistic, since the same errors chose λ. Choosing λ afresh within each leave-one-out fold (nested cross-validation) gives an honest 441: on these sixteen years tuned ridge does not predict better than least squares.

Panel (b) asks what the theorem guarantees in a world where the truth is the least squares fit, 𝛃𝛃β=β^, with σ=s=304.9. There, least squares has total mean squared error 19.33 million, and the best ridge estimate, at λ=0.0019 with df=5.69, has 15.01 million, a ratio of 0.777. Theorem 27.2.3(a) guarantees an improvement only for λ<0.00383, and part (c) gives dominance in the matrix sense for λ≤0.00200; in fact the improvement continues up to λ=0.0058. The ranges are narrow because this “truth” inherits the least squares estimate’s large components along the weak directions.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.longley.load_pandas().data
names = ["GNPDEFL", "GNP", "UNEMP", "ARMED", "POP", "YEAR"]
Z = data[names].to_numpy()
y = data["TOTEMP"].to_numpy()
n, p = Z.shape

X = (Z - Z.mean(axis=0)) / Z.std(axis=0, ddof=1)     # centred, unit standard deviation
yc = y - y.mean()                                     # centred response (the intercept is ybar)

U, d, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)      # X = U diag(d) V^T
V = Vt.T
c = U.T @ yc                                          # coordinates of y along u_1, ..., u_p

def ridge(lam):
    """Ridge coefficients for the centred problem, through the SVD."""
    return V @ (d / (d ** 2 + lam) * c)

def edf(lam):
    """Effective degrees of freedom of the slopes: sum of the shrinkage factors."""
    return np.sum(d ** 2 / (d ** 2 + lam))

beta_ls = V @ (c / d)
print("singular values squared:", np.round(d ** 2, 4))
print("least squares:", np.round(beta_ls, 1))
for lam in [0.01, 0.1, 1.0]:
    print(f"lambda = {lam:5.2f}  edf = {edf(lam):.2f}  ridge =", np.round(ridge(lam), 1))

The second cell computes the leverages of the ridge smoother, the leave-one-out and GCV criteria, and the two choices of λ.

def hat_diag(lam):
    """Leverages of the ridge smoother with an unpenalized intercept."""
    f = d ** 2 / (d ** 2 + lam)
    return 1 / n + np.sum(U ** 2 * f, axis=1)

def loo_and_gcv(lam):
    resid = yc - X @ ridge(lam)
    h = hat_diag(lam)
    loo = np.mean((resid / (1 - h)) ** 2)                  # leave-one-out shortcut
    gcv = np.mean(resid ** 2) / (1 - (1 + edf(lam)) / n) ** 2
    return loo, gcv

grid = np.logspace(-4, 2, 601)
scores = np.array([loo_and_gcv(l) for l in grid])
lam_loo = grid[np.argmin(scores[:, 0])]
lam_gcv = grid[np.argmin(scores[:, 1])]
print(f"LOO chooses lambda = {lam_loo:.4f} (edf {edf(lam_loo):.2f})")
print(f"GCV chooses lambda = {lam_gcv:.4f} (edf {edf(lam_gcv):.2f})")
print("ridge at the LOO choice:", np.round(ridge(lam_loo), 1))

The script also checks the closed form against a direct solve, augmented least squares (Exercise (A1)) and the posterior mean of Theorem 7.6.2, and the leave-one-out shortcut against sixteen refits.

27.2.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that 𝛃β^λR is the least squares estimate for the augmented data y~=(y⊤,0⊤)⊤ and X~=(X⊤,λI)⊤. Show that κ(X~)2=(d12+λ)/(dp2+λ), so ridge can be computed stably by QR (Chapter 10), and that the augmented problem has “residual sum of squares” 𝛃𝛃‖y−Xβ^λR‖2+λ‖β^λR‖2.

Solution. ‖y~−X~b‖2=‖y−Xb‖2+‖0−λb‖2, which is Eq. 27.2.1, so the minimizers coincide and the minimum is the stated sum. X~⊤X~=X⊤X+λI has eigenvalues dj2+λ, and the singular values of X~ are their square roots (Theorem 1.8.1). The condition number is the ratio of the largest to the smallest (Definition 1.8.1).

Exercise (A2)

Suppose X⊤X=I. Show that 𝛃𝛃β^λR=β^/(1+λ), df(λ)=p/(1+λ), and that the value of λ minimizing the total mean squared error is 𝛃pσ2/‖β‖2.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let 𝛃β^λ,(i)R be the ridge estimate computed without case i (no intercept), and hii the ith diagonal entry of Hλ. Show that 𝛃𝛃yi−x(i)⊤β^λ,(i)R=yi−x(i)⊤β^λR1−hii.

Solution. Put K=X⊤X+λI and x=x(i), so hii=x⊤K−1x<1. Without case i the matrix is K−xx⊤ and the right side is X⊤y−xyi. By Eq. 1.6.7, (K−xx⊤)−1=K−1+K−1xx⊤K−1/(1−hii). Hence 𝛃𝛃x⊤β^λ,(i)R=(x⊤K−1+hiix⊤K−11−hii)(X⊤y−xyi)=x⊤β^λR−hiiyi1−hii, and yi minus this is 𝛃(yi−x⊤β^λR)/(1−hii). The argument is that of Theorem 20.3.1 with X⊤X replaced by K; only the rank-one update is used.

Exercise (B2)

Generalized ridge regression uses a separate constant for each principal direction: 𝛃β^k=(X⊤X+VKV⊤)−1X⊤y with K=diag(k1,…,kp), kj≥0. Show that its shrinkage factors are dj2/(dj2+kj), and that the choice kj=σ2/αj2 gives the oracle factors of Proposition 27.1.2(c).

Exercise (B3)

Show that for every t with 𝛃0<t<‖β^‖2 (full column rank) there is exactly one λ>0 with 𝛃‖β^λR‖2=t, and that 𝛃β^λR is then the unique minimizer of ‖y−Xb‖2 subject to ‖b‖2≤t.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

For any estimator 𝛍μ^=SY of 𝛍μ=E(Y) with Cov(Y)=σ2I, show that σ−2∑iCov(μ^i,Yi)=trS. Deduce that df(λ) measures how strongly the ridge fit follows its own data, and that 𝛍E‖Y−HλY‖2=E‖HλY−μ‖2+nσ2−2σ2df(λ).

Exercise (C2)

Let p>n. Show that 𝛃β^λR=X⊤(XX⊤+λIn)−1y, which needs only an n×n inverse, and that 𝛃β^λR∈C(X⊤). What is the limit as λ→0? Chapter 28 studies this limit.

Solution. The identity X⊤(XX⊤+λIn)=(X⊤X+λIp)X⊤ gives (X⊤X+λIp)−1X⊤=X⊤(XX⊤+λIn)−1 after multiplying by the two inverses. The right side is in C(X⊤). By Theorem 27.2.2(d) the limit is X+y, the least squares solution of smallest norm, which interpolates the data when rank(X)=n.

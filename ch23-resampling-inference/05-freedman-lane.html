23.5  Freedman–Lane and related schemes

With a continuous nuisance regressor in Eq. 23.4.2, no permutation of the responses leaves the null distribution unchanged. Several schemes permute something else, or permute the responses and hope. None is exact in general, but their differences can be understood with the projection algebra of Chapter 6.

23.5.1. Five schemes

Keep the notation of Eq. 23.4.2: Z is n×p0 with 1∈C(Z), X1 is n×q, the full model matrix [Z,X1] has rank p=p0+q<n, M0 projects onto C(Z) and M onto C([Z,X1]). The statistic for 𝛃H0:β1=0 is the F statistic of Theorem 11.1.1, written as a function of the response vector:

F(u)=‖(M−M0)u‖2/q‖(I−M)u‖2/(n−p).
(23.5.1)

Since (M−M0)Z=0 and (I−M)Z=0, F(u+Zg)=F(u) for every g. Let 𝛆𝛆ε^0=(I−M0)y,ε^=(I−M)y be the residuals of the reduced and the full model. By Theorem 6.6.2, M−M0 projects onto C(X~1) with X~1=(I−M0)X1.

Definition 23.5.1 (Permutation schemes for a partial hypothesis)

For a permutation matrix P, each scheme computes a permuted statistic; its p-value is Eq. 23.4.1 with T(y) the observed F(y) and T(Pby) replaced by the scheme’s permuted statistic.

  • (a) Raw permutation (Manly 1997): F(Py), refitting the full model to permuted responses.

  • (b) Permuting the regressors of interest (Draper and Stoneman 1966): the F statistic computed from y with model matrix [Z,PX1].

  • (c) Kennedy’s scheme (Kennedy 1995): regress 𝛆Pε^0 on X~1 alone and compute 𝛆FK(Pε^0), where FK(u)=‖(M−M0)u‖2/q(‖u‖2−‖(M−M0)u‖2)/(n−p).

  • (d) Freedman–Lane (Freedman and Lane 1983): form 𝛆y∗=M0y+Pε^0, the reduced-model fit plus permuted reduced-model residuals, and compute F(y∗).

  • (e) ter Braak’s scheme (ter Braak 1992): form 𝛆y∗=My+Pε^, the full-model fit plus permuted full-model residuals, and compute the F statistic for the hypothesis 𝛃𝛃β1=β^1, which is true in the permuted world.

23.5.2. What can be proved

Proposition 23.5.1 (The Freedman–Lane scheme)

In the setting above:

  • (a) 𝛆𝛆F(M0y+Pε^0)=F(Pε^0) for every P, and 𝛆F(y)=F(ε^0). ter Braak’s permuted statistic equals 𝛆F(Pε^).

  • (b) Under H0, 𝛆𝛆ε^0=(I−M0)ε. The p-values of the Freedman–Lane, ter Braak, Kennedy and Draper–Stoneman schemes depend on y only through (I−M0)y, so under H0 their distributions do not depend on 𝛄γ. The raw permutation statistic involves 𝛄γ.

  • (c) Under H0, if PZ=Z for every P in a group G and 𝛆ε is G-invariant in distribution, then the Freedman–Lane test over G is exact, and it coincides with the raw permutation test over G.

  • (d) Suppose the errors are independent draws from one law with mean zero and variance σ2>0, P is uniform over all permutations and independent of 𝛆ε, and p is fixed as n→∞. Then under H0, 𝛆𝛆F(Pε^0)−F(Pε)→0 in probability, and so does 𝛆𝛆F(Pε^)−F(Pε).

Proof.

  • (a) M0y∈C(Z), so the invariance of Eq. 23.5.1 gives the first claim, and 𝛆y=M0y+ε^0 gives the second. In ter Braak’s scheme, the F statistic for 𝛃𝛃β1=β^1 computed from y∗ is the F statistic for 𝛃β1=0 computed from 𝛃y∗−X1β^1, whose reduced model is the set of means 𝛃X1β^1+C(Z): the numerators agree by Theorem 11.3.2(b), and the denominators agree because subtracting 𝛃X1β^1∈C(X) leaves the residuals unchanged. Since 𝛃𝛄𝛆y∗−X1β^1=Zγ^+Pε^ with 𝛄Zγ^∈C(Z), the statistic is 𝛆F(Pε^).

  • (b) Under H0, 𝛆(I−M0)y=(I−M0)ε. Freedman–Lane and Kennedy use y only through 𝛆ε^0, and ter Braak through 𝛆𝛆ε^=(I−M)ε^0. The Draper–Stoneman statistic is unchanged when a vector in C(Z) is added to y. In each case the observed statistic is 𝛆F(y)=F(ε^0) by (a). The raw permutation statistic 𝛄𝛆F(Py)=F(PZγ+Pε) involves 𝛄γ whenever 𝛄PZγ∉C(Z), and its null distribution in general depends on 𝛄γ, as Figure 23.5.1 shows; we do not prove this.

  • (c) PZ=Z implies PM0P⊤=M0, since PM0P⊤ is the orthogonal projection onto C(PZ)=C(Z). So 𝛆𝛆𝛆Pε^0=P(I−M0)ε=(I−M0)Pε under H0, and 𝛆𝛆F(Pε^0)=F(Pε) by invariance. Also 𝛆F(y)=F(ε). So the Freedman–Lane p-value is the permutation p-value of the statistic F applied to 𝛆ε, which is exact by Theorem 23.4.2. The raw permutation statistic is 𝛄𝛆𝛆F(Zγ+Pε)=F(Pε) as well.

  • (d) Put 𝛆v=M0ε, so 𝛆𝛆Pε^0=Pε−Pv. For a fixed vector v and a uniform random permutation, E(Pv)=v¯1 and Cov(Pv)=Svn−1(I−n−111⊤), where Sv=∑i(vi−v¯)2≤‖v‖2 (Exercise (B1)). Since (M−M0)1=0, 𝛆𝛆E[‖(M−M0)Pv‖2∣ε]=Svn−1tr(M−M0)≤q‖M0ε‖2n−1, and 𝛆E‖M0ε‖2=σ2p0, so ‖(M−M0)Pv‖→0 in probability. The numerators of the two statistics are ‖N‖2/q and 𝛅‖N−δ‖2/q with 𝛆N=(M−M0)Pε and 𝛅δ=(M−M0)Pv. Here 𝛆Pε has the distribution of 𝛆ε, so E‖N‖2=qσ2, and 𝛅𝛅𝛅|‖N−δ‖2−‖N‖2|≤2‖N‖‖δ‖+‖δ‖2→0 in probability. For the denominators, 𝛆𝛆|‖(I−M)Pε^0‖−‖(I−M)Pε‖|≤‖v‖=Op(1), while 𝛆‖(I−M)Pε‖2/(n−p) has the distribution of s2 and tends to σ2 in probability (Corollary 5.6.4 needs fourth moments; the argument of Lemma 23.1.3 needs only the second). Both norms are Op(n), so the difference of their squares is at most ‖v‖ times Op(n). Dividing by n−p, the two denominators differ by a quantity that tends to zero, and both tend to σ2. The claim for 𝛆F(Pε^) is the same argument with 𝛆v=Mε, 𝛆E‖Mε‖2=σ2p.

Part (d) compares Freedman–Lane with an oracle that permutes the true errors. The oracle is exact: 𝛆F(y)=F(ε) under H0, and it compares 𝛆F(ε) with 𝛆F(Pbε), a permutation test for the exchangeable 𝛆ε. Freedman–Lane replaces 𝛆Pbε by 𝛆Pbε^0, which changes the statistics by vanishing amounts. To conclude that its size tends to α, one needs also that the oracle statistics have a continuous joint limit, so that vanishing perturbations rarely change their ranks. That holds under conditions like Eq. 23.1.1 by a permutation central limit theorem that we do not prove; Anderson and Robinson (2001) give the argument.

Part (b) is the practical point: four of the schemes see the data only after Z has been projected out, while for large 𝛄Zγ the raw permutation mostly permutes 𝛄Zγ, not the errors. Kennedy’s scheme can be pinned down exactly.

Proposition 23.5.2 (Kennedy’s scheme is anticonservative relative to Freedman–Lane)

For every permutation P, 𝛆𝛆FK(Pε^0)≤F(Pε^0), with equality when P=I. Consequently, for any set of permutations P1,…,PB, the Kennedy p-value is at most the Freedman–Lane p-value.

Proof. The two statistics have the same numerator. Write 𝛆u=Pε^0. Since I=M0+(M−M0)+(I−M) with orthogonal ranges, ‖(I−M)u‖2=‖u‖2−‖(M−M0)u‖2−‖M0u‖2≤‖u‖2−‖(M−M0)u‖2, so Kennedy’s denominator is at least as large. For P=I, 𝛆M0ε^0=0, and the two agree. Each permuted Kennedy statistic is therefore at most the corresponding Freedman–Lane one, while the observed statistics coincide, so fewer permuted values reach the observed one.

The discrepancy 𝛆‖M0Pε^0‖2, the part of the permuted residuals that Z could have explained, has expectation about (p0−1)σ2, small beside (n−p)σ2 for large n; so Kennedy’s scheme is liberal only in small samples.

23.5.3. Stack loss again, and a simulation

Example (Acid concentration given air flow and water temperature)

In the full stack loss regression, test whether acid concentration matters once air flow and water temperature are in the model. The F statistic is 0.947 on 1 and 17 degrees of freedom, with p-value 0.344. With 19999 permutations each, the schemes give: raw permutation 0.346, Draper–Stoneman 0.343, Kennedy 0.315, Freedman–Lane 0.345 and ter Braak 0.345. Only Kennedy’s differs noticeably, in the direction Proposition 23.5.2 predicts. The marginal association of Example (Acid concentration and stack loss) disappears once the correlated regressors are in the model.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats
data = sm.datasets.stackloss.load_pandas().data

def proj(Z):
    """Orthogonal projection onto C(Z), from a QR factorization."""
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Z)
    return Q @ Q.T

def perm_rows(v, B, rng):
    """B random permutations of the vector v, one per row."""
    return v[np.argsort(rng.random((B, len(v))), axis=1)]

def perm_pvalues(y, x, Z, B, rng):
    """p-values for H0: beta = 0 in E(y) = Z gamma + x beta, by six methods."""
    n, p = len(y), Z.shape[1] + 1
    MZ = proj(Z)
    xt = x - MZ @ x                               # x residualized on Z
    yt = y - MZ @ y                               # reduced-model residuals
    e_full = yt - xt * (xt @ yt) / (xt @ xt)      # full-model residuals (FWL)

    def F(Y):
        """F statistic for beta = 0, for each row of Y (model matrix [Z, x] fixed)."""
        num = (Y @ xt) ** 2 / (xt @ xt)
        rss_Z = np.sum(Y ** 2, axis=-1) - np.sum((Y @ MZ) * Y, axis=-1)
        return num / ((rss_Z - num) / (n - p))

    F_obs = F(y)
    out = {"F test": stats.f.sf(F_obs, 1, n - p)}

    def pval(F_star, F0=F_obs):
        return (1 + np.sum(F_star >= F0)) / (len(F_star) + 1)

    out["Manly"] = pval(F(perm_rows(y, B, rng)))                    # permute y
    Xs = perm_rows(x, B, rng)                                       # permute x (Draper-Stoneman)
    xts = Xs - Xs @ MZ
    num = (xts @ y) ** 2 / np.sum(xts ** 2, axis=1)
    out["Draper-Stoneman"] = pval(num / ((yt @ yt - num) / (n - p)))
    Yk = perm_rows(yt, B, rng)                                      # Kennedy: no Z in the refit
    num = (Yk @ xt) ** 2 / (xt @ xt)
    out["Kennedy"] = pval(num / ((np.sum(Yk ** 2, axis=1) - num) / (n - p)))
    out["Freedman-Lane"] = pval(F(perm_rows(yt, B, rng)))           # permute reduced residuals
    out["ter Braak"] = pval(F(perm_rows(e_full, B, rng)))           # permute full residuals
    return out, F_obs

rng = np.random.default_rng(2307)
y = data["STACKLOSS"].to_numpy()
x = data["ACIDCONC"].to_numpy()
Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), data[["AIRFLOW", "WATERTEMP"]]])
pvals, F_obs = perm_pvalues(y, x, Z, B=19_999, rng=rng)
print(f"F = {F_obs:.3f}")
for m, pv in pvals.items():
    print(f"{m:16s} p = {pv:.4f}")

Real data with a p-value near 0.34 cannot separate the schemes. The simulation in Figure 23.5.1 is designed to. It has n=12 cases, normal errors, a nuisance regressor z correlated with the regressor of interest x, and one high-leverage value of x. The null hypothesis holds, and the nuisance coefficient γ ranges from 0 to 30. Each point is the rejection rate of a nominal 5% test in 5000 data sets, with 199 permutations each, so the Monte Carlo standard error is about 0.0031.

The same random numbers are used for every γ, so by Proposition 23.5.1(b) five of the six tests make identical decisions at every γ, and their lines are flat. The F test rejects in 0.048 of the data sets, Freedman–Lane in 0.054, ter Braak and Draper–Stoneman in 0.046, and Kennedy in 0.066, the liberal bias of Proposition 23.5.2 made visible by the small sample. The raw permutation test is exact at γ=0 (0.048), but its size moves with γ, reaching 0.064 at γ=30.

def size_study(gamma, reps, B, seed, n=12, alpha=0.05):
    """Rejection rates at level alpha when beta = 0 and the nuisance coefficient is gamma."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    z = np.linspace(-1, 1, n)
    x = 0.8 * z + 0.6 * np.random.default_rng(1).normal(size=n)   # fixed, correlated with z
    x[0] = 6.0                                    # one high-leverage value of x
    Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), z])
    rejections = {}
    for _ in range(reps):
        y = 1.0 + gamma * z + rng.normal(size=n)  # the null hypothesis beta = 0 holds
        for m, pv in perm_pvalues(y, x, Z, B, rng)[0].items():
            rejections[m] = rejections.get(m, 0) + (pv <= alpha)
    return {m: r / reps for m, r in rejections.items()}

for gamma in (0.0, 30.0):
    print(gamma, size_study(gamma, reps=300, B=99, seed=2308))

As Anderson and Legendre (1999) concluded from a much larger simulation, Freedman–Lane is the sensible default: exact whenever an exact test exists, blind to the nuisance coefficients, and close to the oracle in large samples. ter Braak’s scheme behaves similarly, and since its reference distribution does not need the null hypothesis, it suits the inversion of tests into intervals. Winkler and coauthors (2014) review the schemes for the general linear model.

23.5.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that when Z=1 the Freedman–Lane, Kennedy and raw permutation tests all coincide with the permutation test of Section 23.4.

Solution. With Z=1, every permutation fixes Z, so by Proposition 23.5.1(c) Freedman–Lane and the raw permutation coincide and are exact. Kennedy’s scheme differs only through 𝛆𝛆‖M0Pε^0‖2=n(Pε^0)―2, which is zero because 𝛆ε^0=y−y¯1 sums to zero and permuting does not change the sum.

Exercise (A2)

In ter Braak’s scheme, what is the permuted statistic for P=I? Explain why the observed statistic must still be counted in the numerator and denominator of Eq. 23.4.1.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let v∈Rn be fixed and P a uniformly random permutation matrix. Show that E(Pv)=v¯1 and Cov(Pv)=Svn−1(I−n−111⊤) with Sv=∑i(vi−v¯)2. (The computation is the one in the proof of Proposition 18.4.2, there for permutations within a block.)

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Suppose the errors are independent with variances that depend on X1. Explain why the schemes of Definition 23.5.1 need not be asymptotically valid for 𝛃H0:β1=0, and where the argument of Proposition 23.5.1(d) breaks down. Propose a studentized version in the spirit of Section 23.4.

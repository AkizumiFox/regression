23.4  Permutation tests for linear hypotheses

The bootstrap replaces an unknown distribution by an estimate. A permutation test instead looks for transformations of the data that leave their distribution unchanged under the null hypothesis, and compares the observed statistic with its values on the transformed data. When such transformations exist the test is exact at every sample size, whatever the error law. The idea is Fisher’s (1935), developed by Pitman (1937); in designed experiments the transformations are the rearrangements the randomization could have produced (Section 18.4).

23.4.1. The general construction

Let G be a finite group of n×n permutation matrices. A random vector Y is G-invariant in distribution if PY has the distribution of Y for every P∈G. For the group of all permutations this is exchangeability.

Definition 23.4.1 (Permutation test)

Let T:Rn→R be a statistic, large values of which count against the null hypothesis.

  • (a) The permutation p-value is p(y)=|G|−1#{P∈G:T(Py)≥T(y)}.

  • (b) The Monte Carlo permutation p-value draws P1,…,PB independently and uniformly from G, independently of Y, and is

    pB(y)=1+#{b:T(Pby)≥T(y)}B+1.
    (23.4.1)

The test of level α rejects when the p-value is at most α.

The 1 in Eq. 23.4.1 counts the observed arrangement among those compared; without it the p-value can be zero and the test is not exact (Phipson and Smyth 2010). Exactness rests on a counting fact.

Lemma 23.4.1 (Ranks of exchangeable values)

For real numbers t0,…,tm, let Rj=#{i:ti≥tj}. Then #{j:Rj≤k}≤k for every k. Consequently, if (T0,…,Tm) is an exchangeable random vector and R0=#{i:Ti≥T0}, then Pr(R0≤k)≤k/(m+1).

Proof. Let S={j:Rj≤k}, and suppose it is not empty. Choose j∗∈S with the smallest tj∗. Every j∈S has tj≥tj∗, so Rj∗≥|S|, and |S|≤Rj∗≤k. For the second claim, exchangeability gives Pr(Rj≤k)=Pr(R0≤k) for every j, so (m+1)Pr(R0≤k)=E#{j:Rj≤k}≤k.

Theorem 23.4.2 (Exactness of permutation tests)

If Y is G-invariant in distribution, then for every statistic T, every α∈[0,1] and every B≥1, Pr(p(Y)≤α)≤α,Pr(pB(Y)≤α)≤α.

Proof. Let Γ be uniform on G, independent of everything else. Invariance gives ΓY=dY, since this holds conditionally on each value of Γ.

Full group. p(Y) has the distribution of p(ΓY). As P runs over G so does PΓ, so p(ΓY)=|G|−1#{P∈G:T(PY)≥T(ΓY)}. Given Y, list the values tP=T(PY), P∈G. Then |G|p(ΓY)=RΓ in the notation of Lemma 23.4.1, with Γ uniform over the |G| indices. By the first part of the lemma, Pr(RΓ≤k∣Y)≤k/|G|. With k=⌊α|G|⌋ this gives Pr(p≤α∣Y)≤α, and we take expectations.

Monte Carlo. Put P0=I and Tb=T(PbY), so that (B+1)pB=R0. We show (T0,…,TB) is exchangeable. Since Y is independent of (P1,…,PB) and ΓY=dY with ΓY also independent of them, (T0,…,TB) has the distribution of (T(ΓY),T(P1ΓY),…,T(PBΓY)). The map (G0,G1,…,GB)↦(G0,G1G0,…,GBG0) is a bijection of GB+1, so Γ,P1Γ,…,PBΓ are independent and uniform on G, and independent of Y. Given Y, the B+1 values are therefore independent and identically distributed, hence exchangeable, and so they are unconditionally. The second part of Lemma 23.4.1 with k=⌊α(B+1)⌋ completes the proof.

Nothing about T was used: the statistic affects power, never validity. The full-group test has size exactly α when α|G| is an integer and the values T(PY) are almost surely distinct. In the Monte Carlo test a draw can repeat the identity or another draw, and the resulting ties make R0 not exactly uniform, so its size is at most α and approaches α as |G| grows, when α(B+1) is an integer. And the theorem says nothing about 𝛃β. The hypothesis it tests is the invariance, and translating a regression hypothesis into an invariance is the whole difficulty.

23.4.2. Simple regression

In the straight-line model with fixed xi and independent, identically distributed errors, β1=0 says 𝛆Y=β01+ε, which is exchangeable, so a permutation test of β1=0 is exact for any error law. The usual statistics give the same test.

Proposition 23.4.3 (Equivalent statistics for the slope)

With G the group of all permutations, the statistics |β^1|, |r|, |t| and F=t2 for the simple regression of y on x give the same permutation p-value, for every data set and, in the Monte Carlo version, for every draw of permutations.

Proof. Permuting y leaves y¯ and Syy=∑i(yi−y¯)2 unchanged, and Sxx does not involve y. Now β^1=Sxy/Sxx and r=Sxy/SxxSyy, so |β^1| and |r| are the same increasing function of |Sxy| for every rearrangement. And t2=(n−2)r2/(1−r2), which increases with r2 on [0,1). So all four order the arrangements Py in the same way, and the counts in Definition 23.4.1 agree.

Example (Acid concentration and stack loss)

Brownlee’s stack loss data (Example (Stack loss)) record 21 days of operation of a plant oxidizing ammonia. Regress stack loss on the acid concentration of the absorbing liquid alone. The slope is 0.7590, with t=1.901 and a two-sided t-test p-value of 0.0725. The 5.11×1019 arrangements are too many to enumerate; Monte Carlo with B=19999 permutations gives the p-value 0.0698, with Monte Carlo standard error 0.0018. Figure 23.4.1 shows the permutation distribution of t with the t(19) density. The agreement is close, but the permutation p-value assumes nothing about the error law.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats
data = sm.datasets.stackloss.load_pandas().data

rng = np.random.default_rng(2304)
y = data["STACKLOSS"].to_numpy()
x = data["ACIDCONC"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)
xc = x - x.mean()

def slope_stats(Y):
    """Slope, correlation and t statistic for each row of Y, regressed on the fixed x."""
    Yc = Y - Y.mean(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    b = Yc @ xc / (xc @ xc)
    r = Yc @ xc / np.sqrt((xc @ xc) * np.sum(Yc ** 2, axis=-1))
    t = r * np.sqrt(n - 2) / np.sqrt(1 - r ** 2)
    return b, r, t

b_obs, r_obs, t_obs = slope_stats(y)
p_t = 2 * stats.t.sf(abs(t_obs), n - 2)

B = 19_999
Yperm = y[np.argsort(rng.random((B, n)), axis=1)]  # B random permutations of y
b_perm, r_perm, t_perm = slope_stats(Yperm)
p_perm = (1 + np.sum(np.abs(b_perm) >= abs(b_obs))) / (B + 1)
print(f"slope {b_obs:.4f}, t = {t_obs:.3f}, t-test p = {p_t:.4f}, permutation p = {p_perm:.4f}")

23.4.3. What the permutation test tests

Without identically distributed errors, invariance and β1=0 come apart. If independent cases have E(Y∣X)=0 but Var(Y∣X) depending on X, the slope is zero but the responses are not exchangeable given the x’s. The permutation test tests independence, and rejects too often as a test of zero slope. By the central limit theorem for the sample covariance, when Cov(X,Y)=0, nr→N(0,κ2) with κ2=E[(X−μX)2(Y−μY)2]Var(X)Var(Y), which equals 1 under independence but not in general. The permutation distribution of nr tends to N(0,1) under mild conditions whatever the dependence, because permuting creates independence; we do not prove this permutation central limit theorem (see Lehmann and Romano 2005, chapter 15). Let Y=Xε, X and ε independent standard normals. Then Var(Y)=1 and E(X2Y2)=EX4Eε2=3, so κ2=3. A nominal 5% permutation test based on |r|, or by Proposition 23.4.3 on |β^1|, has limiting size Pr(|N(0,3)|>1.96)=2(1−Φ(1.96/3))=2(1−Φ(1.1316))=0.258. A simulation with 4000 data sets and 399 permutations each gives 0.236 at n=20 and 0.248 at n=200.

The remedy (DiCiccio and Romano 2017) is to studentize: β^1/se^HC0 has a standard normal limit under zero slope whether or not X and Y are independent (Exercise (C1)), and so does its permutation distribution. The test is exact under independence and asymptotically valid for zero slope. In the simulation its size is 0.117 at n=20 and 0.056 at n=200: much better, though at n=20 still far from exact.

import numpy as np

def perm_sizes(n, reps, B, rng, alpha=0.05):
    """Rejection rates of permutation tests of zero slope when y = x * eps (x, eps iid normal)."""
    reject = {"raw": 0, "studentized": 0}
    for _ in range(reps):
        x = rng.normal(size=n)
        y = x * rng.normal(size=n)                # E(y | x) = 0, Var(y | x) = x^2
        Y = np.vstack([y, y[np.argsort(rng.random((B, n)), axis=1)]])   # row 0: the data
        xc = x - x.mean()
        Yc = Y - Y.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        b = Yc @ xc / (xc @ xc)
        e = Yc - b[:, None] * xc
        se_hc0 = np.sqrt((e ** 2) @ xc ** 2) / (xc @ xc)
        for name, T in [("raw", np.abs(b)), ("studentized", np.abs(b / se_hc0))]:
            p = np.mean(T >= T[0])                # (1 + #{T_b >= T_0}) / (B + 1)
            reject[name] += p <= alpha
    return {k: v / reps for k, v in reject.items()}

rng = np.random.default_rng(2305)
for n_sim in (20, 200):
    print(n_sim, perm_sizes(n_sim, reps=400, B=199, rng=rng))

23.4.4. Nuisance regressors

Most regression hypotheses concern some coefficients in the presence of others:

𝛄𝛃𝛆𝛃Y=Zγ+X1β1+ε,H0:β1=0,
(23.4.2)

with Z of size n×p0 containing the nuisance regressors (including the intercept) and X1 of size n×q. Under H0, 𝛄𝛆Y=Zγ+ε, which is not exchangeable unless 𝛄Zγ is a multiple of 1. There is an exact test only when some group of permutations leaves the nuisance part alone.

Proposition 23.4.4 (Permutations that fix the nuisance regressors)

Suppose 𝛆ε is G-invariant in distribution, and PZ=Z for every P∈G. Then under H0 of Eq. 23.4.2 the response Y is G-invariant in distribution, and the permutation test over G with any statistic is exact, whatever the value of 𝛄γ.

Proof. 𝛄𝛆𝛄𝛆𝛄𝛆PY=PZγ+Pε=Zγ+Pε=dZγ+ε=Y. Apply Theorem 23.4.2.

The condition PZ=Z says that P permutes only cases with identical rows of Z. For block indicators, G is the group of permutations within blocks, and the proposition gives, under errors exchangeable within blocks, the same test as the design-based randomization test of a randomized complete block design (Section 18.4, Definition 18.4.1), which needs no error model at all. When Z contains a continuous covariate with distinct values only the identity qualifies, and no exact permutation test exists. The schemes of the next section give up exactness for generality.

23.4.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

With B=99 random permutations, what is the smallest possible value of Eq. 23.4.1? For which α between 0.01 and 0.10 is the size of the Monte Carlo test exactly α when there are no ties (ignoring the chance of drawing the same arrangement twice)?

Solution. The smallest value is 1/100, reached when no permuted statistic reaches the observed one. When there are no ties (ignoring the chance of drawing the same arrangement twice), R0 is uniform on {1,…,100}, so Pr(pB≤α)=⌊100α⌋/100, which equals α exactly when 100α is an integer: α=0.01,0.02,…,0.10.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

For x=(1,2,3,4) and y=(1.2, 0.8, 2.9, 3.1), enumerate all 24 arrangements of y and compute the exact two-sided permutation p-value for the slope. Compare it with the t-test p-value, and explain why the permutation p-value cannot be smaller than 2/24 for any data with n=4 and distinct values.

Solution. By Proposition 23.4.3 it suffices to rank the arrangements by |Sxy|, where Sxy=∑i(xi−x¯)yπ(i) with centred x equal to (−1.5,−0.5,0.5,1.5). For the data, Sxy=−1.8−0.4+1.45+4.65=3.9. The largest values of Sxy come from putting the two largest responses at x=3,4 and the two smallest at x=1,2; the four such arrangements give 3.7, 3.9, 4.1 and 4.3, and every other arrangement gives at most 2.6. Reversing an arrangement negates Sxy. So 6 of the 24 arrangements have |Sxy|≥3.9, and the p-value is 6/24=0.25. The t-test gives 0.139: with four points the permutation distribution is coarse. The observed arrangement and its reversal always count, so p≥2/24.

Exercise (B2)

Compute κ2 when Y=ε1+λX2 with X and ε independent standard normals, and the limiting size of the nominal 5% permutation test of zero slope as a function of λ. What happens as λ→∞?

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

For independent pairs with Cov(X,Y)=0 and finite fourth moments, show that β^1/se^HC0→N(0,1) in distribution. (Use the central limit theorem for n−1/2∑i(Xi−μX)(Yi−μY) and Slutsky’s lemma.)

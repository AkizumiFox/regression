34.5  Deviance and asymptotic inference

Parts II and III rested on exact distribution theory: 𝛃β^ was normal because Y was, and sums of squares were chi-squared because they were quadratic forms in a normal vector (Theorem 4.3.2). None of that survives. This section states what replaces it in the limit, under which conditions, and — just as important — where the approximations fail.

34.5.1. Asymptotic normality

The conditions below are stated for the canonical link, where the argument can be given in full. Throughout 𝛃β0 is the true parameter, 𝛃θi0=x(i)⊤β0, and 𝛃An=In(β0)=1ϕX⊤diag{wib″(θi0)}X is the expected information from Eq. 34.3.2. Because the canonical-link Hessian does not involve y (Theorem 34.3.1(d)), An is a fixed matrix, not a random one. Define the scalars

qni=wib″(θi0)ϕx(i)⊤An−1x(i),i=1,…,n.
(34.5.1)

These are the exact analogue of leverages: nonnegative, summing to tr(An−1An)=p, so they average p/n as in Proposition 6.8.1(b). The conditions are

  • (G1) the responses are independent, the model is that of Definition 34.2.1 with the canonical link, rank(X)=p with p fixed, and ϕ and the wi are known;
  • (G2) 𝛃β0 is interior to B, the natural parameters θi0 lie in a fixed compact subset K of the interior of Θ, and 0<wmin≤wi≤wmax<∞;
  • (G3) maxi≤nqni→0: no single observation carries a fixed share of the information;
  • (G4) λmin(An)→∞: information accumulates in every direction.
Theorem 34.5.1 (Consistency and asymptotic normality of the estimate)

Under (G1)–(G4), with probability tending to one the likelihood equations have a solution 𝛃β^n; it is the unique maximizer of ℓ, it is consistent for 𝛃β0, and

𝛃𝛃An1/2(β^n−β0) →d Np(0,I).
(34.5.2)

The same holds with An replaced by 𝛃A^n=In(β^n). Consequently, for a fixed 𝛌λ≠0, 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌λ⊤β^n−λ⊤β0(λ⊤A^n−1λ)1/2 →d N(0,1), and 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌λ⊤β^n±z1−α/2(λ⊤A^n−1λ)1/2 is an interval with asymptotic coverage 1−α.

One result is imported, not proved: the standard corollary of the convexity lemma of Pollard (1991, section 6), that pointwise convergence of concave random functions is automatically uniform on compact sets. Let Λn be random concave functions on Rp, allowed the value −∞ outside a convex set, of the form Λn(s)=s⊤zn−12‖s‖2+rn(s) with zn=Op(1) and sup‖s‖≤M|rn(s)|→0 in probability for every M. Then a maximizer s^n exists with probability tending to one and s^n−zn→0 in probability. Concavity does the work that compactness conditions do in the usual theory, and Theorem 34.3.3 supplies it free.

Proof. Step 1: the score is asymptotically normal. By Eq. 34.3.3, Un=ϕ−1∑iwi(Yi−μi0)x(i) is a sum of independent mean-zero vectors with Cov(Un)=An. Put zn=An−1/2Un. For a fixed unit vector v write v⊤zn=∑icniξi with ξi=Yi−μi0 and the weights cni=ϕ−1wiv⊤An−1/2x(i). This is the weighted sum of Lemma 21.2.1, and its case (b) applies. Hence maxicni2/‖cn‖2≤C″maxiqni→0 by (G3), which is Eq. 21.2.2. So v⊤zn→N(0,1) for every unit v, and the Cramér–Wold device gives zn→Np(0,I).

Taylor’s theorem with integral remainder, applied to the smooth function ℓ, gives 𝛃Λn(s)=s⊤zn−∫01(1−t)s⊤An−1/2Jn(βt)An−1/2sdt, where 𝛃𝛃βt=β0+tAn−1/2s and 𝛃𝛃Jn(β)=ϕ−1X⊤diag{wib″(x(i)⊤β)}X is the nonrandom observed information. So for large n every perturbed natural parameter θi0+tx(i)⊤An−1/2s lies in a fixed compact neighbourhood of K inside Θ, on which b″ is uniformly continuous and bounded away from 0 and ∞; hence b″(θi0+δi)=b″(θi0){1+ϵni} with supi|ϵni|→0 uniformly in t∈[0,1] and ‖s‖≤M, and An−1/2Jn(⋅)An−1/2=I+Rn with ‖Rn‖→0, and

Λn(s)=s⊤zn−12‖s‖2+rn(s),sup‖s‖≤M|rn(s)|→0.
(34.5.3)

Step 3: the maximizer. By Theorem 34.3.3 each Λn is concave. It is defined only on the convex set 𝛃An1/2(B−β0), a half-space for the canonical links of the gamma and inverse Gaussian; extend it by −∞ outside, which preserves concavity, and note that Step 2 put every ball ‖s‖≤M inside that set for large n, so the expansion holds where the lemma needs it. Then Eq. 34.5.3 is exactly the form the lemma requires, with zn=Op(1) by Step 1 and a nonrandom remainder bound, so a maximizer s^n exists with probability tending to one and s^n−zn→0 in probability. Since 𝛃𝛃s^n=An1/2(β^n−β0), Slutsky’s theorem and Step 1 give Eq. 34.5.2, and Theorem 34.3.3 makes the maximizer unique.

Consistency. 𝛃𝛃‖β^n−β0‖≤λmin(An)−1/2‖s^n‖, and ‖s^n‖=Op(1), so (G4) gives 𝛃𝛃β^n→β0 in probability.

Plug-in. Consistency and the uniform continuity of b″ used in Step 2 give An−1/2A^nAn−1/2→I in probability, whence 𝛃𝛃A^n1/2(β^n−β0)→Np(0,I). The last two displays follow by taking the 𝛌λ component.

Remark (What the conditions mean, and the non-canonical case). Condition (G3) is the honest content of “large sample”. It is the generalized-linear-model version of the requirement maxihii→0 that Chapter 21 needed, and Step 1 used it in exactly that role, to supply Eq. 21.2.2. It fails when it should: a group with a single observation, a regressor that indicates one case, a binary design with a nearly empty cell. The canonical link is used twice, for the concavity and for the nonrandom Hessian, and the next proposition records what survives without it.

Warning. Nothing here is a finite-sample statement: 𝛃β^ is biased, by O(n−1) against a standard error of O(n−1/2) — asymptotically negligible, not zero. Nor does p fixed cover many parameters: a model with a parameter per group and bounded group sizes violates (G3), and Example (Many means, one variance) showed what goes wrong.

Intervals for a fitted mean. For fixed x0 the theorem with 𝛌λ=x0 gives η^0±z1−α/2(x0⊤A^n−1x0)1/2 for 𝛃η0=x0⊤β0, the counterpart of Theorem 12.1.1 with a normal quantile for a t one; applying the monotone h to both endpoints makes it an interval for μ0=h(η0) of the same asymptotic coverage. The interval transforms, not the endpoint formula, so it is not symmetric about μ^0 — a feature, since a log link then keeps it positive and a logit link inside (0,1). The delta-method alternative μ^0±z1−α/2h′(η^0)(x0⊤A^n−1x0)1/2 agrees to first order and is the worse wherever h curves sharply. Neither is a prediction interval: Theorem 12.3.1 has no clean analogue here.

Proposition 34.5.2 (Non-canonical links)

Let the link be any twice continuously differentiable g with g′≠0 on M, and put An=ϕ−1X⊤WX with W the working weights of Theorem 34.3.1 evaluated at 𝛃β0. Then every conclusion of Theorem 34.5.1 — existence with probability tending to one, consistency, the limit Eq. 34.5.2, the plug-in version and the Wald intervals — continues to hold, as do Proposition 34.5.3 and Theorem 34.5.5(a),(b) below, which use the theorem only through the quadratic expansion Eq. 34.5.3. The conditions are those of Fahrmeir and Kaufmann (1985): the divergence condition λmin(An)→∞, and a continuity condition asking that 𝛃𝛃𝛃An(β0)−1/2An(β)An(β0)−1/2→I uniformly over 𝛃β in neighbourhoods of 𝛃β0 that shrink in the information metric. The two together play the part of (G2)–(G4).

Remark. This proposition is quoted, not proved: without concavity and a nonrandom Hessian the argument above collapses, and the replacement is the content of Fahrmeir and Kaufmann (1985). It is recorded because most models in the chapters that follow use a non-canonical link somewhere — the complementary log–log and probit links of Chapter 35, the gamma/log fit of Example (Food expenditure: three models, two decisions), the negative binomial with a log link in Chapter 37.

34.5.2. Wald, score and likelihood ratio

Consider a reduced model C(X0)⊆C(X) with rank(X0)=p−q, and let 𝛃β~ be the maximum likelihood estimate under it, written as a vector in Rp. Since X has full column rank, 𝛃𝛃{β:Xβ∈C(X0)} is a subspace of dimension p−q, so it is 𝛃𝛃{β:Λ⊤β=0} for some p×q matrix Λ of rank q, in the notation of Definition 11.3.1.

Proposition 34.5.3 (The three statistics)

Define 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃Wald=(Λ⊤β^)⊤(Λ⊤A^n−1Λ)−1Λ⊤β^,Score=U(β~)⊤In(β~)−1U(β~),LR=2{ℓ(β^)−ℓ(β~)}. Under (G1)–(G4) and the reduced model, each of the three converges in distribution to χ2(q), and any two of them differ by op(1). For a single coefficient (q=1, Λ=ej) the Wald statistic is β^j2/[A^n−1]jj, the square of the “estimate over standard error” ratio that every program prints.

Proof. Work in the coordinates 𝛃𝛃s=An1/2(β−β0) of the proof of Theorem 34.5.1, where 𝛃β0 is the true parameter, which lies in the reduced model, so 𝛃Λ⊤β0=0. The reduced model is the linear subspace Sn=An1/2N(Λ⊤)=N(Bn) of Rp, where Bn=Λ⊤An−1/2 has rank q; let Pn be the orthogonal projection onto it, so that I−Pn=Bn⊤(BnBn⊤)−1Bn.

For the likelihood ratio, Eq. 34.5.3 gives Λn(s)=s⊤zn−12‖s‖2+rn(s) uniformly on compacts. Maximizing s⊤zn−12‖s‖2 over Rp gives 12‖zn‖2 at s=zn; over Sn it gives 12‖Pnzn‖2 at s=Pnzn. Both maximizers are Op(1), so the remainder is negligible at both, and LR=2{maxRpΛn−maxSnΛn}=‖(I−Pn)zn‖2+op(1). If z∼Np(0,I) then ‖(I−P)z‖2∼χ2(q) for every projection P of rank p−q (Theorem 4.3.2). Projections of a fixed rank form a compact set, so along any subsequence Pn→P, and the continuous mapping theorem gives ‖(I−Pn)zn‖2→χ2(q); the limit is the same for every subsequence, so the whole sequence converges.

For the Wald statistic, write 𝛃𝛃s^n=An1/2(β^−β0). Then 𝛃𝛃𝛃Λ⊤β^=Λ⊤(β^−β0)=Bns^n and Λ⊤An−1Λ=BnBn⊤, so with An in place of A^n the statistic is exactly ‖(I−Pn)s^n‖2. The proof of Theorem 34.5.1 gives s^n=zn+op(1), and An−1/2A^nAn−1/2→I in probability makes the substitution of A^n cost op(1); hence Wald=‖(I−Pn)zn‖2+op(1). The same argument inside the reduced model gives 𝛃𝛃An1/2(β~−β0)=Pnzn+op(1), which is used next.

For the score statistic, differentiating Eq. 34.5.3 — legitimate because pointwise convergence of differentiable concave functions forces convergence of their gradients (Rockafellar 1970, section 25) — gives 𝛃An−1/2U(β0+An−1/2s)=zn−s+op(1) uniformly on compacts; at s=Pnzn+op(1) this is (I−Pn)zn+op(1).

In the normal linear model the three are exact monotone functions of one another and of the F statistic, with Wald>LR>Score in every sample (Proposition 11.4.2). Outside it that ordering is not guaranteed, and the three agree only to first order.

The Wald statistic is not invariant. Testing βj=0 and testing eβj=1 are the same hypothesis with different Wald statistics, because the delta-method standard error of eβ^j is not the transform of that of β^j; the likelihood ratio statistic, which compares maximized likelihoods, is invariant under every reparameterization.

The Wald statistic can move the wrong way. This failure has a name.

Example (The Hauck–Donner effect)

Two groups of 40 trials, with 20 successes in group A and sB in group B, fitted by a logistic model with an intercept and a group indicator. The coefficient is the log odds ratio β^1=log⁡{sB/(40−sB)}, with standard error {1/20+1/20+1/sB+1/(40−sB)}1/2. As sB rises from 21 to 39 the evidence against equality strengthens monotonically and the likelihood ratio statistic records it, climbing to 27.301. The Wald statistic does not: it rises to 13.842 at sB=38 and then falls to 11.924 at sB=39, though the fitted log odds ratio has grown to 3.664. The standard error grows faster than the coefficient.

At complete separation, sB=40, coefficient and standard error are both infinite and the Wald statistic is 0: the test accepts the null precisely where the data support it most strongly (Figure 34.5.1). The effect occurs whenever the log-likelihood is far from quadratic over the range where the estimate lies.

import numpy as np

m_hd, s_a = 40, 20                                  # group A: 20 successes out of 40
s_b = np.arange(21, 40)                             # group B: from 21 to 39 successes
odds_ratio = (s_b / (m_hd - s_b)) / (s_a / (m_hd - s_a))
beta1 = np.log(odds_ratio)                          # the fitted log odds ratio
se1 = np.sqrt(1 / s_a + 1 / (m_hd - s_a) + 1 / s_b + 1 / (m_hd - s_b))
wald = (beta1 / se1) ** 2

def binom_ll(s, m, p):
    return s * np.log(p) + (m - s) * np.log(1 - p)

p_pool = (s_a + s_b) / (2 * m_hd)
lr = 2 * (binom_ll(s_a, m_hd, s_a / m_hd) + binom_ll(s_b, m_hd, s_b / m_hd)
          - binom_ll(s_a, m_hd, p_pool) - binom_ll(s_b, m_hd, p_pool))

for k in (0, 10, 15, 17, 18):
    print(f"successes in B = {s_b[k]:2d}:  log odds ratio {beta1[k]:6.3f}"
          f"   Wald {wald[k]:7.3f}   likelihood ratio {lr[k]:7.3f}")

When the log-likelihood is close to quadratic, the three agree closely. For the age coefficient of Example (Fitted votes by party identification) the script obtains the single-coefficient Wald=2.046, Score=2.054 and LR=2.066, all far from the 5% point 3.84 of χ2(1), so the three lead to the same conclusion.

The score statistic needs only the null fit, so it can test a term from the model without it; but it evaluates the information at 𝛃β~, the wrong place when the alternative is far away. The recommendation is simple: report likelihood ratio tests, and where an interval is wanted invert them, {βj:LR(βj)≤χ1−α2(1)}, rather than using the Wald interval.

34.5.3. The deviance

Definition 34.5.1 (Deviance and scaled deviance)

Let 𝛍μ^ be the fitted means of a model and let θ(⋅) be as in Definition 34.1.2. Assuming every yi∈M, the saturated model is the one that sets μi=yi. The deviance of the fit is

𝛍D(y,μ^)=∑i=1ndi,di=2wi[yi{θ(yi)−θ(μ^i)}−b{θ(yi)}+b{θ(μ^i)}],
(34.5.4)

and the scaled deviance is 𝛍D∗=D/ϕ=2{ℓsat−ℓ(μ^)}.

The deviance does not involve ϕ; the scaled deviance does. For the normal family, θ=μ and b(θ)=θ2/2 give di=wi(yi−μ^i)2: the deviance is the weighted residual sum of squares, the scaled deviance SSE/σ2.

Family di/wi
normal (y−μ^)2
binomial (y a proportion) 2[ylog⁡yμ^+(1−y)log⁡1−y1−μ^]
Poisson 2[ylog⁡yμ^−(y−μ^)]
gamma 2[−log⁡yμ^+y−μ^μ^]
inverse Gaussian (y−μ^)2yμ^2

with the convention 0log⁡0=0.

Proposition 34.5.4 (The deviance is nonnegative)

Every di≥0, with di=0 if and only if μ^i=yi. Consequently D≥0, with equality only for the saturated fit, and D decreases when a model is enlarged.

Proof. Write θ~=θ(yi), θ^=θ(μ^i), so b′(θ~)=yi. Then di2wi=yi(θ~−θ^)−b(θ~)+b(θ^)=b(θ^)−b(θ~)−b′(θ~)(θ^−θ~) ≥0 by convexity of b (Lemma 34.1.1): the right-hand side is the Bregman divergence of b, the amount by which b lies above its tangent at θ~. Strict convexity makes it strict unless θ^=θ~, that is μ^i=yi. Enlarging the model can only raise the maximized likelihood, and 𝛍D=2ϕ{ℓsat−ℓ(μ^)}.

The other standard discrepancy is the Pearson statistic,

X2=∑i=1nwi(yi−μ^i)2V(μ^i),
(34.5.5)

which is ∑i(yi−μ^i)2/Var^(Yi) up to the factor ϕ. It equals the deviance for the normal family and approximates it elsewhere, since a second-order expansion of di about μ^i=yi gives wi(yi−μ^i)2/V(μ^i).

34.5.4. Analysis of deviance

Theorem 34.5.5 (Distribution theory for the deviance)

Assume (G1)–(G4).

  • (a) (Nested models.) Let C(X0)⊆C(X) with rank(X)−rank(X0)=q. Under the reduced model, D0−Dϕ →d χ2(q), where D0 and D are the deviances of the two fits. When ϕ is unknown it is replaced by an estimate, and the ratio {(D0−D)/q}/ϕ^ is referred to F(q,n−p), by analogy with Theorem 11.1.1.

  • (b) (Grouped data.) Suppose the data consist of G groups, G and p fixed, with yi an average of wi=mi observations and mi→∞ for every i. Then under the fitted model D/ϕ→χ2(G−p) and X2/ϕ→χ2(G−p), so both are goodness-of-fit statistics.

  • (c) (Ungrouped data.) If the number of groups grows with the sample size, (b) does not apply and the limit is generally not χ2(n−p). For binary responses with the canonical link the failure is total: D is a function of 𝛃β^ alone, so it carries no information about fit beyond what 𝛃β^ already contains.

Proof.

  • (a) By Definition 34.5.1 the saturated log-likelihood cancels in the difference, so 𝛃𝛃(D0−D)/ϕ=2{ℓ(β^)−ℓ(β~)}, which is the likelihood ratio statistic of Proposition 34.5.3.

  • (b) Regard the data as the ∑imi individual observations rather than the G averages, and take the saturated model to be the one with a separate parameter per group, so that its model matrix is the group indicators and it has G parameters, fixed as mi→∞. For that model the quantities Eq. 34.5.1 evaluated at the individual observations are qi=1/mg(i), with g(i) the group of observation i, so (G3) holds; and the information in group g is mgb″(θg)/ϕ→∞, so (G4) holds. Part (a) applied to the fitted model against the saturated one, with q=G−p, gives D/ϕ→χ2(G−p). For X2, a third-order expansion of di about μ^i=yi gives wi(yi−μ^i)2/V(μ^i)+O(wi|yi−μ^i|3); with wi=mi and yi−μ^i=Op(mi−1/2) the remainder summed over the G groups is Op(maximi−1/2), so X2−D→0 in probability.

  • (c) For a binary response with the logit link, M=(0,1) and yi∈{0,1} is not in M; taking limits in Eq. 34.5.4 with 0log⁡0=0 gives the saturated log-likelihood 0 and 𝛃D=−2∑i{yilog⁡μ^i+(1−yi)log⁡(1−μ^i)}=−2ℓ(β^). Writing 𝛃η^i=x(i)⊤β^ and using log⁡(1−μ^i)=−log⁡(1+eη^i), 𝛃𝛃𝛃ℓ(β^)=∑i{yiη^i−log⁡(1+eη^i)}=β^⊤X⊤y−∑ilog⁡(1+ex(i)⊤β^). By Corollary 34.3.2, 𝛍X⊤y=X⊤μ^, so 𝛃𝛃𝛍𝛃ℓ(β^)=β^⊤X⊤μ^−∑ilog⁡(1+ex(i)⊤β^) depends on the data only through 𝛃β^, and two data sets with the same 𝛃β^ have the same deviance.

Example (Grouped and ungrouped, simulated)

A logistic model with two parameters was fitted to 2000 data sets simulated from it, in two formats with the same number of Bernoulli trials over the same range of x: as 40 groups of 20 trials, and as 800 single trials. (The listing below repeats the experiment with 200 replicates, so that it runs in a few seconds.)

For the grouped data G−p=38, and the simulated deviances have mean 39.74 and standard deviation 9.43, against the χ2(38) values 38 and 76=8.72: usable. For the ungrouped data n−p=798, and the simulated deviances have mean 987.95 and standard deviation 20.62, against the χ2(798) values 798 and 39.95 — the mean off by nearly 200, the spread by a factor of two (Figure 34.5.2). Referring that deviance to χ2(n−p) would reject a correct model with overwhelming confidence.

Nothing is wrong with the sample size. The two designs carry very nearly the same Fisher information — the script checks that the standard errors of 𝛃β^ agree to within 2% — so the estimates behave alike. What fails is the reference distribution, because the saturated model in the second format has 800 parameters and grows with the data.

def logit_irls(X, y, w, steps=25):
    """Maximum likelihood for a binomial model with logit link; y is a proportion."""
    mu = (w * y + 0.5) / (w + 1.0)
    eta = np.log(mu / (1 - mu))
    beta = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps):
        d = mu * (1 - mu)
        W = w * d
        z = eta + (y - mu) / d
        beta = np.linalg.solve((X.T * W) @ X, (X.T * W) @ z)
        eta = X @ beta
        mu = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-eta))
    return beta, mu


def binomial_deviance(y, mu, w):
    t1 = np.where(y > 0, y * np.log(np.where(y > 0, y, 1.0) / mu), 0.0)
    t2 = np.where(y < 1, (1 - y) * np.log(np.where(y < 1, 1 - y, 1.0) / (1 - mu)), 0.0)
    return 2 * np.sum(w * (t1 + t2))


def null_deviances(X, beta, m, reps, rng):
    """Residual deviance of the fitted model, on `reps` data sets simulated from it."""
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ beta)))
    w = np.full(len(p), float(m))
    out = np.empty(reps)
    for r in range(reps):
        y = rng.binomial(m, p).astype(float) / m
        _, mu = logit_irls(X, y, w)
        out[r] = binomial_deviance(y, mu, w)
    return out

rng = np.random.default_rng(3407)
beta0 = np.array([0.3, 0.9])
G, m = 40, 20                                       # grouped: 40 groups of 20 trials
Xg = np.column_stack([np.ones(G), np.linspace(-1.5, 1.5, G)])
N = 800                                             # ungrouped: 800 single trials
Xu = np.column_stack([np.ones(N), np.linspace(-1.5, 1.5, N)])

reps = 200
Dg = null_deviances(Xg, beta0, m, reps, rng)
Du = null_deviances(Xu, beta0, 1, reps, rng)
print(f"grouped   : mean {Dg.mean():8.2f} (df {G - 2}), sd {Dg.std():6.2f}"
      f" (sqrt(2 df) {np.sqrt(2 * (G - 2)):.2f})")
print(f"ungrouped : mean {Du.mean():8.2f} (df {N - 2}), sd {Du.std():6.2f}"
      f" (sqrt(2 df) {np.sqrt(2 * (N - 2)):.2f})")

Idea. Differences of deviances between nested models are reliable, and are what the analysis-of-deviance table uses. The deviance itself is a goodness-of-fit statistic only for grouped data with large groups. The two claims are constantly confused because software prints them in the same table.

The analysis-of-deviance table is built like the sequential analysis-of-variance table of Definition 9.3.1: add terms in a chosen order, recording the drop in deviance at each step with its degrees of freedom. The reference distribution is chi-squared when ϕ is known and F when it is estimated.

Example (Analysis of deviance for Engel’s data)

Continue the gamma/log model of Example (Food expenditure: three models, two decisions), with u the centred logarithm of income and n=235. Fitting the three nested models gives

model residual df deviance change F p
1 234 39.01
1+u 233 4.27 34.73 1975.2 <10−4
1+u+u2 232 4.18 0.0918 5.22 0.023

Here ϕ is unknown, so each change is divided by ϕ^=0.01758 and referred to F(1,232), following Theorem 34.5.5(a). One qualification: the log link is not the gamma’s canonical link, so (G1) does not hold and the calibration rests not on the proof given but on Proposition 34.5.2, which extends the limit to this case without proving it. Income matters overwhelmingly; the quadratic term is borderline. The residual deviance 4.18 is not to be compared with χ2(232): the data are ungrouped, so Theorem 34.5.5(b) does not apply, and the small value reflects the small dispersion of the gamma fit, not a suspiciously good one.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats

engel = sm.datasets.engel.load_pandas().data
y = engel["foodexp"].to_numpy()
u = np.log(engel["income"].to_numpy())
u = u - u.mean()                                   # centred, so the terms are less collinear
n = len(y)

def gamma_deviance(y, mu):
    """D = 2 sum { -log(y/mu) + (y - mu)/mu } for the gamma family."""
    return 2 * np.sum(-np.log(y / mu) + (y - mu) / mu)

models = {"1": np.ones((n, 1)),
          "1 + u": np.column_stack([np.ones(n), u]),
          "1 + u + u^2": np.column_stack([np.ones(n), u, u ** 2])}
fits, dev = {}, {}
for name, Xm in models.items():
    f = sm.GLM(y, Xm, family=sm.families.Gamma(sm.families.links.Log())).fit()
    fits[name], dev[name] = f, gamma_deviance(y, f.fittedvalues)

full = fits["1 + u + u^2"]
mu_full = full.fittedvalues
phi_pearson = np.sum((y - mu_full) ** 2 / mu_full ** 2) / (n - 3)
phi_deviance = dev["1 + u + u^2"] / (n - 3)

names = list(models)
print(f"{'term added':14s} {'df':>4s} {'deviance':>10s} {'change':>9s} {'F':>8s} {'p':>8s}")
prev = None
for name in names:
    df_res = n - models[name].shape[1]
    row = f"{name:14s} {df_res:4d} {dev[name]:10.4f}"
    if prev is not None:
        change = dev[prev] - dev[name]
        F = change / phi_pearson
        row += f" {change:9.4f} {F:8.2f} {stats.f.sf(F, 1, n - 3):8.4f}"
    print(row)
    prev = name
print(f"dispersion: Pearson {phi_pearson:.5f}   deviance {phi_deviance:.5f}")

34.5.5. Estimating the dispersion

For the binomial and Poisson families ϕ=1 by construction; if the data contradict that, the remedy is a different model, which is Chapter 38. For the normal, gamma and inverse Gaussian families ϕ is free. Maximum likelihood would take it from the full likelihood, which for the gamma involves the digamma function and behaves badly under misspecification. The standard choice is instead the Pearson estimate

ϕ^=X2n−p=1n−p∑i=1nwi(yi−μ^i)2V(μ^i).
(34.5.6)

The motivation is the moment identity E{wi(Yi−μi)2/V(μi)}=ϕ, exact at the true mean; replacing μi by μ^i costs p degrees of freedom, by analogy with s2=SSE/(n−p), the exact normal-theory case (Theorem 5.6.1). The alternative D/(n−p) is also in use; for Engel’s data the two give 0.01758 and 0.01803. They can differ sharply for skewed data, and the Pearson version is the more reliable, since it does not depend on the far tail of the assumed density.

Because the likelihood equations do not involve ϕ (Theorem 34.3.1(a)), estimating it does not disturb 𝛃β^. It does enter every standard error, through A^n−1=ϕ^(X⊤W^X)−1, and its own variability is why the tests are then referred to an F distribution.

34.5.6. Residuals and diagnostics

The raw residual yi−μ^i is uninformative on its own, because its variance depends on μ^i. Three standardizations are in use.

Definition 34.5.2 (Pearson, deviance and Anscombe residuals)

For a fitted generalized linear model, with di the deviance contributions of Eq. 34.5.4 and hii the diagonal of the weighted hat matrix H=W^1/2X(X⊤W^X)−1X⊤W^1/2, define riP=wi(yi−μ^i)ϕ^V(μ^i),riD=sign(yi−μ^i)di/ϕ^,riA=wi{A(yi)−A(μ^i)}ϕ^A′(μ^i)V(μ^i), where A(μ)=∫μV(t)−1/3dt. The standardized versions divide by 1−hii.

The Pearson residuals have squares adding to X2/ϕ^, the deviance residuals squares adding to D/ϕ^; each splits its own discrepancy measure into per-observation pieces, so a large deviance residual marks an observation the likelihood explains badly. The Anscombe residual first applies the transformation that brings the family closest to normal, A(μ)=32μ2/3 for the Poisson and 3μ1/3 for the gamma.

Example (Which residual is closest to normal)

Two thousand observations were simulated from a Poisson log-linear model with fitted means between about 1 and 6, and the model refitted. Figure 34.5.3 shows normal quantile plots of the three residual types. The Pearson residuals are visibly right-skewed, with sample skewness 0.618 — the skewness μ−1/2 of Exercise (B3), averaged over the fitted means. The deviance residuals have skewness −0.010 and the Anscombe residuals −0.081; both are far closer to normal, and neither is uniformly better. Discreteness remains visible in all three as short horizontal runs. The leverages sum to 2 (Proposition 6.8.1(b)) and the largest is 0.0029.

rng = np.random.default_rng(3405)
N = 2000
Xp = np.column_stack([np.ones(N), rng.uniform(-1, 1, N)])
mu_true = np.exp(Xp @ np.array([1.0, 0.8]))                # means between about 1 and 6
yp = rng.poisson(mu_true).astype(float)
pois = sm.GLM(yp, Xp, family=sm.families.Poisson()).fit()
mu_p = pois.fittedvalues

pearson = (yp - mu_p) / np.sqrt(mu_p)                                   # r = (y - mu)/sqrt(V)
d_i = 2 * (np.where(yp > 0, yp * np.log(np.maximum(yp, 1e-300) / mu_p), 0.0) - (yp - mu_p))
deviance_r = np.sign(yp - mu_p) * np.sqrt(d_i)                          # signed root of d_i
anscombe = 1.5 * (yp ** (2 / 3) - mu_p ** (2 / 3)) / mu_p ** (1 / 6)    # A(mu) = 1.5 mu^{2/3}

W = mu_p                                                   # working weights for Poisson/log
root = np.sqrt(W)[:, None] * Xp
H = root @ np.linalg.inv(root.T @ root) @ root.T           # the weighted hat matrix
h = np.diag(H)

for name, r in [("Pearson", pearson), ("deviance", deviance_r), ("Anscombe", anscombe)]:
    print(f"{name:9s} sd {r.std(ddof=2):6.3f}  skewness {stats.skew(r):7.3f}")
print(f"leverages: sum {h.sum():.4f}, largest {h.max():.4f}")

The rest of Chapter 20 transfers under the substitution X↦W^1/2X, which is Pregibon’s (1981) extension of the linear-model diagnostics: the leverages of Proposition 6.8.1, a Cook-type measure Ci=(riP)2hii/{p(1−hii)2} (Definition 20.4.1), and the one-step deletion approximations of Theorem 20.3.1 with the working response in place of y. The exact deletion identities do not, because dropping a case changes the weights too. For ungrouped binary data the residuals take only two values at each μ^i, so only smoothed plots carry a signal — a point Proposition 35.4.1 develops.

34.5.7. Model selection

Because the fit is by maximum likelihood, Definition 29.2.2 applies directly, with 𝛃AIC=−2ℓ(β^)+2d and d=p when ϕ is known, p+1 when it is estimated; for a ϕ=1 family comparing AIC is comparing D+2p. The rest of Chapter 29 transfers unchanged, Proposition 29.5.1 included.

Exercise (A1)

Verify the normal and Poisson rows of the deviance table from Eq. 34.5.4. For the Poisson, show that if the model has an intercept then ∑i(yi−μ^i)=0 by Corollary 34.3.2, so the deviance reduces to 2∑iyilog⁡(yi/μ^i).

Exercise (A2)

A binomial model is fitted to 60 groups with 4 parameters, and the residual deviance is 71.2 on 56 degrees of freedom. When is comparing it with χ2(56) legitimate, and what would you need to know about the data first?

Solution. By Theorem 34.5.5(b) the comparison needs the number of groups fixed and the group sizes large, so one needs the mi: if they are all in the hundreds the comparison is reasonable, and if many are 1 or 2 it is not, whatever the total sample size. A rough rule is that the expected counts miμ^i and mi(1−μ^i) should all exceed about five.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let β^ have asymptotic standard error s. Compute the Wald statistic for H0:β=0 and, using the delta method, the Wald statistic for the equivalent hypothesis H0:eβ=1. Show that their ratio is {(eβ^−1)/β^}2e−2β^, and evaluate it at β^=1 and β^=3. Why is the likelihood ratio statistic unaffected?

Exercise (B2)

Show that E{wi(Yi−μi)2/V(μi)}=ϕ exactly, for every observation in an exponential dispersion family. Deduce that X2/n is unbiased for ϕ when the true means are used, and explain why X2/(n−p) is only approximately unbiased when they are estimated.

Exercise (B3)

Derive the Anscombe residual for the Poisson family from A(μ)=∫μt−1/3dt, and check that the standardizing denominator is μ^1/6. Explain why A is not the variance-stabilizing transformation 2μ of Corollary 22.4.2, and what each one is optimizing.

Solution. A(μ)=32μ2/3, so A′(μ)=μ−1/3 and A′(μ^)V(μ^)=μ^−1/3μ^1/2=μ^1/6, giving rA=32(y2/3−μ^2/3)/μ^1/6. The variance-stabilizing transformation solves A′∝V−1/2 and makes the variance constant; the Anscombe transformation solves A′∝V−1/3 and makes the third cumulant, hence the skewness, vanish to the order considered. Exercise (B4)

Two summaries of predictive power for a generalized linear model are the sample correlation between y and 𝛍μ^, and the likelihood-based RL2=(ℓM−ℓ0)/(ℓsat−ℓ0), where ℓ0, ℓM and ℓsat are the maximized log-likelihoods of the intercept-only model, the fitted model and the saturated model. Show that for a family with ϕ=1 the second is (D0−D)/D0, with D0 the null deviance, and deduce that it lies in [0,1] and never decreases when a term is added. Why does that last property make it a poor basis for choosing a model?

Solution. With ϕ=1, Definition 34.5.1 gives D=2(ℓsat−ℓM) and D0=2(ℓsat−ℓ0), so ℓM−ℓ0=(D0−D)/2 and RL2=(D0−D)/D0. Both deviances are nonnegative by Proposition 34.5.4 and D≤D0, since enlarging a model cannot lower the maximized likelihood; so RL2∈[0,1] and cannot decrease when a term is added. That is the defect of the unadjusted R2 of Section 9.5, the point Proposition 9.5.3 makes there: it rewards complexity unconditionally. AIC instead charges 2 per parameter against −2ℓ, and so can rise.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Extend Theorem 34.5.5(c) to a Poisson log-linear model in which every yi is 0 or 1: show that the deviance is again a function of 𝛃β^ alone. Then show that the argument breaks down as soon as some yi≥2, and explain in words what extra information the larger counts supply. (This is the reason the deviance is a serviceable goodness-of-fit statistic for Poisson data with large means and a useless one for sparse counts.)

Solution. For the Poisson deviance, D=2∑i{yilog⁡(yi/μ^i)−(yi−μ^i)}. When every yi∈{0,1} the term yilog⁡yi is zero, so D=−2∑iyilog⁡μ^i+2∑i(yi−μ^i); the second sum vanishes by Corollary 34.3.2 if the model has an intercept, and the first is 𝛃𝛃𝛍−2β^⊤X⊤y=−2β^⊤X⊤μ^, again by Corollary 34.3.2. So D is a function of 𝛃β^. If some yi≥2, the term ∑iyilog⁡yi is a nonzero function of the data that is not determined by X⊤y: two data sets with the same sufficient statistic, one with counts (2,0) at two identical covariate values and one with (1,1), have the same 𝛃β^ but different deviances. The larger counts carry information about the spread of the responses within a covariate pattern, which is precisely what a goodness-of-fit statistic needs and what binary data cannot supply.

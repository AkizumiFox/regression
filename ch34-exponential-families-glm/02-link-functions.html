34.2  Link functions

The linear model says 𝛃E(Yi)=x(i)⊤β. For a count that must be nonnegative, or a probability that must lie in (0,1), that equation is a promise the right-hand side cannot keep: a linear function of unbounded regressors takes every real value. The resolution is to put a monotone function between the mean and the linear predictor.

34.2.1. The model

Definition 34.2.1 (Generalized linear model)

Let X be an n×p model matrix with rows x(i)⊤, and let w1,…,wn>0 be known prior weights. A generalized linear model consists of

  • (a) a random component: Y1,…,Yn are independent, and Yi has the density Eq. 34.1.1 with natural parameter θi, weight wi and a dispersion ϕ that is the same for all i;

  • (b) a linear predictor 𝛃ηi=x(i)⊤β, with 𝛈𝛃η=Xβ∈Rn;

  • (c) a link function g:M→R, strictly monotone and twice continuously differentiable, with g(μi)=ηi,μi=E(Yi)=b′(θi).

The inverse h=g−1 is the response function, so μi=h(ηi). The admissible set of the model is 𝛃𝛃B={β∈Rp: x(i)⊤β∈g(M) for all i}.

The linear model of Chapter 5 is the case: normal random component, identity link, wi=1, ϕ=σ2, B=Rp. Every classical result of Parts II and III is about that one cell of a large table.

Three remarks. The dispersion is common to all observations: the regressors move the mean, the variance follows through V, and nothing else may vary (modelling ϕ too is the subject of Chapter 45, Definition 45.5.1). The weights wi are known, not estimated, and usually record group sizes. And g transforms the mean, not the data: Chapter 22 transformed Y itself, whereas here the response is left alone and the link acts inside the expectation.

Definition 34.2.2 (Canonical link)

The canonical link of a family is g=(b′)−1, so that θi=ηi: the natural parameter is the linear predictor. Equivalently, g′(μ)=1/V(μ).

Family canonical link g(μ) range of η other links in use
normal identity μ R log, inverse
binomial logit log⁡{μ/(1−μ)} R probit, complementary log–log, identity
Poisson log log⁡μ R identity, square root
gamma inverse −1/μ (−∞,0) log, identity
inverse Gaussian inverse square −1/(2μ2) (−∞,0) log
negative binomial log⁡{μ/(μ+k)} as shown (−∞,0) log

The sign conventions for the gamma and inverse Gaussian are cosmetic: 1/μ and −1/μ give the same fitted values with 𝛃β negated. The column headed “range of η” is not. For the normal, binomial and Poisson the canonical link maps M onto all of R, so B=Rp and no constraint is ever active. For the gamma and inverse Gaussian it maps onto a half-line, and B is the polyhedron 𝛃𝛃{β:x(i)⊤β<0 for all i} — an open convex set with a boundary a fitting algorithm can walk into.

34.2.2. What a link choice costs and buys

Idea. The canonical link is a mathematical convenience, not a scientific claim. It makes the likelihood concave (Theorem 34.3.3), makes observed and expected information agree (Theorem 34.3.1), reduces the data to p sufficient statistics (Section 34.1), and forces the fitted values to reproduce the observed weighted column totals of X exactly (Corollary 34.3.2). None of that is a reason to believe the mean depends on the regressors in that particular way.

Four considerations decide a link in practice.

Range. A link whose range is all of R removes constraints from the fitting problem. The log link for a Poisson response guarantees μ^i>0 for every 𝛃β∈Rp, and the logit link for a binomial response guarantees μ^i∈(0,1). An identity link for either does not.

Example (An identity link close to its boundary)

Eight counts were simulated from a Poisson model with mean 12−1.2x at x=1,…,8. An identity-link Poisson fit recovers the declining mean, and every fitted value is positive — but the fitted line reaches zero at x=9.89, barely outside the range of the data. Predicting at x=10 would return a negative mean, and a slightly different sample would push the boundary inside the observed range, where the likelihood is −∞. The log link has no such boundary.

Interpretation. A log link makes the regressors act multiplicatively, μ=eβ0∏jeβjxj, so βj is a log rate ratio and eβj the factor multiplying the mean per unit of xj. A logit link makes βj a log odds ratio, the subject of Chapter 35 (Definition 35.1.1). An identity link keeps βj a difference of means, often what a policy question asks about. These are different scientific statements, and the choice belongs on the scale where effects are plausibly additive, not on convenience.

Fit and stability. Different links give genuinely different mean curves, and a badly chosen one shows up in the residuals like any other misspecification. Non-canonical links can also produce a log-likelihood with several stationary points, and links with a restricted range need the fitting algorithm to be protected; Section 34.4 returns to this.

Example (Food expenditure: three models, two decisions)

Engel’s classic budget survey records annual income and food expenditure for 235 working-class households. Food expenditure is positive, right-skewed, and visibly more variable at high incomes. Write u=log⁡(income) and fit three models with the same linear predictor β0+β1u: a normal random component with the identity link (ordinary least squares), a gamma random component with the identity link, and a gamma random component with the log link. The first two differ only in the variance function, the last two only in the link.

The identity-link fits give β^1=571.02 (normal) and 474.54 (gamma) francs per unit of log income. The gamma/log fit gives β^1=0.8629, an elasticity: a one per cent rise in income is associated with a 0.86 per cent rise in expected food expenditure. Its estimated dispersion is ϕ^=0.0177, so by Example (The gamma and its constant coefficient of variation) the fitted coefficient of variation is ϕ^=0.133, the same at every income.

Figure 34.2.1 separates the two decisions. In panel (a) the identity-link mean curve is concave in income and flattens, while the log-link curve is a power law that keeps climbing; at the largest income in the sample the two fitted means differ by a factor of 1.786. Panel (b) holds the link at the identity and changes the variance function, so its bands come from the identity-link fits, the gamma one using ϕ^=0.0240 and a coefficient of variation of 0.155. The normal band has constant width and at low incomes dips below zero; the gamma band widens in proportion to the mean, as the data do.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

engel = sm.datasets.engel.load_pandas().data
y = engel["foodexp"].to_numpy()                  # food expenditure
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), np.log(engel["income"].to_numpy())])

gauss = sm.GLM(y, X, family=sm.families.Gaussian()).fit()            # identity link
gam_id = sm.GLM(y, X, family=sm.families.Gamma(sm.families.links.Identity())).fit()
gam_log = sm.GLM(y, X, family=sm.families.Gamma(sm.families.links.Log())).fit()

for name, fit in [("normal/identity", gauss), ("gamma/identity", gam_id), ("gamma/log", gam_log)]:
    print(f"{name:16s} coefficients {fit.params[0]:9.3f} {fit.params[1]:8.3f}"
          f"   dispersion {fit.scale:8.4f}")

34.2.3. Links and transformations are not the same thing

Transforming the response and choosing a link both replace μ by g(μ), but in different places. Modelling 𝛃E{g(Y)}=x⊤β changes the quantity modelled: by Jensen’s inequality E{g(Y)}≠g{E(Y)} unless g is affine, so the fitted curve estimates the mean of a transformed variable, and returning to the mean of Y needs the retransformation correction of Proposition 22.5.1. Modelling 𝛃g{E(Y)}=x⊤β leaves the response alone, and 𝛃μ^i=h(x(i)⊤β^) estimates the mean of Y directly.

The transformation also had a second job the link does not do: stabilizing the variance (Theorem 22.4.1). A generalized linear model does not need that, because the variance function is already specified, and splitting one overloaded decision into two independent ones is the framework’s main practical advantage. The weighted regression on empirical logits of Exercise (B2) is the historical compromise: the right link, but one weighted least squares step in place of the likelihood.

34.2.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Describe the admissible set B of Definition 34.2.1 for (a) a Poisson model with the identity link, (b) a binomial model with the identity link, (c) a gamma model with the log link. Which of the three is all of Rp?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let Y be positive with E(Y)=μ and Var(Y)=ϕμ2, as in the gamma family. Using a second-order Taylor expansion of log⁡Y about μ, show that E(log⁡Y)≈log⁡μ−ϕ/2. Deduce that a least squares regression of log⁡yi on X and a gamma/log generalized linear model estimate intercepts that differ by approximately ϕ/2, while their slopes agree to this order. For the fit of Example (Food expenditure: three models, two decisions), how large is the discrepancy?

Solution. With g(y)=log⁡y, g(Y)≈g(μ)+(Y−μ)/μ−(Y−μ)2/(2μ2); taking expectations gives E(log⁡Y)≈log⁡μ−Var(Y)/(2μ2)=log⁡μ−ϕ/2. Since the correction is the same constant for every observation, it is absorbed by the intercept when the model has one, and the slopes are unaffected to this order. For Engel’s data ϕ^=0.0177, so the two intercepts differ by about ϕ^/2=0.0177/2=0.009, less than one per cent on the expenditure scale. (For the gamma, E(log⁡Y) can be computed exactly with the digamma function; the approximation is good when ϕ is small.)

Exercise (B2)

A Poisson model is sometimes fitted with the square-root link g(μ)=μ, because Theorem 22.4.1 makes Y approximately variance-stabilized. Find B for this link, find dμ/dη, and explain why the link being variance-stabilizing for Y is irrelevant to whether it is a good link.

Solution. g(M)=(0,∞), so 𝛃𝛃B={β:x(i)⊤β>0 ∀i}, and μ=η2 gives dμ/dη=2η=2μ. Variance stabilization matters when the model is fitted by least squares on the transformed data, because least squares wants constant variance. A generalized linear model already knows V(μ)=μ and weights each observation accordingly (Theorem 34.3.1), so the only question left about the link is whether EY is plausibly linear in the regressors.

Exercise (B3)

The complementary log–log link for a binomial response is g(μ)=log⁡{−log⁡(1−μ)}. Show that it is a bijection from (0,1) onto R, compute dμ/dη, and show that it is not symmetric: g(μ)≠−g(1−μ), unlike the logit and probit links. Which tail does it approach more slowly?

Solution. μ↦−log⁡(1−μ) maps (0,1) onto (0,∞) increasingly, and log maps (0,∞) onto R, so g is an increasing bijection with inverse h(η)=1−exp⁡(−eη) and dμ/dη=eηexp⁡(−eη)=(1−μ){−log⁡(1−μ)}. Symmetry would require h(−η)=1−h(η), which fails, for example at η=0, where h(0)=1−e−1=0.632≠12. As η→−∞, μ≈eη→0 exponentially, while as η→+∞, 1−μ=exp⁡(−eη)→0 doubly exponentially: the approach to 1 is far faster than the approach to 0. Chapter 35 derives the link from a proportional-hazards argument (Proposition 35.2.1).

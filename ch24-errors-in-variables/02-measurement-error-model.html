24.2  The classical measurement error model

This section sets up the two versions of the classical model, shows that its normal version cannot identify the slope from the observed data alone, and separates classical error from Berkson error, which does no harm to a linear regression.

24.2.1. Structural and functional models

In a structural model the true values are random draws, as in Theorem 24.1.1. In a functional model they are fixed unknown constants, one nuisance parameter per observation, which cannot be conditioned on because they are not observed. Attenuation is the same in both.

Proposition 24.2.1 (Attenuation with fixed true values)

Let t1,t2,⋯∈Rp be fixed vectors with first coordinate 1, such that n−1∑i=1ntiti⊤→A, a positive definite matrix. Let 𝛉yi=ti⊤θ+εi and si=ti+u~i, where the pairs (u~i,εi) are independent and identically distributed, u~i and εi are independent with mean zero and finite variances, the first coordinate of u~i is zero (so that si keeps the constant and Σ~ has zero first row and column), and Cov(u~i)=Σ~. Then the least squares coefficient vector from regressing y on s converges in probability to 𝛉(A+Σ~)−1Aθ.

Proof. Stack the vectors as rows of T, S and U~. Then 1nS⊤S=1nT⊤T+1n(T⊤U~+U~⊤T)+1nU~⊤U~. The last term tends to Σ~ in probability by the weak law of large numbers. An entry n−1∑itiju~ik of n−1T⊤U~ has mean zero and variance Σ~kkn−2∑itij2→0, so it tends to zero by Chebyshev’s inequality. Hence n−1S⊤S→A+Σ~, which is positive definite. In the same way, 𝛉𝛆𝛆n−1S⊤y=n−1S⊤Tθ+n−1T⊤ε+n−1U~⊤ε. Here n−1S⊤T→A by the argument just given, 𝛆n−1T⊤ε→0 by Chebyshev’s inequality, and 𝛆n−1U~⊤ε→0 by the weak law, since E(u~iεi)=0 by independence. Matrix inversion is continuous at a nonsingular matrix, which finishes the proof.

For a straight line the slope tends to βsx2/(sx2+σu2), where sx2 is the limiting variance of the fixed values: what matters is the spread of the true values relative to the error. Because the number of parameters grows with n, maximum likelihood can fail in the functional model (Example (Many means, one variance), Exercise (C1)).

24.2.2. What the data cannot tell you

In the normal structural model, X∼N(μx,σx2), U∼N(0,σu2) and ε∼N(0,σ2) are independent, W=X+U and Y=α+βX+ε. Then (W,Y) is bivariate normal (Theorem 3.3.1) with mean (μx,α+βμx) and covariance matrix

(σwwσwyσwyσyy)=(σx2+σu2βσx2βσx2β2σx2+σ2).
(24.2.1)

A bivariate normal distribution is fixed by five numbers, but the model has six parameters (μx,α,β,σx2,σu2,σ2), so data on (W,Y) cannot determine all six.

Proposition 24.2.2 (The slope is identified only up to an interval)

Let (W,Y) be bivariate normal with covariance matrix entries σww,σwy,σyy, where σwwσyy>σwy2. If σwy≠0, a number b is the slope of some normal structural model with σx2>0, σu2≥0 and σ2≥0 that produces this distribution iff b lies in the closed interval with end points b1=σwyσww,b2=σyyσwy. If σwy=0, the only such slope is b=0.

Proof. A normal distribution is determined by its mean and covariance matrix, so a model reproduces the distribution iff it reproduces both. The means can always be matched by μx=EW and α=EY−bμx. By Eq. 24.2.1 the covariances are matched iff σx2+σu2=σww,bσx2=σwy,b2σx2+σ2=σyy. If σwy=0, the middle equation with σx2>0 forces b=0, and then σx2∈(0,σww] and σ2=σyy complete a model. If σwy≠0, then b≠0 and the equations force σx2=σwy/b, σu2=σww−σwy/b and σ2=σyy−bσwy. These are admissible iff b has the sign of σwy (so σx2>0), |b|≥|σwy|/σww=|b1| (so σu2≥0) and |b|≤σyy/|σwy|=|b2| (so σ2≥0). Since b1 and b2 share the sign of σwy, these conditions say that b lies between b1 and b2. The interval is not empty, because |b1|≤|b2| is equivalent to σwy2≤σwwσyy.

The end point b1 is the least squares slope of Y on W, correct if there is no measurement error. The end point b2 is the inverted slope of W on Y, correct if there is no equation error. Their ratio is the squared correlation, so the interval is short only when the correlation is high.

Example (The interval for the blood-pressure study)

For the first reading in the running example, the sample covariances are sww=216.21, swy=10.786 and syy=1.889. The compatible slopes form the interval [0.0499,0.1752], and the squared correlation is 0.285. It contains the true 0.08, whose implied variances σx2=134.8, σu2=81.4 and σ2=1.026 are close to the generating 144, 81 and 1, but the data cannot prefer them to those of any other slope in the interval (Figure 24.2.1).

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2401)
n = 300
age = rng.normal(55, 10, n)
x = 130 + 0.6 * (age - 55) + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(108), n)   # long-run blood pressure, sd 12
w = x[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 9, (n, 2))                      # two clinic readings
y = -4 + 0.08 * x + 0.0 * age + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)          # age has no effect given x

S = np.cov(w[:, 0], y)                       # sample covariance matrix of (reading, outcome)
s_ww, s_wy, s_yy = S[0, 0], S[0, 1], S[1, 1]
b_direct = s_wy / s_ww                       # assumes no measurement error (sigma_u^2 = 0)
b_reverse = s_yy / s_wy                      # assumes no equation error (sigma^2 = 0)
print(f"slopes compatible with the data: [{b_direct:.4f}, {b_reverse:.4f}]")

for b in [b_direct, 0.08, b_reverse]:        # the variances each slope would imply
    sx2 = s_wy / b
    print(f"b = {b:.4f}: sigma_x^2 = {sx2:7.2f}, sigma_u^2 = {s_ww - sx2:7.2f}, "
          f"sigma^2 = {s_yy - b * s_wy:.4f}")

The sign of β, whether β=0, and the lower bound |b1| on |β| are identified. Any information that pins down one variance identifies everything: σu2, λ, the ratio σ2/σu2, or a bound λ≥λ0 that narrows the interval (Exercise (A2)). The rest of the chapter is about where such information comes from.

Remark (Nonnormal true values). Reiersøl (1950) showed that when β≠0 and the errors are normal and independent of X, the slope is identified exactly when X is not normal; higher moments then carry the missing information. We do not prove this. Estimators based on higher moments are highly variable unless X is far from normal, and rarely replace external information.

24.2.3. Berkson error

Sometimes the truth scatters around the recorded value instead: a furnace set to w degrees, every resident of a district assigned the district monitor’s pollution level, a solution prepared at a nominal concentration. Then X=W+U,U independent of W, which is Berkson error (Berkson 1950). The difference from classical error is which variable the error is independent of. It cannot be settled from data on (W,Y), only from how the recorded value was produced. The scalar case with fixed settings appeared in Section 19.5; the proposition below allows several regressors and random recorded values.

Proposition 24.2.3 (Berkson error in linear regression)

Let 𝛃Y=α+β⊤x+ε and x=w+u, where w is fixed or random and, given w, the errors u and ε are independent, with means zero, covariance matrix Σuu and variance σ2, not depending on w. Then, given w, 𝛃𝛃𝛃E(Y∣w)=α+β⊤w,Var(Y∣w)=σ2+β⊤Σuuβ, and if u and ε are normal, so is Y given w. Independent observations of this kind therefore satisfy the linear model in the recorded w, with the same coefficients and error variance 𝛃𝛃σ2+β⊤Σuuβ.

Proof. Given w, 𝛃𝛃Y=α+β⊤w+(β⊤u+ε), and the composite error is a linear combination of the independent u and ε (Theorem 2.2.1).

So least squares on the settings is unbiased, and under normality the t and F procedures are exact, with s2 estimating the enlarged error variance. Nothing needs correcting.

Example (Concentrations set on a dial)

Five solutions are prepared at each nominal concentration 10,20,…,60 mg/L (n=30); the achieved concentration adds N(0,42) error, and the response is 2+0.5x+ε, ε∼N(0,32). Regressing on the nominal concentrations in 20000 simulated experiments gives a mean slope of 0.4994 and a mean s2 of 13.01, matching σ2+β2σu2=9+0.25⋅16=13. The nominal 95% t intervals for the slope covered 0.5 in a proportion 0.952 of the experiments.

from scipy import stats

rng_b = np.random.default_rng(2410)
settings = np.repeat([10.0, 20.0, 30.0, 40.0, 50.0, 60.0], 5)      # dial settings, n = 30
Xd = np.column_stack([np.ones(30), settings])
reps = 20_000
achieved = settings + rng_b.normal(0, 4, (reps, 30))               # x = w + u, u independent of w
Y = 2 + 0.5 * achieved + rng_b.normal(0, 3, (reps, 30))
B = np.linalg.solve(Xd.T @ Xd, Xd.T @ Y.T)                          # least squares on the settings
s2 = np.sum((Y.T - Xd @ B) ** 2, axis=0) / 28
se = np.sqrt(s2 * np.linalg.inv(Xd.T @ Xd)[1, 1])
covered = np.abs(B[1] - 0.5) <= stats.t.ppf(0.975, 28) * se
print(f"mean slope {B[1].mean():.4f}, mean s^2 {s2.mean():.2f} (sigma^2 + beta^2 sigma_u^2 = 13), "
      f"coverage {covered.mean():.3f}")

Warning. Berkson error is harmless only because the model is linear in x. For nonlinear g, E{g(x)∣w}≠g(w) in general (Exercise (B1)). Real measurements often mix Berkson and classical components, which must then be handled separately.

24.2.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Classify each regressor error as classical, Berkson or neither, and say whether it biases the slope: (a) weight from a noisy scale; (b) a prescribed dose, when patients take a random fraction of it with mean one; (c) income rounded down to the nearest 10,000.

Exercise (A2)

In Proposition 24.2.2, suppose it is known only that the reliability is at least λ0∈(0,1]. Show that the compatible slopes are those between b1 and min(|b2|,|b1|/λ0) in magnitude, with the sign of b1. Evaluate the interval for Example (The interval for the blood-pressure study) with λ0=0.5.

Solution. The implied reliability of slope b is σx2/σww=b1/b, so λ≥λ0 iff |b|≤|b1|/λ0. Intersect with the interval of the proposition. For the example, b1/λ0=0.0499/0.5=0.0998<0.1752, so the interval shrinks to [0.0499,0.0998], which still contains 0.08.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let Y=β0+β1X+β2X2+β3X3+ε and X=W+U with Berkson error U∼N(0,σu2) independent of W and ε. Compute E(Y∣W). Which coefficients of a cubic regression on W are biased, and by how much? What happens for a quadratic?

Solution. With EU=EU3=0 and EU2=σu2, E(X2∣W)=W2+σu2 and E(X3∣W)=W3+3Wσu2. So E(Y∣W)=(β0+β2σu2)+(β1+3β3σu2)W+β2W2+β3W3. The cubic regression on W estimates the intercept with bias β2σu2 and the linear coefficient with bias 3β3σu2. The top two coefficients are unbiased. For a quadratic (β3=0) only the intercept is biased. The conditional variance also changes with W, so the errors are heteroscedastic.

Exercise (B2)

In the normal structural model, show that the reciprocal of the least squares slope of W on Y converges to b2=β+σ2/(βσx2), so that it overstates |β| exactly when there is equation error. Evaluate b2 for the population of the running example and compare it with Example (The interval for the blood-pressure study).

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In the functional model wi=xi+ui, yi=α+βxi+εi, with ui,εi independent N(0,σ2) (equal, unknown variances) and x1,…,xn unknown constants:

  • (a) show that, for fixed (α,β), maximizing the likelihood over each xi leaves di2=(yi−α−βwi)2/(1+β2), the squared perpendicular distance from (wi,yi) to the line;

  • (b) deduce that the maximum likelihood line is the orthogonal regression line of Section 24.3, and that σ^ML2=(2n)−1∑id^i2;

  • (c) show that σ^ML2→σ2/2 in probability, assuming the line is estimated consistently. Compare Example (Many means, one variance).

Solution. (a) The log likelihood is −nlog⁡(2πσ2)−∑i{(wi−xi)2+(yi−α−βxi)2}/(2σ2). For each i the bracket is the squared distance from (wi,yi) to the point (xi,α+βxi) of the line, and its minimum over xi is the squared perpendicular distance di2. (b) The profile log likelihood is −nlog⁡(2πσ2)−∑idi2/(2σ2). It is maximized over the line by minimizing ∑idi2, which is orthogonal regression, and over σ2 by ∑id^i2/(2n). (c) At the true line, yi−α−βwi=εi−βui has variance σ2(1+β2), so n−1∑idi2→σ2 by the law of large numbers. A consistent estimate of the line gives the same limit, so σ^ML2→σ2/2: each nuisance xi uses up half of its observation’s two degrees of freedom.

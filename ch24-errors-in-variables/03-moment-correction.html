24.3  Reliability and moment correction

This section supplies the missing information of Section 24.2 directly, as a reliability ratio, an error covariance or a variance ratio, and solves Theorem 24.1.1 for 𝛉θ.

24.3.1. Correcting with a known reliability

If λ is known, for instance from a test–retest correlation (Exercise (A1) in Section 24.1), then β^/λ is consistent in simple regression. For correlations this is Spearman’s (1904) correction for attenuation, Corr(X1,X2)=Corr(W1,W2)/λ1λ2 (Exercise (A1)). With covariates the right divisor is λx∣z (Exercise (B1)). Dividing by λ undercorrects and leaves the covariates’ bias untouched.

24.3.2. A known error covariance: the corrected estimator

Work with raw second moments, so the intercept needs no special treatment. Collect the true regressors, including the constant, in t=(1,x⊤,z⊤)⊤∈Rp, and the observed ones in s=t+u~, where u~=(0,u⊤,0⊤)⊤ has zeros for the constant and for the exactly measured z. Write the model as 𝛉Y=t⊤θ+ε. Under classical error, 𝛉E(ss⊤)=E(tt⊤)+Σ~,E(sY)=E(tt⊤)θ,Σ~=E(u~u~⊤), since the cross terms vanish. So 𝛉θ={E(ss⊤)−Σ~}−1E(sY): subtract the error’s share of the moment matrix before inverting. To cover a known Σ~ and one estimated from replicates at once, let observation i carry a matrix Di, computed from its own data, with expectation Σ~. If Σ~ is known, Di=Σ~.

Theorem 24.3.1 (Method-of-moments correction)

Let (ti,u~i,εi,Di), i=1,2,…, be independent copies of (t,u~,ε,D), where si=ti+u~i and 𝛉yi=ti⊤θ+εi are observed, and Di is a symmetric p×p matrix computed from the data of observation i. Assume that E(ε)=0, E(tε)=0, E(u~ε)=0, E(tu~⊤)=0, E(D)=Σ~=E(u~u~⊤), and that A=E(tt⊤) is positive definite. Let

𝛉A^n=1n∑i=1n(sisi⊤−Di),θ^mm=A^n−11n∑i=1nsiyi.
(24.3.1)

  • (a) With probability one, A^n is nonsingular for all large n and 𝛉𝛉θ^mm→θ.

  • (b) If t, u~ and ε have finite fourth moments and D has finite second moments, then 𝛉𝛉n(θ^mm−θ)→Np(0,A−1BA−1)in distribution, where 𝛙𝛙B=E(ψψ⊤) and 𝛙𝛉𝛉ψ=s(y−s⊤θ)+Dθ.

  • (c) Under the conditions of (b), A^n−1B^nA^n−1→A−1BA−1 in probability, where 𝛙𝛙B^n=n−1∑iψ^iψ^i⊤ and 𝛙ψ^i is 𝛙ψi with 𝛉θ^mm in place of 𝛉θ.

Proof. (a) By the strong law of large numbers, n−1∑isisi⊤→E(ss⊤)=A+Σ~ (the cross terms vanish since E(tu~⊤)=0) and n−1∑iDi→Σ~. So A^n→A, which is nonsingular, and A^n is nonsingular for all large n because the determinant is continuous. Also 𝛉𝛉n−1∑isiyi→E{(t+u~)(t⊤θ+ε)}=Aθ. By continuity of the inverse, 𝛉𝛉𝛉θ^mm→A−1Aθ=θ.

(b) Directly from the definitions, 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛙1n∑isiyi−A^nθ=1n∑i{siyi−sisi⊤θ+Diθ}=1n∑iψi, so 𝛉𝛉𝛙n(θ^mm−θ)=A^n−1n−1/2∑iψi. The 𝛙ψi are independent and identically distributed with mean 𝛙𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉E(ψ)=E(sY)−E(ss⊤)θ+E(D)θ=Aθ−(A+Σ~)θ+Σ~θ=0. Each entry of 𝛙ψ is a sum of products of at most two entries of (t,u~,ε), plus a linear function of D, so the moment conditions give B finite entries. By the central limit theorem (the remark in Section 14.4), 𝛏𝛙ξn=n−1/2∑iψi→Np(0,B). Split 𝛏𝛏𝛏A^n−1ξn=A−1ξn+(A^n−1−A−1)ξn.

By continuous mapping (Section 19.3) the first term converges in distribution to A−1Np(0,B)=Np(0,A−1BA−1). Each entry of the second is a sum of products of an entry of A^n−1−A−1, tending to 0, and an entry of 𝛏ξn; by the scalar Slutsky lemma it tends to 0 in probability. The sum form of Slutsky’s lemma finishes the proof.

(c) Write Gi=sisi⊤−Di and 𝛅𝛉𝛉δ=θ^mm−θ. Then 𝛙𝛙𝛅ψ^i=ψi−Giδ and 𝛙𝛙𝛙𝛅𝛅𝛙𝛅𝛅B^n=1n∑iψiψi⊤−1n∑i(ψiδ⊤Gi⊤+Giδψi⊤)+1n∑iGiδδ⊤Gi⊤. The first term tends to B by the strong law. Every entry of the second is at most 𝛅𝛙2‖δ‖n−1∑i‖ψi‖‖Gi‖ in absolute value (with the Frobenius norm for Gi). The average converges to 𝛙E(‖ψ‖‖G‖), which is finite by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and 𝛅‖δ‖→0 by (a). Similarly the third term is at most 𝛅‖δ‖2n−1∑i‖Gi‖2→0. Combine with A^n→A.

In simple regression with known error variance, Eq. 24.3.1 is swy/(sww−σu2), with sample moments taken with divisor n, the naive slope divided by the estimated reliability. The covariance A−1BA−1 has the sandwich form of Theorem 21.4.2, to which it reduces when u~=0 and D=0. No assumption about the distribution of X or homoscedasticity is needed.

Replicates. Suppose each unit has m≥2 readings wij=xi+uij, j=1,…,m, with errors independent across j, independent of (xi,zi,εi), and with covariance Σuu. Use the mean w¯i as the observed regressor. Its error u¯i has covariance Σuu/m, and an unbiased estimate of that covariance from unit i alone is 1m(m−1)∑j=1m(wij−w¯i)(wij−w¯i)⊤, padded with zeros to make Di (Exercise (A2)). For m=2 and a scalar regressor this is (wi1−wi2)2/4. Because Di enters 𝛙ψi, the sandwich of (c) includes the uncertainty from estimating the error variance. An external estimate treated as known would need that uncertainty added separately.

Example (Correcting with two readings)

In the running example, regress y on (1,w¯,age), where w¯ is the mean of the two readings. The reliability of the mean is 144/(144+40.5)=0.780, and given age it is 108/(108+40.5)=0.727. The naive coefficients are 0.0562 (standard error 0.0054) for blood pressure and 0.0120 (standard error 0.0070) for age. The replicates estimate σu2 as 74.1 (true 81), and Eq. 24.3.1 gives β~x=0.0757 (0.0076),β~age=0.0018 (0.0076), with sandwich standard errors. The 95% interval for βx is [0.0608,0.0905], and the spurious age effect is gone. The standard error is 1.40 times the naive one: the bias is paid for in variance. With σu2=81 known the estimate would be 0.0782. In 10000 repeated samples the corrected intervals covered 0.08 in a proportion 0.949, the naive ones in 0.013.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2401)
n = 300
age = rng.normal(55, 10, n)
x = 130 + 0.6 * (age - 55) + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(108), n)   # long-run blood pressure, sd 12
w = x[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 9, (n, 2))                      # two clinic readings
y = -4 + 0.08 * x + 0.0 * age + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)          # age has no effect given x

def corrected(w, age, y):
    """Moment-corrected coefficients of (1, x, age) and their sandwich standard errors."""
    n = len(y)
    W = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), w.mean(axis=1), age])   # observed regressors
    D = np.zeros((n, 3, 3))
    D[:, 1, 1] = (w[:, 0] - w[:, 1]) ** 2 / 4                  # E D_i = Var(error of the mean)
    A = (W.T @ W - D.sum(axis=0)) / n
    theta = np.linalg.solve(A, W.T @ y / n)
    psi = W * (y - W @ theta)[:, None] + D @ theta               # estimating function, one row per patient
    B = psi.T @ psi / n
    Ainv = np.linalg.inv(A)
    cov = Ainv @ B @ Ainv / n
    return theta, np.sqrt(np.diag(cov))

theta, se = corrected(w, age, y)
W = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), w.mean(axis=1), age])
naive = np.linalg.lstsq(W, y, rcond=None)[0]
s2_u = np.mean((w[:, 0] - w[:, 1]) ** 2) / 2                   # estimate of sigma_u^2 (true 81)
print(f"sigma_u^2 estimate {s2_u:.1f}")
print("naive     (1, x, age):", naive.round(4))
print("corrected (1, x, age):", theta.round(4), " se:", se.round(4))
def coverage(reps, seed):
    """Coverage of nominal 95% intervals for beta_x = 0.08: corrected (sandwich) and naive."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    hits_c = hits_n = 0
    for _ in range(reps):
        a = r.normal(55, 10, n)
        xx = 130 + 0.6 * (a - 55) + r.normal(0, np.sqrt(108), n)
        ww = xx[:, None] + r.normal(0, 9, (n, 2))
        yy = -4 + 0.08 * xx + r.normal(0, 1.0, n)
        th, s = corrected(ww, a, yy)
        hits_c += abs(th[1] - 0.08) <= 1.96 * s[1]
        Wr = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), ww.mean(axis=1), a])
        b = np.linalg.lstsq(Wr, yy, rcond=None)[0]
        e = yy - Wr @ b
        sb = np.sqrt(e @ e / (n - 3) * np.linalg.inv(Wr.T @ Wr)[1, 1])
        hits_n += abs(b[1] - 0.08) <= 1.96 * sb
    return hits_c / reps, hits_n / reps

print("coverage (corrected, naive):", coverage(500, 2421))

Warning. The theorem is asymptotic, and the correction is fragile when the reliability is low. A^n can fail to be positive definite, and under normality the corrected slope has no finite mean (Exercise (C1)), as with Proposition 12.5.2; Fuller (1987) gives modified estimators. Gleser and Hwang (1987) showed that, as in Proposition 12.5.4, any confidence set with guaranteed coverage has infinite expected length. Wald intervals are reliable only when the estimated reliability is well away from zero.

24.3.3. Deming and orthogonal regression

Sometimes only a ratio of error variances is known. In a method-comparison study two instruments measure the same specimens, W=X+U and Y=α+βX+ε, with ε the second instrument’s error, and repeatability experiments give δ=Var(ε)Var(U). The question is whether α=0 and β=1.

Proposition 24.3.2 (Deming regression)

Let (W,Y) have covariances σww,σwy,σyy with σwy≠0 and σwwσyy≥σwy2, and let δ>0 be known.

  • (a) The equations σww=σx2+σu2, σwy=βσx2, σyy=β2σx2+δσu2 have exactly one solution with σx2>0, namely

    βδ=σyy−δσww+(σyy−δσww)2+4δσwy22σwy,
    (24.3.2)

    and for it σu2≥0. The slope βδ lies in the interval of Proposition 24.2.2. It tends to b2 as δ→0 and to b1 as δ→∞.

  • (b) For δ=1, β1 is the slope of the line that minimizes the mean squared perpendicular distance from (W,Y), whose direction is an eigenvector of the covariance matrix of (W,Y) for its largest eigenvalue. For general δ, βδ is δ times the δ=1 slope computed for (W,Y/δ).

Proof. (a) For β≠0 the first two equations give σx2=σwy/β and σu2=σww−σwy/β. Substituting in the third and multiplying by β, q(β)=σwyβ2+(δσww−σyy)β−δσwy=0. The product of the roots is −δ<0, so there is one root of each sign, and σx2>0 selects the root with the sign of σwy. That root is Eq. 24.3.2. Take σwy>0 (otherwise replace Y by −Y). Then q(b1)=σwyσww2(σwy2−σwwσyy)≤0,q(b2)=δσwy(σwwσyy−σwy2)≥0, and q is an upward parabola with q(0)<0, so its positive root lies in [b1,b2]. Then σu2=σww(1−b1/βδ)≥0. As δ→0 the formula tends to (σyy+σyy)/(2σwy)=b2. Multiplying the numerator and denominator of Eq. 24.3.2 by the conjugate of the numerator gives βδ=2δσwy/{(σyy−δσww)2+4δσwy2+δσww−σyy}, and the denominator is 2δσww+O(1), so βδ→b1 as δ→∞.

(b) Let Σ be the covariance matrix of v=(W,Y)⊤. A line through the point Ev with unit normal vector n has mean squared perpendicular distance E{n⊤(v−Ev)}2=n⊤Σn, and a line through any other point c adds {n⊤(Ev−c)}2≥0. By Theorem 1.7.7, n⊤Σn is minimized by an eigenvector for the smallest eigenvalue, so the line’s direction is an eigenvector (v1,v2) for the largest eigenvalue μ. From (σww−μ)v1+σwyv2=0, the slope is v2/v1=(μ−σww)/σwy. Put b=v2/v1. Then μ−σww=bσwy, and the second eigenvector equation σwyv1+(σyy−μ)v2=0 gives μ−σyy=σwy/b. Subtracting, σyy−σww=bσwy−σwy/b, which is q(b)=0 with δ=1. Since μ≥σww, b has the sign of σwy, so b=β1. For the last claim, substitute σyy/δ and σwy/δ for σyy and σwy in Eq. 24.3.2 with δ=1 and multiply by δ.

With sample moments, Eq. 24.3.2 is Deming regression, consistent because it is continuous where σwy≠0. With δ=1 it is orthogonal regression (total least squares), whose line is the first principal axis of the scatter.

Warning. Orthogonal regression makes sense only when the errors have equal variances in the units used. Changing the units of Y changes the fitted line by more than a change of scale. Deming regression, with δ rescaled along with the units, does not have this problem.

Example (Comparing two assays)

Sixty specimens with true concentrations uniform on [2,20] are measured by method A with error standard deviation 0.8 and by method B, which reads 0.3+1.05x with error standard deviation 1.0, so δ=1.5625. The four slopes are

Method Slope
least squares of B on A 1.0042
Deming, δ=1.5625 1.0308
orthogonal (δ=1, unjustified) 1.0382
least squares of A on B, inverted 1.0709

with Deming intercept 0.482. Over 20000 simulated studies the mean slopes were 1.0262 for least squares, attenuated by 27/(27+0.64)=0.9768, and 1.0505 for Deming. With B in units ten times smaller, orthogonal regression gives 1.0703 (converted back to the original units) instead of 1.0382.

import numpy as np


def deming(w, y, delta):
    """Slope and intercept of Deming regression; delta = Var(error in y) / Var(error in w)."""
    S = np.cov(w, y)
    s_ww, s_wy, s_yy = S[0, 0], S[0, 1], S[1, 1]
    d = s_yy - delta * s_ww
    b = (d + np.sqrt(d ** 2 + 4 * delta * s_wy ** 2)) / (2 * s_wy)
    return b, y.mean() - b * w.mean()


rng = np.random.default_rng(2430)
x = rng.uniform(2, 20, 60)                        # true concentrations
w = x + rng.normal(0, 0.8, 60)                    # method A
y = 0.3 + 1.05 * x + rng.normal(0, 1.0, 60)       # method B
delta = 1.0 ** 2 / 0.8 ** 2

b_ls = np.polyfit(w, y, 1)[0]                     # regress y on w
b_rev = 1 / np.polyfit(y, w, 1)[0]                # regress w on y, inverted
b_dem, a_dem = deming(w, y, delta)
b_orth, _ = deming(w, y, 1.0)                     # orthogonal regression
print(f"least squares {b_ls:.4f}, Deming {b_dem:.4f} (intercept {a_dem:.3f}), "
      f"orthogonal {b_orth:.4f}, reverse {b_rev:.4f}")

24.3.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Prove Spearman’s correction: if Wk=Xk+Uk for k=1,2, with classical errors independent of each other and of (X1,X2), then Corr(W1,W2)=λ1λ2Corr(X1,X2).

Exercise (A2)

Show that the matrix Di defined from m replicates has expectation Σuu/m, the covariance matrix of u¯i, padded with zeros.

Solution. wij−w¯i=uij−u¯i, so xi drops out. For independent vectors with common covariance Σuu, the sample covariance matrix (m−1)−1∑j(uij−u¯i)(uij−u¯i)⊤ is unbiased for Σuu (Proposition 2.7.1). Dividing by m gives expectation Σuu/m=Cov(u¯i).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

For a scalar X and a scalar exactly measured Z, show that λx∣z=λ−ρwz21−ρwz2, where ρwz is the correlation between W and Z. Check the formula on the running example, and deduce that λx∣z<λ whenever ρwz≠0 and λ<1.

Solution. σx⋅z2=σx2−σxz2/σz2, and since σwz=σxz, σxz2/σz2=ρwz2σw2. Divide the numerator and the denominator of λx∣z=σx⋅z2/(σx⋅z2+σu2) by σw2. The numerator becomes λ−ρwz2 and the denominator λ−ρwz2+(1−λ)=1−ρwz2. In the example ρwz2=602/(225⋅100)=0.16, so λx∣z=(0.64−0.16)/0.84=0.571, as in Section 24.1. Finally (λ−ρ2)/(1−ρ2)<λ iff λ−ρ2<λ−λρ2 iff ρ2(1−λ)>0.

Exercise (B2)

In simple regression with centred variables and known σu2, take D=diag(0,σu2) in Theorem 24.3.1 and suppose (X,U,ε) are jointly normal and independent. Show that the asymptotic variance of n(β~−β) is σw2σv2+β2σu4σx4,σv2=σ2+β2σu2. Compare it with σ2/σx2, the asymptotic variance when X is observed. What does this give for the running example with the mean of two readings?

Solution. The slope’s estimating function (the intercept decouples for centred variables) is ψ=W(Y−Wβ)+σu2β=Wv+σu2β with v=ε−βU, and the slope entry of A is σx2. Now (W,v) is bivariate normal with Cov(W,v)=−βσu2, and for such a pair E(W2v2)=σw2σv2+2Cov(W,v)2. So Var(ψ)=E(W2v2)−{E(Wv)}2=σw2σv2+β2σu4, and the sandwich is Var(ψ)/σx4. It exceeds σ2/σx2 because σw2σv2>σx2σ2. For the example, use σu2=40.5 for the mean of two readings: σw2=184.5, σv2=1+0.0064⋅40.5=1.2592, and the variance is (184.5⋅1.2592+0.0064⋅1640.25)/1442≈0.01171, a standard error of about 0.01171/300≈0.0062, below the 0.0076 of Example (Correcting with two readings), where σu2 is estimated and age lowers the reliability.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In the normal structural model with known σu2 and n≥3, show that β~=swy/(sww−σu2) has E|β~|=∞. (Condition on W1,…,Wn, and use the fact that sww has a continuous density that is positive at σu2.)

Solution. Given the Wi, (Wi,Yi) is jointly normal and E(Y∣W)=a+λβW with constant conditional variance τ2>0 (Theorem 3.5.2). So, given the Wi, swy∼N(λβsww,τ2sww/(n−1)), and E(|swy|∣W)≥τ2sww/{π(n−1)}, because a normal variable’s absolute mean is at least that of its centred version. Hence E|β~|≥cE{sww/|sww−σu2|}. Here (n−1)sww/σw2∼χ2(n−1), whose density is continuous and positive on (0,∞). So the density of sww is bounded below near σu2, and the integral of 1/|s−σu2| diverges there.

24.1  Attenuation bias

When a regressor is recorded with error, conditioning on the recorded value is still legitimate, but it describes how the response varies with the reading, not with the underlying quantity. This section measures the difference.

24.1.1. A running example

One simulated study is used throughout the chapter. For n=300 patients, let xi be long-run systolic blood pressure, the average that many visits would give, with mean 130 mmHg and standard deviation 12. Age has mean 55 years, standard deviation 10, and correlation 0.5 with xi. Each patient has two clinic readings, wij=xi+uij, with independent errors uij∼N(0,92). The outcome is yi=−4+0.08xi+0⋅agei+εi,εi∼N(0,1), so age has no effect given long-run pressure. In a real study xi is unseen; here it lets every method be checked.

24.1.2. Classical error

Throughout the chapter, a regression model is stated in terms of true regressors x (the ones measured with error) and z (the ones measured exactly):

𝛃𝛃Y=α+βx⊤x+βz⊤z+ε,
(24.1.1)

where ε has mean zero and variance σ2 and is uncorrelated with (x,z). We write a generic observation without an index. The data are n independent copies of it unless stated otherwise.

Definition 24.1.1 (Classical measurement error)

Suppose x is observed only through w=x+u. The error u is classical if E(u)=0, Cov(u)=Σuu, and u is independent of (x,z,ε).

The error is additive and unrelated to the true value, and nondifferential: independent of ε, so the reading says nothing about the response beyond what x says. (People with a disease may recall past exposures differently, a differential error.) This section uses only that u has mean zero and is uncorrelated with x, z and ε.

Definition 24.1.2 (Reliability ratio)

The reliability ratio of W=X+U is λ=σx2σx2+σu2=Var(X)Var(W). If z is measured exactly, let 𝛔𝛔σx⋅z2=σx2−σzx⊤Σzz−1σzx be the residual variance of X after its best linear prediction from z (Theorem 6.11.1), where 𝛔σzx=Cov(z,X). The reliability given z is λx∣z=σx⋅z2σx⋅z2+σu2.

In the running example a single reading has λ=144/(144+81)=0.64. Age explains a quarter of the variance of long-run pressure, leaving σx⋅z2=108, so λx∣z=108/189=0.571. Covariates remove part of the signal in W and none of the noise, so λx∣z≤λ.

24.1.3. Least squares on the reading

Substituting X=W−U in Y=α+βX+ε gives

Y=α+βW+(ε−βU).
(24.1.2)

Its error is correlated with the regressor, Cov(W,ε−βU)=−βσu2, so least squares does not estimate β. It estimates the population projection of Y on the recorded regressors (Proposition 6.11.3), which the next theorem computes.

Theorem 24.1.1 (Attenuation)

Let (x(i),z(i),u(i),εi), i=1,2,…, be independent copies of (x,z,u,ε) with finite second moments, satisfying Eq. 24.1.1 and Definition 24.1.1. Write t=(x⊤,z⊤)⊤ for the true regressors, s=(w⊤,z⊤)⊤ for the observed ones, 𝛉𝛃𝛃θ=(βx⊤,βz⊤)⊤, Σtt=Cov(t), assumed positive definite, and Σ~=diag(Σuu,0) for the covariance matrix of the error in s. Let α^n and 𝛉θ^n be the least squares intercept and slopes from regressing Y on s in the first n observations. Then, with probability one:

  • (a) 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉θ^n→θw=(Σtt+Σ~)−1Σttθ=θ−(Σtt+Σ~)−1Σ~θ, and 𝛍𝛉𝛉α^n→α+μt⊤(θ−θw), where 𝛍μt=E(t);

  • (b) with a single regressor and no z, the slope converges to λβ;

  • (c) with a single mismeasured scalar X and exactly measured z, the coefficient of W converges to λx∣zβx and the coefficients of z converge to 𝛃𝛑βz+(1−λx∣z)βxπ, where 𝛑𝛔π=Σzz−1σzx is the vector of coefficients from the best linear prediction of X from z (Theorem 2.5.2);

  • (d) the residual sum of squares divided by n (or by n minus the number of coefficients) converges to 𝛉𝛉σ2+θ⊤Σtt(Σtt+Σ~)−1Σ~θ, which in case (b) equals σ2+λβ2σu2.

Proof. Because u is uncorrelated with t, Cov(s)=Σtt+Σ~, which is positive definite as the sum of a positive definite and a nonnegative definite matrix. By Proposition 6.11.3, the least squares coefficients converge with probability one to those of the best linear predictor of Y from s, which by Theorem 6.11.1 has slopes Cov(s)−1Cov(s,Y) and intercept 𝛉EY−θw⊤Es. Now 𝛉𝛉Cov(s,Y)=Cov(t+(u⊤,0⊤)⊤, t⊤θ+ε)=Σttθ, since u is uncorrelated with t and ε, and ε with t. This is the first form in (a). The second follows from Σtt=(Σtt+Σ~)−Σ~. For the intercept, 𝛍Es=μt and 𝛉𝛍EY=α+θ⊤μt.

(b) With scalars, θw=σx2β/(σx2+σu2)=λβ.

(c) Here 𝛉Σ~θ=σu2βxe1, so by the second form in (a) the bias 𝛉𝛉θ−θw is σu2βx times the first column of Cov(s)−1. Order the observed regressors as (z,W) for the moment, so that 𝛔𝛔Cov(s)=(Σzzσzxσzx⊤σx2+σu2). The Schur complement of Σzz is 𝛔𝛔σx2+σu2−σzx⊤Σzz−1σzx=σx⋅z2+σu2. By Theorem 1.6.4 the last column of the inverse is 𝛑(−π⊤,1)⊤/(σx⋅z2+σu2). So the bias in the coefficient of W is σu2βx/(σx⋅z2+σu2)=(1−λx∣z)βx, and the bias in the coefficients of z is 𝛑−(1−λx∣z)βxπ.

(d) The residual sum of squares over n is 𝛉n−1∑i(Yi−α^n−θ^n⊤si)2. Expanded, it is a continuous function of the coefficients and of second sample moments, which converge by (a) and the strong law, so the limit is 𝛉E(Y−αw−θw⊤s)2, the mean squared error of the best linear predictor. By Eq. 6.11.1 it equals 𝛉𝛉Var(Y)−θw⊤Cov(s)θw, which is 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉σ2+θ⊤Σttθ−θ⊤Σtt(Σtt+Σ~)−1Σttθ=σ2+θ⊤Σtt(Σtt+Σ~)−1Σ~θ. In case (b) the last term is β2σx2σu2/(σx2+σu2)=λβ2σu2.

Part (b) is attenuation: the slope is shrunk towards zero by the reliability ratio, whatever the sample size. More data give a more precise estimate of the wrong number λβ.

Example (One reading instead of long-run pressure)

In the running example, regressing y on the true x gives slope 0.0755 (standard error 0.0050). Regressing on the first clinic reading gives 0.0499 (standard error 0.0046), a ratio of 0.661, close to λ=0.64; the limit is λβ=0.0512. The usual 95% interval, [0.0409,0.0589], misses 0.08, and in 4000 repeated samples the observed coverage was 0.000. The standard error measures variability around λβ, not the distance from λβ to β. The residual variance tends to 1+0.64⋅0.082⋅81=1.3318 by Theorem 24.1.1(d).

24.1.4. Several regressors: bias spreads

Part (c) is more troubling. Correctly measured covariates lower the relevant reliability to λx∣z, and they inherit part of the effect of X. The part of X that the reading misses acts like an omitted regressor (Theorem 19.1.2), and covariates correlated with X pick up its effect in proportion to 𝛑π. This is residual confounding: adjusting for a noisy measurement of a confounder only partly adjusts for it.

Example (Age looks important)

Add age to the regression on the first reading. Theory predicts a coefficient of λx∣zβ=0.0457 for the reading and (1−0.571)⋅0.08⋅0.6=0.0206 per year for age, which truly has no effect. The sample gives 0.0455 for the reading and 0.0182 for age, with standard error 0.0070 and p-value 0.0098. In 4000 repeated samples the t test of “no age effect” rejected at the 5% level in a proportion 0.812 of them. Figure 24.1.1(b) shows both limits as functions of the reliability.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

rng = np.random.default_rng(2401)
n = 300
age = rng.normal(55, 10, n)
x = 130 + 0.6 * (age - 55) + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(108), n)   # long-run blood pressure, sd 12
w = x[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 9, (n, 2))                      # two clinic readings
y = -4 + 0.08 * x + 0.0 * age + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)          # age has no effect given x

oracle = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x)).fit()                   # uses the true x
naive = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(w[:, 0])).fit()              # uses one reading
lam = 144 / (144 + 81)                                         # reliability of one reading
print(f"slope on x:          {oracle.params[1]:.4f}")
print(f"slope on reading:    {naive.params[1]:.4f}   limit lambda*beta = {lam * 0.08:.4f}")
print("naive 95% interval:", naive.conf_int()[1].round(4))

both = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(np.column_stack([w[:, 0], age]))).fit()
print(f"with age: reading {both.params[1]:.4f}, age {both.params[2]:.4f} (p = {both.pvalues[2]:.4f})")

The repeated-sample figures come from this loop (with fewer samples in the cell).

def repeat(reps, seed):
    """Coverage of the naive 95% interval for the slope, and the rate at which age is 'significant'."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    cover = reject = 0
    for _ in range(reps):
        a = r.normal(55, 10, n)
        xx = 130 + 0.6 * (a - 55) + r.normal(0, np.sqrt(108), n)
        ww = xx + r.normal(0, 9, n)
        yy = -4 + 0.08 * xx + r.normal(0, 1.0, n)
        lo, hi = sm.OLS(yy, sm.add_constant(ww)).fit().conf_int()[1]
        cover += lo <= 0.08 <= hi
        reject += sm.OLS(yy, sm.add_constant(np.column_stack([ww, a]))).fit().pvalues[2] < 0.05
    return cover / reps, reject / reps

print("coverage of naive interval, rate of rejecting 'no age effect':", repeat(200, 2404))

With several mismeasured regressors, (Σtt+Σ~)−1Σtt need not shrink each coefficient. A coefficient can grow, change sign, or appear from nothing (Exercise (B3)).

24.1.5. What survives

Two things survive classical error intact.

Error in the response. Suppose instead that the response is measured with error, Y∗=Y+V, where V has mean zero and variance σv2 and is independent of the regressors and of ε.

Proposition 24.1.2 (Error in the response)

Under these assumptions 𝛃Y∗=α+β⊤x+(ε+V), a linear model with the same coefficients and error variance σ2+σv2. If ε and V are normal, so is their sum, and all the exact theory of Parts II and III applies to Y∗ with σ2 replaced by σ2+σv2.

The proof is the display. Error in the response costs precision, not bias, unless it depends on the regressors (heavier people under-reporting weight) or is correlated with a regressor’s error (both from the same self-report).

Tests of no effect. Since λβ=0 iff β=0, attenuation can hide an effect of the mismeasured regressor but cannot create one. The protection does not extend to the covariates.

Proposition 24.1.3 (Which tests survive classical error)

Assume Eq. 24.1.1 and Definition 24.1.1 with a single mismeasured scalar X, ε∼N(0,σ2) independent of (X,z), and the covariance matrix of (X,z⊤)⊤ positive definite. Consider the least squares regression of Y on (1,W,z).

  • (a) If βx=0, the usual t test of “the coefficient of W is zero” has exactly its nominal level.

  • (b) If βx≠0 and 𝛑π≠0, the usual F test of “the coefficients of z equal 𝛃βz” rejects with probability tending to one as n→∞.

Proof. (a) If βx=0, then 𝛃Y=α+βz⊤z+ε, and ε is independent of (W,z) because it is independent of (X,z,U): it is independent of (X,z) by hypothesis, and U is independent of (X,z,ε) by Definition 24.1.1. So, conditionally on the observed regressors, Y follows the normal linear model of Definition 14.1.1 with regressors (1,W,z), in which the coefficient of W is zero. Theorem 14.1.2 gives the exact t distribution.

(b) By Theorem 24.1.1(c), 𝛃𝛃𝛑β^z−βz→c=(1−λx∣z)βxπ≠0. The F statistic is 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃n(β^z−βz)⊤Kn(β^z−βz), where Kn=[n(S⊤S)−1]zz−1/(qs2), S is the observed model matrix and q the dimension of z. By the strong law and Theorem 24.1.1(d), Kn converges to a positive definite limit, so the statistic grows like n, while the critical value tends to a finite limit.

So “is there any association with the underlying quantity?” can be tested with the reading, with reduced power. “Does this covariate matter once the underlying quantity is accounted for?” cannot.

24.1.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that λ=Corr(W,X)2. If W1=X+U1 and W2=X+U2 are two readings with independent classical errors of the same variance, show that Corr(W1,W2)=λ. This is why test–retest correlations are used as reliability estimates.

Solution. Cov(W,X)=σx2, so Corr(W,X)2=σx4/{σx2(σx2+σu2)}=λ. For two readings, Cov(W1,W2)=Var(X)=σx2 because the errors are independent of each other and of X, and Var(W1)=Var(W2)=σx2+σu2. The correlation is σx2/(σx2+σu2)=λ.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let W¯ be the mean of m readings with independent classical errors of variance σu2, and let λ be the reliability of one reading. Show that the reliability of W¯ is λm=mλ1+(m−1)λ. How many readings are needed in the running example for the attenuation factor to reach 0.9?

Solution. λm=σx2/(σx2+σu2/m). Dividing through by σx2+σu2 and using σu2/(σx2+σu2)=1−λ gives λ/{λ+(1−λ)/m}, the formula. With λ=0.64, λm≥0.9 iff 0.64m≥0.9+0.576(m−1), so m≥5.0625: six readings (without age in the model).

Exercise (B2)

In the setting of Theorem 24.1.1(b), show that the population R2 of Y on W equals λ times the population R2 of Y on X. Measurement error lowers the apparent strength of a relation twice: once through the slope and once through the residual variance.

Exercise (B3)

Let t=(X1,X2) have unit variances and correlation ρ, let X1 be measured exactly and X2 with classical error of variance σu2, and let 𝛉θ=(β1,β2). Show that the limit of the coefficient of X1 is β1+(1−λ2∣1)ρβ2, where λ2∣1=(1−ρ2)/(1−ρ2+σu2). With ρ=0.5, σu2=1, β2=1 and β1=−0.2, show that the sign of the first coefficient is reversed.

Solution. This is Theorem 24.1.1(c) with z=X1, X=X2 and 𝛑π=ρ, since the coefficient of the best linear prediction of X2 from X1 is ρ and σx⋅z2=1−ρ2. With the numbers given, λ2∣1=0.75/1.75=3/7, so the limit is −0.2+(4/7)(0.5)=−0.2+2/7≈0.086>0. A regressor with a negative effect acquires a positive coefficient.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Regressors are often recorded after rounding. Model this as S=T+Δ with T the true model matrix and Δ a fixed matrix of rounding errors, and suppose 𝛉E(Y)=Tθ, Cov(Y)=σ2I. Use Proposition 5.5.7 to show that least squares on S has bias 𝛉−(S⊤S)−1S⊤Δθ, that its covariance matrix is exactly σ2(S⊤S)−1, and that the expected residual mean square is at least σ2.

Solution. Here 𝛉𝛅E(Y)=Sθ+δ with 𝛅𝛉δ=−Δθ. By Proposition 5.5.7(a) with model matrix S, the expectation is 𝛉𝛅θ+(S⊤S)−1S⊤δ, which gives the bias. The covariance is σ2(S⊤S)−1 by Proposition 5.5.7(b) with Σ=σ2I: nothing random has been added. For the residual mean square, Theorem 2.3.1 gives 𝛅𝛅E‖(I−MS)Y‖2=σ2(n−p)+δ⊤(I−MS)δ, where MS projects onto C(S) and 𝛉(I−MS)Sθ=0. The last term is nonnegative. Unlike random error, fixed rounding error does not inflate the variance of the estimator. It only biases it, and the bias is small when rounding is fine compared with the spread of the regressors.

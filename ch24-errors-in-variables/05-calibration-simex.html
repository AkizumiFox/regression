24.5  Regression calibration and SIMEX

The moment correction and IV are tied to linear moment equations. Two more portable ideas were designed for logistic, Cox and other nonlinear regressions, where they are approximations. Regression calibration replaces the unobserved regressor by its best prediction from what was observed. SIMEX watches the naive estimate deteriorate as error is added, and extrapolates back to no error. In the linear model both can be exact.

24.5.1. Regression calibration

Error is nondifferential if the distribution of Y given (x,z,w) does not depend on w, as for classical error.

Proposition 24.5.1 (Regression calibration)

Suppose 𝛃𝛃E(Y∣x,z)=α+βx⊤x+βz⊤z and the error is nondifferential, and let m(w,z)=E(x∣w,z).

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃E(Y∣w,z)=α+βx⊤m(w,z)+βz⊤z.

  • (b) If moreover Var(Y∣x,z)=σ2 and Cov(x∣w,z)=C does not depend on (w,z), as when (x,z,u) is jointly normal, then 𝛃𝛃Var(Y∣w,z)=σ2+βx⊤Cβx. The regression of Y on (1,m(w,z),z) is then a homoscedastic linear model with the original coefficients.

  • (c) Without distributional assumptions, let the error be classical with finite second moments. With t=(1,x⊤,z⊤)⊤, s=(1,w⊤,z⊤)⊤, A=E(tt⊤) positive definite and S=E(ss⊤), the best linear predictor of t from s is t^=AS−1s, and the best linear predictor of Y from t^ is 𝛉θ⊤t^, where 𝛉𝛃𝛃θ=(α,βx⊤,βz⊤)⊤.

  • (d) The sample version of (c) reproduces the method-of-moments estimator exactly. If S^=n−1∑isisi⊤, A^=S^−D for a symmetric matrix D with A^ nonsingular, and t^i=A^S^−1si, then the least squares coefficients of y on the t^i are A^−1n−1∑isiyi, which is Eq. 24.3.1 with Di=D.

Proof. (a) By the tower property and nondifferential error, 𝛃𝛃E(Y∣w,z)=E{E(Y∣x,z,w)∣w,z}=E{α+βx⊤x+βz⊤z∣w,z}, which is the claim.

(b) The law of total variance, conditioning on x inside (w,z), gives 𝛃𝛃Var(Y∣w,z)=E{Var(Y∣x,z,w)∣w,z}+Var{E(Y∣x,z,w)∣w,z}=σ2+βx⊤Cβx. Under joint normality the conditional covariance of x given (w,z) does not depend on the conditioning values (Theorem 3.5.2).

(c) The best linear predictor (Definition 6.11.1, Theorem 2.5.2), with the constant among the predictors, is the projection in L2 onto the span of the entries of s, so it is E(ts⊤)S−1s. Under classical error E(ts⊤)=E{t(t+u~)⊤}=A, and AS−1s follows. The entries of t^ span the same space as those of s, since AS−1 is nonsingular. Their second-moment matrix is AS−1SS−1A=AS−1A, and 𝛉E(t^Y)=AS−1E(sY)=AS−1Aθ, because 𝛉E(sY)=Aθ as in Section 24.3. The projection coefficients are 𝛉𝛉(AS−1A)−1AS−1Aθ=θ.

(d) With the calibrated regressors t^i=A^S^−1si, the normal equations (Theorem 6.4.2) have matrix n−1∑it^it^i⊤=A^S^−1A^ and right side A^S^−1n−1∑isiyi. Solving, (A^S^−1A^)−1A^S^−1n−1∑isiyi=A^−1n−1∑isiyi.

The coordinates of t^ for the constant and z reproduce them, so only the mismeasured regressors are replaced. By (c) and (d), linear regression calibration needs no distributional assumption and is the moment correction, with the standard errors of Theorem 24.3.1. Second-stage standard errors, which treat the calibrated values as data, are too small. A validation subsample in which x is measured identifies the calibration directly, by regressing x on (w,z) there, and needs only (a): nondifferential error and a correct model for E(x∣w,z). In a logistic regression, (a) gives E{G(α+βX)∣w}, not G(α+βm(w)), so calibration is an approximation, good when Var(X∣W) is small (Carroll, Ruppert, Stefanski and Crainiceanu, 2006).

Remark (Two meanings of “calibration”). In Section 12.5 calibration meant inverse prediction of x0 from a new response. Regression calibration instead predicts the regressor from its measurement.

Example (Calibrating the mean reading)

The replicates estimate the error variance of the mean reading as 37.04. The calibrated pressure x^i has standard deviation 10.36, against 13.11 for the mean reading: each reading is shrunk towards what age predicts. Regressing y on (1,x^,age) gives 0.0757 and 0.0018, exactly the moment correction of Example (Correcting with two readings), by Proposition 24.5.1(d).

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2401)
n = 300
age = rng.normal(55, 10, n)
x = 130 + 0.6 * (age - 55) + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(108), n)   # long-run blood pressure, sd 12
w = x[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 9, (n, 2))                      # two clinic readings
y = -4 + 0.08 * x + 0.0 * age + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)          # age has no effect given x

wbar = w.mean(axis=1)
s2_ubar = np.mean((w[:, 0] - w[:, 1]) ** 2) / 4               # error variance of the mean reading
W = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), wbar, age])
S = W.T @ W / n                                               # raw second moments of (1, wbar, age)
Suu = np.diag([0.0, s2_ubar, 0.0])

# calibration: best linear predictor of (1, x, age) from (1, wbar, age), using E(w x^T) = S - Suu
Gamma = np.linalg.solve(S, S - Suu)
x_hat = (W @ Gamma)[:, 1]                                     # predicted long-run pressure
b_rc = np.linalg.lstsq(np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x_hat, age]), y, rcond=None)[0]
b_mom = np.linalg.solve(S - Suu, W.T @ y / n)                 # method of moments, Section 24.3
print("regression calibration:", b_rc.round(4))
print("method of moments:     ", b_mom.round(4))

24.5.2. SIMEX

Cook and Stefanski (1994) treat the naive estimator as a black box. Data cannot be made more accurate, but they can be made less accurate by adding simulated error; watching the naive estimate move as the error variance grows, we extrapolate back to zero. Let σu2 be the known or estimated error variance of W.

  1. Simulation. For each ζ in a grid such as {0.5,1,1.5,2} and b=1,…,B, form remeasured data Wb,i(ζ)=Wi+ζσuVb,i, with Vb,i∼iidN(0,1) independent of the data, and compute the naive estimate 𝛉θ^b(ζ) from (Wb(ζ),z,Y). Average over b to get 𝛉θ^(ζ), and put 𝛉θ^(0) equal to the naive estimate.

  2. Extrapolation. Fit a function 𝛄g(ζ;γ) of ζ to the points 𝛉(ζ,θ^(ζ)), one coefficient at a time, and report 𝛄g(−1;γ^).

The remeasured error U+ζσuV is still classical, with variance (1+ζ)σu2, so ζ=−1 means no error. Averaging over b removes simulation noise only.

Proposition 24.5.2 (SIMEX in the linear model)

Assume the conditions of Theorem 24.1.1(c), with one mismeasured scalar regressor of error variance σu2, and fix ζ≥0. For each b, the naive least squares coefficients from the remeasured data converge with probability one to g(ζ)=(gx(ζ),gz(ζ)), where

𝛃𝛑gx(ζ)=βxσx⋅z2σx⋅z2+(1+ζ)σu2,gz(ζ)=βz+(1+ζ)σu2σx⋅z2+(1+ζ)σu2βxπ,
(24.5.1)

and so does their average over b. Moreover:

  • (a) gx(−1)=βx and 𝛃gz(−1)=βz;

  • (b) every coefficient has the form a+b/(c+ζ) with c=1+σx⋅z2/σu2; in particular 1/gx(ζ) is linear in ζ when βx≠0;

  • (c) if a polynomial of fixed degree is fitted by least squares to the points 𝛉(ζk,θ^(ζk)) on a fixed grid, the SIMEX estimate converges to the value at −1 of the polynomial fitted in the same way to (ζk,g(ζk)), which in general differs from g(−1).

Proof. The remeasured data satisfy Definition 24.1.1 with error U+ζσuV, whose variance is (1+ζ)σu2, and the observations (Xi,zi,Ui,Vb,i,εi) are independent over i. By Theorem 24.1.1(c), with σu2 replaced by (1+ζ)σu2, the coefficients converge to λζβx and 𝛃𝛑βz+(1−λζ)βxπ, where λζ=σx⋅z2/{σx⋅z2+(1+ζ)σu2}. This is Eq. 24.5.1. A finite average of sequences converging with probability one converges to the average of the limits. (a) At ζ=−1, λζ=1. (b) The denominator is σu2(c+ζ). So gx(ζ)=(βxσx⋅z2/σu2)/(c+ζ), and (1+ζ)/(c+ζ)=1−(c−1)/(c+ζ) gives 𝛃𝛑𝛑gz(ζ)=βz+βxπ−(c−1)βxπ/(c+ζ). (c) On a fixed grid the fitted polynomial’s coefficients are a fixed linear function of the fitted points (the least squares solution with a fixed model matrix), so its value at −1 is a continuous function of the points, and the points converge.

So the rational form matches the limit exactly, and the rational SIMEX estimate is consistent provided the nonlinear fit is unique and continuous in the points, which we do not verify. The quadratic, a common default because it is linear in its parameters, recovers only part of the attenuation (Exercise (B1)). With several mismeasured regressors, or in nonlinear models, no simple extrapolant is exact and SIMEX is only approximately consistent. Its appeal is that it needs nothing but the naive estimator.

Example (SIMEX for the blood-pressure study)

Apply SIMEX to the regression on the mean reading and age, with error variance 37.04, grid {0,0.5,1,1.5,2} and B=200 (Figure 24.5.1). Extrapolating to ζ=−1:

Coefficient naive SIMEX, quadratic SIMEX, a+b/(c+ζ) moment correction truth
blood pressure 0.0562 0.0709 0.0744 0.0757 0.08
age 0.0120 0.0042 0.0024 0.0018 0

The rational extrapolant nearly reproduces the moment correction; the quadratic stops short, and by Proposition 24.5.2(c) its large-sample limit is 0.0749, only 0.77 of the way from the naive limit 0.0582 to 0.08, and 0.0030 for age. A pairs bootstrap (Definition 23.1.1) over patients, re-estimating the error variance and rerunning SIMEX (B=50, 200 resamples), gives standard error 0.0076 for the rational SIMEX slope, as for the moment correction.

from scipy.optimize import curve_fit

def naive_fits(wcol, age, y):
    """Least squares coefficients of y on (1, wcol, age) for a stack of remeasured columns."""
    m, k = wcol.shape                                         # m remeasured data sets of size k
    Xs = np.stack([np.ones((m, k)), wcol, np.broadcast_to(age, (m, k))], axis=2)
    XtX = np.einsum("mki,mkj->mij", Xs, Xs)
    Xty = np.einsum("mki,k->mi", Xs, y)
    return np.linalg.solve(XtX, Xty[:, :, None])[:, :, 0]


def simex(wbar, age, y, s2_ubar, zetas, B, rng):
    """Average naive coefficients after adding error of variance zeta * s2_ubar."""
    out = [naive_fits(wbar[None, :], age, y)[0]]
    for z in zetas[1:]:
        extra = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(z * s2_ubar), (B, len(y)))
        out.append(naive_fits(wbar + extra, age, y).mean(axis=0))
    return np.array(out)                                      # one row per zeta


def rational(z, a, b, c):
    return a + b / (c + z)


zetas = np.array([0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0])
G = simex(wbar, age, y, s2_ubar, zetas, 200, np.random.default_rng(2450))
est_quad, est_rat = [], []
for j in [1, 2]:                                              # coefficient of x, of age
    est_quad.append(np.polyval(np.polyfit(zetas, G[:, j], 2), -1.0))
    p, _ = curve_fit(rational, zetas, G[:, j], p0=[0.0, G[0, j], 1.5], maxfev=20000)
    est_rat.append(rational(-1.0, *p))
print("naive (zeta = 0):   ", G[0, 1:].round(4))
print("SIMEX, quadratic:   ", np.round(est_quad, 4))
print("SIMEX, rational:    ", np.round(est_rat, 4))

24.5.3. Which correction?

In the linear model the moment correction and regression calibration coincide, rational SIMEX agrees up to simulation noise, and IV trades precision for not needing an error variance. Beyond it, only regression calibration and SIMEX carry over. All rest on information from outside the regression of Y on W. Without it, Proposition 24.2.2 is the honest summary: a range of slopes, with the naive estimate at one end.

24.5.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In simple regression without z, show directly that regression calibration (Proposition 24.5.1) with the linear calibration x^=w¯+λ^(w−w¯), λ^=(sww−σu2)/sww, gives the slope β^/λ^, where β^ is the naive slope.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In simple regression with reliability λ, write g(ζ)=λβ/{1+ζ(1−λ)}. Show that the quadratic through (ζ,g(ζ)) for ζ=0,1,2 takes the value 3g(0)−3g(1)+g(2) at ζ=−1. Evaluate it for λ=0.5 and λ=0.64, as fractions of β.

Solution. The quadratic through three equally spaced points is determined by the forward differences. Written as p(ζ)=g0+ζΔg0+12ζ(ζ−1)Δ2g0, it gives p(−1)=g0−Δg0+Δ2g0=g0−(g1−g0)+(g2−2g1+g0)=3g0−3g1+g2. With λ=0.5: g0=β/2, g1=β/3, g2=β/4, so p(−1)=β(3/2−1+1/4)=0.75β. With λ=0.64: g0=0.64β, g1=0.64β/1.36, g2=0.64β/1.72, so p(−1)=β(1.92−1.4118+0.3721)≈0.880β. The quadratic removes most of the attenuation but not all of it, and it does worse as the reliability falls.

Which of the assumptions of Proposition 24.5.1 does it need, and which does it avoid?

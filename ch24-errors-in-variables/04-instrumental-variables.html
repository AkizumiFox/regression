24.4  Instrumental variables

The corrections so far used a number from outside the regression. Instrumental variables use a variable, related to the mismeasured regressor and unrelated to everything that makes least squares fail. The idea goes back to Philip Wright (1928), who used it to estimate supply and demand curves.

24.4.1. Endogeneity

By Eq. 24.1.2, Y=α+βW+e with e=ε−βU correlated with W. An omitted common cause (Theorem 19.1.2) or a regressor partly determined by the response has the same effect. Such regressors are called endogenous. In every case the equation

𝛃yi=x(i)⊤β+ei,E(x(i)ei)≠0,
(24.4.1)

describes a relation of scientific interest, not the best linear predictor that least squares estimates. Here x(i) holds all the regressors, including the constant. In this section x collects all regressors and z all instruments; the exactly measured covariates of earlier sections appear in both.

Definition 24.4.1 (Instruments)

A random q-vector z is a vector of instruments for Eq. 24.4.1, with random regressor p-vector x, if

  • (i) exogeneity: E(ze)=0;

  • (ii) relevance: Σzx=E(zx⊤) has rank p, which requires q≥p;

and Σzz=E(zz⊤) is positive definite.

Regressors uncorrelated with e, such as the constant, are their own instruments; the rank condition asks the excluded instruments to carry extra information about the endogenous regressors. With q=p the equation is just identified, and with q>p overidentified.

In the blood-pressure study the second reading instruments the first: x=(1,W1,age), z=(1,W2,age) and e=ε−βU1, which is uncorrelated with W2=X+U2. Nothing about σu2 needs to be known, but the two errors must be independent, which rules out two readings that share a transient cause (Exercise (A2)).

24.4.2. The estimators

With the n×p matrix X of regressors and the n×q matrix Z of instruments, the sample version of 𝛃E{z(y−x⊤β)}=0 in the just-identified case is 𝛃Z⊤(y−Xβ)=0, with solution 𝛃β^IV=(Z⊤X)−1Z⊤y. When q>p these are more equations than unknowns. Two-stage least squares (2SLS) combines them through the projection MZ=Z(Z⊤Z)−1Z⊤ onto C(Z):

𝛃β^2SLS=(X⊤MZX)−1X⊤MZy.
(24.4.2)

The name comes from a two-step computation.

Lemma 24.4.1 (Two-stage least squares as projection)

Let Z have full column rank and X⊤MZX be nonsingular, and let X^=MZX be the matrix of first-stage fitted values, obtained by regressing each column of X on Z.

  • (a) 𝛃β^2SLS is the least squares coefficient of y on X^, and also the IV estimator with instruments X^: 𝛃β^2SLS=(X^⊤X^)−1X^⊤y=(X^⊤X)−1X^⊤y.

  • (b) Columns of X that are also columns of Z are unchanged by the first stage.

  • (c) If q=p and Z⊤X is nonsingular, 𝛃𝛃β^2SLS=β^IV.

Proof. (a) MZ is the orthogonal projection onto C(Z) (Theorem 6.3.6), so it is symmetric and idempotent (Theorem 6.3.3). Hence X^⊤X^=X⊤MZMZX=X⊤MZX, and X^⊤X=X⊤MZX and X^⊤y=X⊤MZy as well. Substitute these in Eq. 24.4.2. (b) A column in C(Z) is fixed by MZ. (c) With Z⊤X square and invertible, (X⊤Z(Z⊤Z)−1Z⊤X)−1=(Z⊤X)−1(Z⊤Z)(X⊤Z)−1, and multiplying by X⊤Z(Z⊤Z)−1Z⊤y leaves (Z⊤X)−1Z⊤y.

The first stage replaces each endogenous regressor by the part of it that the instruments explain, which is uncorrelated with e. As with the residualized regressions of Section 6.6, the second-stage coefficients are right but its residuals 𝛃y−X^β^ are not; the equation’s residuals are 𝛃y−Xβ^.

Theorem 24.4.2 (Consistency and asymptotic normality of IV)

Let (x(i),z(i),ei) be independent copies of (x,z,e) with finite second moments, satisfying Eq. 24.4.1 and Definition 24.4.1. Let H=ΣxzΣzz−1Σzx, where Σxz=Σzx⊤.

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃β^2SLS→β with probability one.

  • (b) If E(e2‖z‖2)<∞, then 𝛃𝛃n(β^2SLS−β)→Np(0,V) in distribution, where V=H−1ΣxzΣzz−1ΩΣzz−1ΣzxH−1,Ω=E(e2zz⊤). If E(e2∣z)=σe2 is constant, then V=σe2H−1.

  • (c) 𝛃σ^e2=n−1‖y−Xβ^2SLS‖2→σe2=E(e2) with probability one.

Proof. (a) Write C^=n−1Z⊤X and S^=n−1Z⊤Z. Substituting 𝛃𝛆y=Xβ+ε in Eq. 24.4.2, 𝛃𝛃𝛆β^2SLS−β=(C^⊤S^−1C^)−1C^⊤S^−11nZ⊤ε. By the strong law, C^→Σzx, S^→Σzz and 𝛆n−1Z⊤ε→E(ze)=0. The matrix H is positive definite: for a≠0, Σzxa≠0 by the rank condition, so a⊤Ha=(Σzxa)⊤Σzz−1(Σzxa)>0. By continuity the right side tends to H−1ΣxzΣzz−10=0.

(b) Multiply the display by n. The vectors z(i)ei are independent and identically distributed with mean 0 and covariance Ω, finite by assumption, so 𝛆n−1/2Z⊤ε→Nq(0,Ω) by the central limit theorem. The matrix in front converges to L=H−1ΣxzΣzz−1, and the splitting used for Theorem 24.3.1(b) gives the limit LNq(0,Ω)=Np(0,V). If E(e2∣z)=σe2, then Ω=E{E(e2∣z)zz⊤}=σe2Σzz, and V collapses to σe2H−1.

(c) With 𝛅𝛃𝛃δ=β^2SLS−β, 𝛃𝛆𝛅y−Xβ^2SLS=ε−Xδ, so 𝛆𝛆𝛅𝛆𝛅𝛅σ^e2=1nε⊤ε−2δ⊤1nX⊤ε+δ⊤(1nX⊤X)δ. The averages converge to E(e2), E(xe) and E(xx⊤), all finite, and 𝛅δ→0 by (a).

In practice V is estimated with sample moments and Ω^=n−1∑ie^i2z(i)z(i)⊤, as in Theorem 21.4.2, or under homoscedasticity by σ^e2(X⊤MZX)−1. Among estimators using linear combinations of the instruments, 2SLS has the smallest asymptotic covariance under homoscedasticity (Exercise (C2)).

Example (The second reading as an instrument)

Instrument the first reading with the second, and let age and the constant instrument themselves. The IV estimates are 0.0708 for blood pressure, with standard error 0.0085 (0.0081 from the sandwich), and 0.0054 for age (standard error 0.0081). The spurious age effect has gone. The first-stage F statistic for the second reading is 160.1, a strong instrument. The correct residuals give σ^e2=1.438, estimating σ2+β2σu2=1.5184; the second-stage residuals give 1.376. The moment correction of Example (Correcting with two readings) gave 0.0757 with standard error 0.0076. It uses both readings symmetrically, while IV uses one only as an instrument and pays in precision. But IV needs no estimate of the error variance, and the instrument need only be correlated with X, not a second unbiased reading of it.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2401)
n = 300
age = rng.normal(55, 10, n)
x = 130 + 0.6 * (age - 55) + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(108), n)   # long-run blood pressure, sd 12
w = x[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 9, (n, 2))                      # two clinic readings
y = -4 + 0.08 * x + 0.0 * age + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)          # age has no effect given x

X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), w[:, 0], age])    # regressors: first reading, age
Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), w[:, 1], age])    # instruments: second reading, age
b_iv = np.linalg.solve(Z.T @ X, Z.T @ y)
e = y - X @ b_iv                                    # residuals use X, not the first-stage fit
ZXinv = np.linalg.inv(Z.T @ X)
cov_h = e @ e / n * ZXinv @ (Z.T @ Z) @ ZXinv.T               # homoscedastic
cov_s = ZXinv @ ((Z * e[:, None] ** 2).T @ Z) @ ZXinv.T       # heteroscedasticity-robust
print("IV (1, x, age):", b_iv.round(4))
print("se, homoscedastic:", np.sqrt(np.diag(cov_h)).round(4))
print("se, sandwich:     ", np.sqrt(np.diag(cov_s)).round(4))

# first stage: regress the first reading on the instruments; F for the excluded instrument
g, rss1 = np.linalg.lstsq(Z, w[:, 0], rcond=None)[:2]
rss0 = np.sum((w[:, 0] - np.column_stack([np.ones(n), age]) @
               np.linalg.lstsq(np.column_stack([np.ones(n), age]), w[:, 0], rcond=None)[0]) ** 2)
F1 = (rss0 - rss1[0]) / (rss1[0] / (n - 3))
print(f"first-stage F = {F1:.1f}")

Wald’s (1940) grouping estimator (Section 7.1) is an IV estimator whose instrument is a group indicator (Exercise (B1)).

24.4.3. Weak instruments

If the instruments explain little of the endogenous regressor, the limit theory of Theorem 24.4.2 is a poor guide at realistic n. The simplest case can be solved exactly.

Proposition 24.4.3 (The exact distribution with one instrument)

Let yi=βxi+ei and xi=πzi+vi, i=1,…,n, with fixed zi, Szz=∑izi2>0, and (ei,vi) independent bivariate normal with means zero, standard deviations σe,σv and correlation ρ, |ρ|<1. Let β^=∑iziyi/∑izixi and μ=πSzz/σv.

  • (a) β^−β has the same distribution as

    σeσv⋅ρη+1−ρ2ξμ+η,η,ξ∼iidN(0,1).
    (24.4.3)

  • (b) E|β^|=∞ for every μ.

  • (c) As μ→∞, (μσv/σe)(β^−β)→N(0,1) in distribution. If μ=0, β^−β has median ρσe/σv, which is the large-sample bias of least squares when π=0.

  • (d) (∑izixi)2/(σv2Szz)∼χ2(1,μ2), with mean 1+μ2.

Proof. (a) Put η=∑izivi/(σvSzz) and ζ=∑iziei/(σeSzz). They are jointly normal (Theorem 3.3.1) with unit variances and covariance ∑izi2ρσeσv/(σeσvSzz)=ρ. By Theorem 3.5.2, ξ=(ζ−ρη)/1−ρ2 is standard normal and independent of η. Now ∑izixi=πSzz+σvSzzη=σvSzz(μ+η), and β^−β=∑iziei/∑izixi=σeζ/{σv(μ+η)}.

(b) Given η, the numerator ρη+1−ρ2ξ is normal with standard deviation 1−ρ2>0, so its conditional absolute mean is at least c=2(1−ρ2)/π. Hence E|β^−β|≥(σe/σv)cE|μ+η|−1=∞, because the density of μ+η is continuous and positive at zero, as in the proof of Proposition 12.5.2. Finally |β^|≥|β^−β|−|β|.

(c) (μσv/σe)(β^−β) has the law of (ρη+1−ρ2ξ)/(1+η/μ). The numerator is standard normal and η/μ→0 in probability, so Slutsky’s lemma applies. For μ=0 the representation is (σe/σv)(ρ+1−ρ2ξ/η), and ξ/η is symmetric about zero, so the median is ρσe/σv. When π=0, x=v and least squares converges to β+Cov(v,e)/σv2=β+ρσe/σv.

(d) ∑izixi/(σvSzz)=μ+η∼N(μ,1), and its square is χ2(1,μ2), whose mean is 1+μ2 (the convention of Chapter 4, without a factor 12).

The design and π enter only through the concentration parameter μ2, which acts as a sample size, and by (d) the first-stage statistic estimates 1+μ2. Hence the rule of thumb of Staiger and Stock (1997): an instrument is weak if the first-stage F is below about 10. A useless instrument (μ=0) gives an estimator centred on the least squares bias, with Cauchy tails.

Example (How weak is too weak?)

With n=200, β=1, σe=σv=1 and ρ=0.9, least squares tends to about 1.9 for a weak instrument. In 20000 samples for each concentration:

μ2 median quartiles coverage of nominal 95% Wald interval
1 1.298 0.711 to 1.646 0.843
10 1.008 0.740 to 1.181 0.921
100 1.002 0.929 to 1.064 0.947

With μ2=1 the median moves a third of the way to the least squares limit, a proportion 0.200 of estimates fall outside [−1,3], and the Wald interval undercovers, as it still does at μ2=10 (Figure 24.4.1).

def iv_draws(mu2, reps, seed, n=200, rho=0.9):
    """IV estimates of beta = 1 and Wald-interval coverage when the concentration is mu2."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    z = r.normal(size=n)
    z = z * np.sqrt(n / (z @ z))                  # fixed instrument with sum z^2 = n
    pi = np.sqrt(mu2 / n)
    ev = r.multivariate_normal([0, 0], [[1, rho], [rho, 1]], size=(reps, n))
    xx = pi * z + ev[:, :, 1]
    yy = 1.0 * xx + ev[:, :, 0]
    b = (yy @ z) / (xx @ z)
    res = yy - b[:, None] * xx
    se = np.sqrt((res ** 2).mean(axis=1) * n / (xx @ z) ** 2)
    return b, np.abs(b - 1) <= 1.96 * se


for mu2 in [1, 10, 100]:
    b, hit = iv_draws(mu2, 2000, 2440)
    print(f"mu^2 = {mu2:3d}: median {np.median(b):.3f}, "
          f"quartiles {np.percentile(b, 25):.3f} to {np.percentile(b, 75):.3f}, coverage {hit.mean():.3f}")

With many weak instruments, 2SLS is biased towards least squares, and Bound, Jaeger and Baker (1995) showed that it can nearly reproduce least squares; we do not prove this. For inference, invert a test whose null distribution does not depend on instrument strength, such as the Anderson–Rubin test (Exercise (C1)). Its confidence sets are of Fieller type (Proposition 12.5.1) and can be unbounded, as they must sometimes be (Proposition 12.5.4).

Warning. Exogeneity cannot be checked in a just-identified model: all the equations E(ze)=0 are used to estimate 𝛃β. With more instruments, tests of overidentifying restrictions (Sargan 1958) check only that the instruments agree. An instrument is justified by knowledge of how the data arose; for replicates, by the independence of their errors. Instruments for causal effects are taken up in Chapter 25.

24.4.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For x=(1,W) and z=(1,Z), show that the IV slope is szy/szw, the ratio of two sample covariances. Show that the population ratio equals β when Z is correlated with X and uncorrelated with U and ε.

Exercise (A2)

If the errors of two readings are correlated, Cov(U1,U2)=κ, find the limit of the IV slope in simple regression that instruments W1 with W2. In which direction is it biased when κ>0?

Solution. Cov(W2,Y)=βσx2 and Cov(W2,W1)=σx2+κ, so the limit is βσx2/(σx2+κ). With κ>0 it is attenuated, though less than least squares unless κ=σu2. The instrument is not exogenous: Cov(W2,ε−βU1)=−βκ.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let gi∈{0,1} be a group indicator, and let z(i)=(1,gi) instrument x(i)=(1,wi). Show that the IV slope is (y¯1−y¯0)/(w¯1−w¯0), the difference of group means of y divided by that of w. This is Wald’s (1940) estimator. Show that it is consistent if g is independent of (U,ε) and E(X∣g) differs between the groups. Explain why forming the groups by splitting at the median of w itself, as Wald proposed, does not satisfy these conditions under classical error.

Solution. By Exercise (A1) the slope is sgy/sgw. For a binary g with n1 ones, ∑i(gi−g¯)yi=n1(y¯1−y¯) and y¯1−y¯=(n0/n)(y¯1−y¯0). The same holds for w, and the factors cancel. Exogeneity needs Cov(g,ε−βU)=0, which holds when g is independent of (U,ε). Relevance needs Cov(g,W)=Cov(g,X)≠0, that is, different group means of X. If the groups are formed from w, membership depends on U: readings with large positive errors tend to land in the upper group, so Cov(g,U)>0 and exogeneity fails. The grouping must come from information independent of the measurement error, such as a second reading or a design variable.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In Eq. 24.4.1 with x=(1,W), let z=(1,z1) with z1 a k-vector of excluded instruments, and suppose e is independent of z and N(0,σe2). To test H0:β=β0, regress y−β0w on z and compute the F statistic for z1. Show that under H0 it has an exact F(k,n−k−1) distribution, whatever the strength of the instruments. This is the test of Anderson and Rubin (1949). Inverting it gives a confidence set for β. Show that for k=1 the set is of the form in Proposition 12.5.1.

Solution. Under H0, y−β0w=α+e with e normal and independent of z: given Z, a normal linear model in which the coefficients of z1 are zero. The F statistic of Theorem 11.1.1 is exactly F(k,n−k−1) given Z, hence unconditionally, and w plays no role. For k=1, let a^ and b^ be the slopes of y and of w on (1,z), let v=1/∑i(zi−z¯)2, and let s11, s12, s22 be the residual mean squares and mean cross-product of y and w from these two regressions. The regression of y−β0w on (1,z) has slope a^−β0b^ and residual mean square s11−2β0s12+β02s22, so F=t2 and the acceptance region is (a^−β0b^)2≤tn−2,α/22v(s11−2β0s12+β02s22). This is Eq. 12.5.1 with s2vij replaced by vsij: a quadratic inequality in β0. Like Fieller’s set, it can be an interval, the complement of an interval, or the whole line.

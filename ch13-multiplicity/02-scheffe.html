13.2  Scheffé’s projection method

Scheffé’s method answers the hardest version of the problem: the family is every estimable function in a subspace, and the analyst may choose which ones to look at after seeing the data. The intervals have simultaneous coverage exactly 1−α, because the largest standardized error in the family is the length of a projection, and Scheffé’s method is the F test, read one direction at a time.

Throughout this section the model is the normal linear model Eq. 7.3.1, 𝛃Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I), with r=rank(X)<n. Write 𝛍𝛃μ=Xβ, ν=n−r for the error degrees of freedom, s2=SSE/ν, G=(X⊤X)− for any generalized inverse, and Fα(q,ν) for the upper α point of F(q,ν). For an estimable 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β the standard error is 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌se(λ⊤β^)=sλ⊤Gλ (Corollary 7.5.2).

13.2.1. Linear functions of the mean as vectors

An estimable function is a linear function of the mean vector, and a linear function of 𝛍μ∈C(X) can be represented by a vector of C(X). If 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β is estimable, then 𝛌𝛒λ=X⊤ρ for some vector 𝛒ρ∈Rn (Theorem 8.3.1), and with 𝛒v=Mρ,

𝛌𝛃𝛒𝛃𝛍𝛌𝛃𝛒𝛌𝛌𝛒𝛒𝛒𝛒λ⊤β=ρ⊤Xβ=v⊤μ,λ⊤β^=ρ⊤MY=v⊤Y,λ⊤Gλ=ρ⊤XGX⊤ρ=ρ⊤Mρ=‖v‖2.
(13.2.1)

The second identity is Theorem 6.4.4, and the third uses M=XGX⊤ (Theorem 6.3.6). The vector v does not depend on the choice of 𝛒ρ, since two choices differ by some 𝛅δ with 𝛅X⊤δ=0, hence 𝛅Mδ=0. So the estimate is the inner product of v with the data, the estimand its inner product with the mean, and the standard error is s‖v‖.

A family of estimable functions closed under linear combination is then a subspace V⊆C(X). It is usually described in one of two ways. If the family is 𝛌𝛃𝛌{λ⊤β:λ∈C(Λ)} for a p×q matrix Λ=X⊤T, then v=MTa and V=C(MT), the test space of 𝛃Λ⊤β=0 (Theorem 6.5.2); if Λ has rank q, so does MT, because X⊤MT=Λ. If instead the family is the set of functions about which a reduced model C(X0)⊆C(X) makes a claim, those vanishing on C(X0), then V=C(M−M0), of dimension r−r0 (Theorem 6.5.1).

13.2.2. The theorem

Lemma 13.2.1 (The largest standardized component)

Let V be a subspace of Rn with orthogonal projection PV, and let u∈Rn. Then maxv∈V, v≠0(v⊤u)2‖v‖2=‖PVu‖2, and if PVu≠0 the maximum is attained exactly at the nonzero multiples of PVu.

Proof. For v∈V, v=PVv, and since PV is symmetric, v⊤u=v⊤PVu. By Cauchy–Schwarz (Proposition 1.1.1), (v⊤PVu)2≤‖v‖2‖PVu‖2, with equality iff v is a multiple of PVu. That multiple lies in V, so the bound is attained. If PVu=0 both sides are zero.

The lemma is Exercise (C1) of Chapter 9, where it described the sum of squares of a row of an analysis of variance table as the largest one-degree-of-freedom sum of squares inside it. Applied to 𝛍u=Y−μ, it describes the worst standardized error in a family.

Theorem 13.2.2 (Scheffé’s simultaneous intervals)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with r<n, and let 0<α<1.

  • (a) Let V⊆C(X) be a subspace of dimension q≥1. Then 𝛍𝛍maxv∈V, v≠0(v⊤Y−v⊤μ)2qs2‖v‖2=‖PV(Y−μ)‖2/qs2∼F(q,ν), and consequently 𝛍Pr(|v⊤(Y−μ)|≤qFα(q,ν)s‖v‖  ∀v∈V)=1−α.

  • (b) Let Λ be p×q of rank q with C(Λ)⊆C(X⊤). With probability exactly 1−α, for every 𝛌λ∈C(Λ) simultaneously,

    𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃λ⊤β∈[λ⊤β^−qFα(q,ν)se(λ⊤β^),  λ⊤β^+qFα(q,ν)se(λ⊤β^)].
    (13.2.2)

    Moreover, with 𝛉𝛃θ=Λ⊤β, 𝛉𝛃θ^=Λ⊤β^ and W=Λ⊤GΛ,

    𝛌𝛌𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉maxλ∈C(Λ), λ≠0(λ⊤β^−λ⊤β)2se(λ⊤β^)2=(θ^−θ)⊤W−1(θ^−θ)s2,
    (13.2.3)

    attained at 𝛌𝛉𝛉λ=ΛW−1(θ^−θ).

Proof. (a) The first equality is Lemma 13.2.1 with 𝛍u=Y−μ. The map v↦X⊤v is one to one on C(X) (Step 1 of the proof of Theorem 12.4.1), so L={X⊤v:v∈V} is a subspace of C(X⊤) of dimension q. For x=X⊤v, Eq. 13.2.1 with 𝛒ρ=v gives 𝛃𝛃𝛍x⊤β^−x⊤β=v⊤(Y−μ) and x⊤Gx=‖v‖2. So the event in the display is the event Eq. 12.4.1 with k=q, and it has probability exactly 1−α by Theorem 12.4.1. That event is also the event that the maximum is at most Fα(q,ν). Since this holds for every α∈(0,1), the maximum has the distribution function of F(q,ν).

(b) Write Λ=X⊤T and V=C(MT), of dimension q. Every 𝛌λ∈C(Λ) is Λa=X⊤(Ta), and by Eq. 13.2.1 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛍λ⊤β^−λ⊤β=v⊤Y−v⊤μ and 𝛌𝛃se(λ⊤β^)=s‖v‖ with v=MTa∈V. As a ranges over Rq, v ranges over all of V. So the event in (b) is the event in (a). Finally, Eq. 13.2.3 is Proposition 11.6.2 for the hypothesis 𝛃𝛉Λ⊤β=θ, which is testable because 𝛉θ is the true value: its statistic Eq. 11.3.1 is 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉(θ^−θ)⊤W−1(θ^−θ)/(qs2), and each T(a)2 is the ratio on the left of Eq. 13.2.3 at 𝛌λ=Λa, so the maximum is attained at 𝛉𝛉a∝W−1(θ^−θ).

The coverage is exact, not a bound: some function in the family, depending on the data, always lies on the edge of the event. Only the subspace matters, so dependent columns of Λ may be dropped first. For q=1 the multiplier is tν,α/2, since Fα(1,ν)=tν,α/22, and the interval is that of Theorem 12.1.1.

Part (b) is the observation-space form of a fact met twice already. Section 11.6 proved Eq. 13.2.3, with d in place of 𝛉θ, as the statement that a group F statistic is the largest single t statistic in the group, and Section 12.2 showed that the intervals Eq. 13.2.2 are the shadows of the confidence ellipsoid for 𝛉𝛃θ=Λ⊤β (Theorem 12.2.1). The ellipsoid covers 𝛉θ iff every shadow covers the corresponding 𝛉a⊤θ, which is why an infinite family costs no more than one ellipsoid. The coverage statement itself is the Working–Hotelling band of Theorem 12.4.1 for the subspace L=C(Λ): a band for a regression surface and a family of intervals for estimable functions are one object, written in the coordinates of C(X⊤) or of C(X). Figure 13.2.1 extends Figure 12.2.1, which drew the same ellipse for two slopes of the crime regression, by adding the Bonferroni rectangle and the tangent lines that bound the Scheffé interval for a combination a1β1+a2β2.

Example (Three slopes of a crime regression)

Return to the regression of the murder rate on poverty, single parenthood and urbanization for the 50 states of Example (Poverty and murder rates), with ν=46 error degrees of freedom. The poverty coefficient is 0.2538 with standard error 0.0934, so t=2.718. Suppose the family of interest is every linear combination of the three slopes, q=3. The 95% intervals for the poverty coefficient are:

method multiplier interval
unadjusted t 2.013 [0.066, 0.442]
Bonferroni, three slopes 2.485 [0.022, 0.486]
Scheffé, all combinations 2.902 [−0.017, 0.525]

The poverty coefficient is significant on its own and among three planned comparisons, but not once every combination of the slopes is admitted. The F statistic for the hypothesis that all three slopes are zero is 27.49, and the largest |t| over all combinations is 9.081=3×27.49, in agreement with Eq. 13.2.3.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats
data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data
data = data.drop(index="District of Columbia")

y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), data[["poverty", "single", "urban"]]])
n, p = X.shape
nu = n - p                                            # error degrees of freedom
XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
beta_hat = XtX_inv @ X.T @ y
s2 = np.sum((y - X @ beta_hat) ** 2) / nu
se = np.sqrt(s2 * np.diag(XtX_inv))
alpha, slopes = 0.05, [1, 2, 3]                       # poverty, single, urban
k = len(slopes)
mult = {
    "unadjusted": stats.t.ppf(1 - alpha / 2, nu),
    "Bonferroni": stats.t.ppf(1 - alpha / (2 * k), nu),
    "Scheffe": np.sqrt(k * stats.f.ppf(1 - alpha, k, nu)),   # q = 3 slopes
}
for name, c in mult.items():
    lo, hi = beta_hat[slopes] - c * se[slopes], beta_hat[slopes] + c * se[slopes]
    print(f"{name:10s} c = {c:.3f}  poverty [{lo[0]:.3f}, {hi[0]:.3f}]")
L = np.eye(p)[:, slopes]                              # Lambda: the three slopes
W = L.T @ XtX_inv @ L
d = L.T @ beta_hat
F = d @ np.linalg.solve(W, d) / (k * s2)              # F test that all slopes are 0
a_star = np.linalg.solve(W, d)                        # the most significant combination
t_star = (a_star @ d) / np.sqrt(s2 * a_star @ W @ a_star)
print(f"F = {F:.2f};  largest |t| over all combinations = {t_star:.3f} = sqrt(3F)")

13.2.3. Scheffé’s method is the F test

Let d∈Rq and consider the hypothesis 𝛃Λ⊤β=d, with Λ as in Theorem 13.2.2(b). Because Λ⊤ has rank q, the hypothesis is consistent for every d. By Theorem 11.3.2 its F statistic is 𝛉𝛉F=(θ^−d)⊤W−1(θ^−d)qs2. The hypothesis implies a single-degree-of-freedom statement for every 𝛌λ=Λa in the family, namely 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=a⊤d. Call such a statement Scheffé-rejected if its hypothesized value lies outside the Scheffé interval Eq. 13.2.2.

Corollary 13.2.3 (The F test and Scheffé’s intervals)

In the setting of Theorem 13.2.2(b), for every d∈Rq:

  • (a) 𝛉maxa≠0(a⊤θ^−a⊤d)2s2a⊤Wa=qF, attained at 𝛉a=W−1(θ^−d);

  • (b) the level-α F test rejects 𝛃Λ⊤β=d iff at least one statement 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=a⊤d, 𝛌λ=Λa, is Scheffé-rejected;

  • (c) if 𝛃Λ⊤β=d is true, the probability that any of these statements is Scheffé-rejected is exactly α. For any 𝛃β, the probability of Scheffé-rejecting at least one true statement 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=c, 𝛌λ∈C(Λ), is at most α.

Proof. (a) For 𝛌λ=Λa, the ratio is (a⊤w)2/(s2a⊤Wa) with 𝛉w=θ^−d, and its maximum is w⊤W−1w/s2=qF, attained at a=W−1w, by Corollary 1.7.8(c) with W positive definite (Theorem 9.2.4(a)); this is the maximum identity of Section 11.6. (b) The statement for a is Scheffé-rejected iff its squared t statistic exceeds qFα(q,ν), so some statement is rejected iff the maximum in (a) exceeds qFα(q,ν), that is, iff F>Fα(q,ν). (c) The first claim follows from (b) and the exact level of the F test (Theorem 11.3.2). The second is Proposition 13.1.2(a) applied to the simultaneous intervals of Theorem 13.2.2.

When the F test rejects, some function in the family is Scheffé-significant, though perhaps not one of interest; conversely, no function is Scheffé-significant unless the F test rejects, so no separate protecting F test is needed. For a reduced model the same holds with V=C(M−M0): by Lemma 13.2.1 the F statistic of Theorem 11.1.1 is the maximum over v∈V of (v⊤Y)2/((r−r0)s2‖v‖2) (Exercise (B1)). Taking C(Λ)=C(X⊤), so that q=r and the family contains the mean response 𝛃x⊤β at every x∈C(X⊤), gives the band 𝛃x⊤β^±rFα(r,ν)sx⊤Gx, the Working–Hotelling band of Theorem 12.4.1.

13.2.4. Smaller families give shorter intervals

The Scheffé multiplier depends on the family only through its dimension q, and qFα(q,ν) is strictly increasing in q for fixed ν and α, as a χ2 coupling shows (an exercise of Section 11.6). So one should use the smallest subspace containing every function of interest, whose dimension can be smaller than the length of a list spanning it (Exercise (A2)). For a few planned functions, Bonferroni’s method (Section 13.4) is usually shorter still, as in Example (Three slopes of a crime regression); Scheffé’s multiplier is earned when functions are picked after the estimates are seen.

13.2.5. All contrasts in a one-way layout

In the one-way model E(ykj)=μ+αk with g levels, nk≥1 observations at level k and n=∑knk, the estimable functions of the effects alone are the contrasts ψ=∑kckμk with ∑kck=0 (Theorem 8.7.1). They form a space of dimension g−1, the test space of the hypothesis of equal means. The estimate is ψ^=∑kcky¯k with standard error s(∑kck2/nk)1/2, and ν=n−g. Scheffé’s intervals are

∑kcky¯k±(g−1)Fα(g−1,n−g)s(∑kck2nk)1/2for every contrast c,
(13.2.4)

with simultaneous coverage exactly 1−α, balanced or not. The most significant contrast has ck=nk(y¯k−y¯) (Exercise (B1)), and its squared t statistic is (g−1)F, where F is the one-way F statistic.

Example (Education and the perceived position of a candidate)

The 1996 American National Election Study asked respondents to place Bill Clinton on a seven-point scale from extremely liberal (1) to extremely conservative (7). Group the n=944 respondents by education, coded from 1 (least) to 7 (most). The group sizes are 13, 52, 248, 187, 90, 227, 127, and the mean placements are 4.08, 3.58, 3.21, 2.95, 2.79, 2.66, 2.65. More educated respondents place Clinton further to the left. The pooled standard deviation is s=1.354 on ν=937 degrees of freedom, and the one-way F statistic is 7.88, with p-value 2.7×10−8. The between-groups sum of squares is 86.72.

Having seen the means, one might ask how the two least educated groups compare with the two most educated, the contrast ψ=12(μ1+μ2)−12(μ6+μ7). Its estimate is 1.176 with standard error 0.223, so t=5.27. Because the contrast was suggested by the data, the unadjusted t interval is not legitimate. The Scheffé interval Eq. 13.2.4, with multiplier 6F0.05(6,937)=3.557, is [0.383, 1.969], and it is legitimate however the contrast was found. The most significant contrast of all has t=6.88=6×7.88, and its sum of squares is the whole between-groups sum of squares.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats
anes = sm.datasets.anes96.load_pandas().data

y = anes["ClinLR"].to_numpy()
level = anes["educ"].to_numpy().astype(int) - 1       # 0, ..., 6
g = 7
n_k = np.bincount(level).astype(float)
means = np.bincount(level, weights=y) / n_k
n = len(y)
nu = n - g                                            # error degrees of freedom
s2 = np.sum((y - means[level]) ** 2) / nu             # pooled variance estimate
s = np.sqrt(s2)
between = np.sum(n_k * (means - y.mean()) ** 2)
F = between / (g - 1) / s2
print("group sizes", n_k.astype(int))
print("means      ", means.round(3))
print(f"s = {s:.4f} on {nu} df;  F = {F:.3f},  p = {stats.f.sf(F, g - 1, nu):.2g}")
alpha = 0.05
c_sch = np.sqrt((g - 1) * stats.f.ppf(1 - alpha, g - 1, nu))   # Scheffe multiplier, q = g - 1

def contrast(c):
    """Estimate, standard error and sum of squares of the contrast sum_k c_k mu_k."""
    est = c @ means
    v = np.sum(c ** 2 / n_k)
    return est, s * np.sqrt(v), est ** 2 / v

c_max = n_k * (means - y.mean())                      # the most significant contrast
c_lohi = np.array([0.5, 0.5, 0, 0, 0, -0.5, -0.5])    # levels 1-2 against levels 6-7
for name, c in (("maximal", c_max), ("1-2 vs 6-7", c_lohi)):
    est, se, ss = contrast(c)
    print(f"{name:11s} estimate {est:8.3f}  t = {est / se:6.2f}  SS = {ss:8.2f}"
          f"  Scheffe interval [{est - c_sch * se:.3f}, {est + c_sch * se:.3f}]")
print(f"between-groups SS = {between:.2f};  Scheffe multiplier = {c_sch:.3f}")

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that Scheffé’s multiplier cannot be reduced: if c<qFα(q,ν), the intervals 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^±cse(λ⊤β^), 𝛌λ∈C(Λ), have simultaneous coverage less than 1−α. So among intervals of the form estimate ± constant × standard error, valid for the whole family, Scheffé’s are the shortest.

Solution. By Eq. 13.2.3, all intervals cover iff the maximal squared t statistic, which has q times an F(q,ν) distribution, is at most c2. So the coverage is Pr{F(q,ν)≤c2/q}, which is less than 1−α when c2/q<Fα(q,ν), because the F distribution has a positive density.

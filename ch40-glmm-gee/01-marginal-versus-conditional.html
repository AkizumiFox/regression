40.1  Marginal versus conditional models

A clinician asks what a treatment would do to this patient’s odds of recovery; a health minister asks what it would do to the recovery rate of the whole population. In a linear model the two questions have the same answer, because averaging a straight line over patients returns the same straight line. As soon as the link is nonlinear they do not, and no amount of data removes the difference: it is a property of the model, not of the sample. This section sets up the two targets and proves that the population-averaged coefficient of a logistic model is strictly smaller in magnitude than the individual one.

40.1.1. Two coefficients, one model

Throughout this chapter the data are clustered: units i=1,…,m each carry ni observations Yi1,…,Yini, with n=∑ini — a patient observed repeatedly, a school with its pupils, a firm across years. The simplest model that makes observations within a cluster dependent gives each cluster its own level Ui, with the link g of Chapter 34:

g{E(Yij∣Ui)}=α+βxij+Ui,Ui∼N(0,τ2),
(40.1.1)

with Yi1,…,Yini conditionally independent given Ui. Here β is a conditional, or subject-specific, coefficient: it compares two covariate values within one cluster, with that cluster’s level held wherever it happens to be. Write it βC when the distinction matters.

The other target concerns the response averaged over clusters. Write h=g−1 and

m(x)=E{h(α+βx+U)},U∼N(0,τ2),
(40.1.2)

for the marginal mean function: the mean response at x in a cluster drawn at random. The marginal, or population-averaged, coefficients are the ones a model with the same link would report if fitted to the population: the solution (αM,βM) of

E[(1X){m(X)−h(αM+βMX)}]=0,
(40.1.3)

the expectation being over the covariate distribution, and assumed to have a unique solution — which it does whenever E{(1,X)⊤(1,X)} is nonsingular and the marginal mean function stays away from the ends of the range of h. This is the population version of the likelihood equations Eq. 34.3.1 with the dispersion and the prior weights dropped; for a canonical link the remaining factor h′(η)/V(μ) is identically one, so for the logit, log and identity links of this section (αM,βM) is exactly what an ordinary generalized linear model fitted to the clustered data estimates consistently — a point Section 40.4 returns to. For a binary covariate Eq. 40.1.3 is solved by matching the two means exactly, h(αM)=m(0) and h(αM+βM)=m(1), so that βM is the log odds ratio between the two marginal probabilities.

Warning. Neither coefficient is “the” right one and neither is an approximation to the other. They are answers to different questions, and a paper that reports one while discussing the other has made a substantive error, not a numerical one.

40.1.2. The attenuation inequality

Proposition 40.1.1 (Marginal and conditional coefficients)

Let U have mean zero and let h be the inverse link of the model Eq. 40.1.1.

  • (a) Identity link. If h(η)=η then m(x)=α+βx, so αM=α and βM=β for every distribution of U with mean zero. The two targets coincide.

  • (b) Log link. If h(η)=eη and EeU<∞, then m(x)=E(eU)eα+βx, so βM=β and αM=α+log⁡EeU. For U∼N(0,τ2) the shift is τ2/2. Only the intercept moves.

  • (c) Probit link. If h=Φ and U∼N(0,τ2) then

    m(x)=Φ(α+βx1+τ2),
    (40.1.4)

    so the marginal mean function is again a probit, with αM=α/1+τ2 and βM=β/1+τ2.

  • (d) Logit link. Let h(η)=eη/(1+eη), let U be any random variable with 0<Var{h(θ+U)}<∞ for every θ, and put Pθ=h(θ+U) and F(θ)=g{EPθ}, the marginal linear predictor. Then

    F′(θ)=1−Var(Pθ)E(Pθ)E(1−Pθ)∈(0,1).
    (40.1.5)

    Consequently, if β≠0, then for any x1<x2 the marginal log odds ratio is strictly attenuated: 0<sign(β){gm(x2)−gm(x1)}<|β|(x2−x1). In particular a binary covariate has 0<|βM|<|βC|.

Proof.

  • (a) E(α+βx+U)=α+βx, and this is exactly Eq. 40.1.3 with h the identity.

  • (b) Eeα+βx+U=eα+βxEeU. For a normal U, Theorem 3.2.1 gives EeU=eτ2/2. The right-hand side is again of the form exp⁡(αM+βMx), so it solves Eq. 40.1.3 exactly.

  • (c) Let Z∼N(0,1) be independent of U and write η=α+βx. Then EΦ(η+U)=EPr(Z≤η+U∣U)=Pr(Z−U≤η), and Z−U∼N(0,1+τ2), which is Eq. 40.1.4. Again the result is of the assumed form, so it solves Eq. 40.1.3.

  • (d) Write Pθ=h(θ+U) and p¯(θ)=EPθ. The logistic inverse link satisfies h′=h(1−h), so differentiating under the expectation (legitimate because 0≤h′≤1/4, so the difference quotients are dominated) gives p¯′(θ)=E{Pθ(1−Pθ)}. Since F(θ)=log⁡p¯(θ)−log⁡{1−p¯(θ)}, F′(θ)=p¯′(θ)p¯(θ){1−p¯(θ)}=E(Pθ)−E(Pθ2)E(Pθ)E(1−Pθ). The denominator is E(Pθ)−{E(Pθ)}2, so numerator minus denominator equals {E(Pθ)}2−E(Pθ2)=−Var(Pθ), which gives Eq. 40.1.5. The ratio is positive because Pθ∈(0,1) makes E{Pθ(1−Pθ)}>0, and it is strictly less than one because Var(Pθ)>0. Integrating F′ from α+βx1 to α+βx2, an interval of positive length because β≠0, gives the stated inequality; for a binary covariate the left-hand side is βM by the remark after Eq. 40.1.3.

Part (d) needs no normality of U, no particular covariate and no asymptotics; it is an identity about the logistic function. Its factor F′(θ) is one minus the ratio of the variance of the individual probabilities to the variance a single Bernoulli variable with the average probability would have, so the more the clusters differ, the flatter the population curve.

Idea. A nonlinear link and a random effect do not commute. Averaging h(η+U) over U gives a different function of η, and for the logit and probit it is a flatter one. The linear and log links are the exceptions, because for them averaging changes at most an additive or multiplicative constant.

40.1.3. How large is the difference?

The probit factor in Eq. 40.1.4 is exact; the logit has no closed form, but the two links are so nearly proportional that a good approximation comes from pretending it does. Matching a standard logistic distribution to a normal one scaled by c=163/(15π)=0.5881 and applying Eq. 40.1.4 gives the classical factor

βM≈βC1+c2τ2,c=16315π,
(40.1.6)

due to Zeger, Liang and Albert (1988). It is an approximation, not a theorem.

Example (Attenuation in a logistic random-intercept model)

Take α=0, βC=1 and a covariate X∼N(0,1), and solve Eq. 40.1.3 numerically. With τ=0.5 the marginal slope is 0.9502; with τ=1 it is 0.8379; with τ=2 it is 0.6142. The approximation Eq. 40.1.6 gives 0.8620 and 0.6477 in the last two cases, too large by about three and five per cent. The probit factor at τ=1 is 0.7071, far smaller — but that is a different model, with a different meaning for βC. The covariate distribution matters little: at τ=1 a binary covariate gives a marginal log odds ratio of 0.8318. Figure 40.1.1(a) shows why the approximation works at all — the average of the conditional curves is almost exactly a flatter logistic curve — and panel (b) tracks βM/βC as the clusters become more heterogeneous.

import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial.hermite_e import hermegauss
from scipy.optimize import root
from scipy.stats import norm

def expit(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))

# nodes and weights that integrate g(U) over U ~ N(0, 1): sum_k w_k g(z_k)
z_node, z_w = hermegauss(80)
z_w = z_w / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi)

def marginal_curve(eta, tau, link="logit"):
    """E[H(eta + U)] for U ~ N(0, tau^2), by Gauss-Hermite quadrature."""
    H = expit if link == "logit" else norm.cdf
    grid = np.atleast_1d(eta)[..., None] + tau * z_node
    return np.sum(z_w * H(grid), axis=-1)

def beta_marginal(alpha, beta, tau, link="logit"):
    """Population-averaged coefficients: the root of E[(1, x){m(x) - H(a + b x)}] = 0
    when x ~ N(0, 1), with m the marginal curve of the conditional model."""
    H = expit if link == "logit" else norm.cdf
    m = marginal_curve(alpha + beta * z_node, tau, link)

    def score(par):
        resid = m - H(par[0] + par[1] * z_node)
        return [np.sum(z_w * resid), np.sum(z_w * z_node * resid)]

    sol = root(score, [alpha, beta], tol=1e-13)
    return sol.x

C_LOGIT = 16 * np.sqrt(3) / (15 * np.pi)

def approx_factor(tau, link="logit"):
    c = C_LOGIT if link == "logit" else 1.0
    return 1.0 / np.sqrt(1.0 + c**2 * tau**2)

for t in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    print(f"tau = {t}:  logit {beta_marginal(0.0, 1.0, t)[1]:.4f}"
          f"   approximation {approx_factor(t):.4f}"
          f"   probit {1 / np.sqrt(1 + t**2):.4f}")

40.1.4. Attenuation is not bias

Attenuation is not bias: both coefficients are correct answers to different questions. The phenomenon is the non-collapsibility of the odds ratio, met in Section 35.5 as Example (The odds ratio does not collapse) — averaging 2×2 tables that all share one odds ratio produces a table whose odds ratio is closer to one, with no confounding whatever. Risk differences and risk ratios do not behave this way, which is parts (a) and (b).

It follows that the conditional coefficient depends on what else is in the model: adding a covariate that explains part of the cluster heterogeneity lowers τ2 and, by Eq. 40.1.5, moves the conditional and marginal coefficients of the other covariates closer together. That is not evidence of confounding. Nor is either coefficient a causal quantity by itself: randomization (Theorem 25.2.3) licenses a causal reading of the marginal contrast between treated and untreated, but does not turn the marginal log odds ratio into the conditional one, and does not license a conditional reading of βC unless the random effect really is a property of the cluster that the intervention leaves alone.

Which target, then? The conditional coefficient answers questions about a change within a unit — a dose for a patient, a policy for a school — and is comparable across studies with different amounts of unmeasured heterogeneity, provided the random-effect distribution is right. The marginal coefficient answers questions about a population total or rate, and is safer when the cluster structure is a nuisance, because it assumes nothing about the cluster effects. For the identity link the distinction disappears, which is why Chapter 32 never raised it: in Definition 32.2.1 the marginal model Eq. 32.2.2 has the same 𝛃Xβ as the conditional one.

40.1.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

A random-intercept logistic model for whether a pupil passes an examination has β^C=0.60 for one extra hour of study per week and τ^=1.2. Use Eq. 40.1.6 to say roughly what a population-averaged logistic model would report, and state in one sentence each what the two numbers mean.

Solution. The factor is (1+0.58812×1.22)−1/2. Now 0.58812=0.3459 and 1.22=1.44, so the factor is (1+0.4981)−1/2=1.4981−1/2≈0.817, giving β^M≈0.49. The first says: for a given pupil, an extra hour of study multiplies the odds of passing by e0.60≈1.82. The second says: comparing two randomly chosen pupils from the population who differ by an hour of study, the odds ratio is about e0.49≈1.63.

Exercise (A2)

A Poisson random-intercept model with a log link is fitted and reports α^=−1.2, β^=0.35, τ^2=0.8. What does a population-averaged log-linear model estimate? Which of the three numbers changes?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Use Eq. 40.1.5 to show that in a logistic random-intercept model with Pr(Y=1∣U)=h(U) and U symmetric about 0, the attenuation factor at θ=0 equals 1−4Var{h(U)}. Evaluate it for U∼N(0,1), for which Var{h(U)}=0.0434, and compare with the ratio 0.8379 of Example (Attenuation in a logistic random-intercept model).

Solution. By symmetry Eh(U)=1/2, so the denominator of Eq. 40.1.5 is 1/4 and F′(0)=1−4Var{h(U)}. With Var{h(U)}=0.0434 this is 1−4×0.0434=0.8264. This is the local attenuation at θ=0, and it is the strongest: as |θ| grows, Pθ concentrates near 0 or 1, its variance vanishes faster than EPθE(1−Pθ) and F′→1. The global ratio βM/βC=0.8379 of Example (Attenuation in a logistic random-intercept model) is accordingly a little larger, because Eq. 40.1.3 averages F′ over the whole range of X.

Exercise (B2)

Extend Proposition 40.1.1(c) to a probit model with a random slope: Pr(Y=1∣U0,U1)=Φ(α+βx+U0+U1x) with (U0,U1)∼N2(0,G). Show that the marginal curve is Φ{(α+βx)/s(x)} with s(x)2=1+(1,x)G(1,x)⊤, and explain why the marginal model is now not a probit model in x at all.

Solution. Exactly as in the proof of (c), with Z−U0−U1x normal with mean zero and variance 1+g00+2g01x+g11x2=s(x)2. The marginal linear predictor (α+βx)/s(x) is a ratio of a linear function to the square root of a quadratic, which is linear in x only if g11=g01=0. With a random slope the attenuation is different at different x, so no single marginal coefficient describes it.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Eq. 40.1.6 is an approximation built for a normal random effect. This exercise computes the attenuation exactly for a two-point one and shows how different the answer can be. Take a binary covariate and the model logitPr(Y=1∣x,U)=βx+U, where U=±c with probability 12 each, c>0 and β>0.

  • (a) Show that m(0)=12 exactly, so that αM=0 and βM is the marginal log odds at x=1.

  • (b) Show that βM=log⁡{1+e2β−11+eβcosh⁡c}=β+log⁡cosh⁡c+eβ1+eβcosh⁡c.

  • (c) Check that βM=β at c=0, that βM decreases in c, and that 0<βM<β for c>0, as Proposition 40.1.1(d) requires.

  • (d) Show that βM→0 as c→∞, and that βM∼4sinh⁡(β)e−c. Since Var(U)=c2, compare with the prediction of Eq. 40.1.6 at τ=c. What does the comparison say about that formula?

Solution.

  • (a) m(0)=12{h(c)+h(−c)}=12, because h(−η)=1−h(η) for the logistic h. So gm(0)=0, and by the remark after Eq. 40.1.3 a binary covariate matches the two marginal means exactly, giving αM=0 and βM=gm(1).

  • (b) Write b=eβ and t=ec. Then h(β+c)=bt/(1+bt) and h(β−c)=b/(t+b), so h(β+c)+h(β−c)=b{t(t+b)+(1+bt)}(1+bt)(t+b)=b(t2+1+2bt)t(1+b2)+b(1+t2). Hence 2m(1) is that expression, and 2{1−m(1)}={2t(1+b2)+2b(1+t2)−b(t2+1+2bt)}/{t(1+b2)+b(1+t2)}, whose numerator simplifies to 2t+b+bt2. The marginal odds are therefore m(1)1−m(1)=b(t2+1+2bt)b(1+t2)+2t=b(cosh⁡c+b)1+bcosh⁡c, after dividing numerator and denominator by 2t and using (t2+1)/(2t)=cosh⁡c. Taking logarithms gives the second form; and b(cosh⁡c+b)/(1+bcosh⁡c)=1+(b2−1)/(1+bcosh⁡c) gives the first.

  • (c) At c=0, cosh⁡c=1 and the first form is log⁡{1+(b2−1)/(1+b)}=log⁡b=β. The first form is visibly decreasing in cosh⁡c, hence in c, because b2>1; and it stays positive for the same reason. This is Proposition 40.1.1(d) in closed form.

  • (d) As c→∞, (b2−1)/(1+bcosh⁡c)→0, so βM→0; and log⁡(1+u)∼u with cosh⁡c∼ec/2 gives βM∼(b2−1)/(bec/2)=2(b−b−1)e−c=4sinh⁡(β)e−c. The approximation Eq. 40.1.6, applied at τ=c, gives β/1+c2⋅0.58812, which falls only like 1/c. At c=6, say, the exact attenuation is of order e−6 while the formula still reports a third of β. The formula is not a general fact about attenuation: it is a calculation for a normal U, where the tails of the random effect keep some clusters at intermediate probabilities, and it fails for a U that pushes every cluster to the flat ends of the logistic curve. Attenuation depends on the whole distribution of U, not on τ alone.

Exercise (C2)

With a random slope the marginal coefficient need not even share the sign of the conditional one. Extend Exercise (B2) to the logit: let x be binary and logitPr(Y=1∣x,U1)=α+βx+U1x,U1=±d with probability 12, and take α=log⁡3, β=log⁡2, d=log⁡6.

  • (a) Show that the two clusters have conditional log odds ratios β+d=log⁡12 and β−d=log⁡13, whose mean is β=log⁡2>0.

  • (b) Show that m(0)=111148 and m(1)=109148, so that the marginal odds ratio is 109117<1 and βM<0.

  • (c) Show that the marginal risk difference is −174 and equals the average of the two cluster-specific risk differences 33148 and −37148, while the marginal log odds ratio is not the average of the two conditional ones.

  • (d) Explain why Proposition 40.1.1(d) does not apply, and why “attenuation” is the wrong word for what has happened.

Solution.

  • (a) Within a cluster, U1 is fixed, so the log odds at x=1 minus the log odds at x=0 is β+U1, namely log⁡2+log⁡6=log⁡12 or log⁡2−log⁡6=log⁡13. The mean over clusters is β because EU1=0.

  • (b) At x=0 the random slope drops out, so m(0)=h(α)=3/4=111/148. At x=1 the linear predictors are α+β+d=log⁡36 and α+β−d=log⁡1=0, so m(1)=12(3637+12)=12⋅10974=109148. The marginal odds are (109/148)/(39/148)=109/39 at x=1 and 3 at x=0, so the marginal odds ratio is 109/117, less than one.

  • (c) m(1)−m(0)=−2/148=−1/74. The cluster-specific risk differences are 3637−34=144−111148=33148 and 12−34=−37148, whose average is (33−37)/296=−1/74. Equality holds because m(1)=Eh(α+β+U1) is itself an average, so risk differences pass through it; log odds ratios do not, and log⁡(109/117)≠12{log⁡12+log⁡13}=log⁡2.

  • (d) Part (d) of the proposition concerns h(θ+U), a random level added to a common linear predictor, and its proof differentiates θ↦gEh(θ+U). Here the random term multiplies x, so the two conditional curves have different slopes and the argument does not start. The conclusion fails too: βM and βC have opposite signs. Calling the difference attenuation would suggest that the marginal coefficient is a shrunken version of the conditional one, and here it is not a version of it at all — βC is an average on the log odds scale, dominated by the cluster in which the treatment helps a great deal, while m(1) is an average of probabilities, in which the cluster where the treatment hurts costs more than the other gains. Two averages on two scales, of a quantity that varies across clusters, and they need not agree even in sign; the warning after Eq. 40.1.3 applies with full force.

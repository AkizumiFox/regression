9.2  Extra sums of squares

Most questions asked of a regression compare two models. Each question names a reduced model inside the full one, and the natural measure of what the full model adds is how much it lowers the residual sum of squares. This section develops that measure, its computation when regressors are added to a fitted model, and a closed form when the reduced model is defined by linear constraints.

9.2.1. Notation

Definition 9.2.1 (Extra sum of squares)

Let X1 and X2 be matrices with n rows, let X=[X1,X2], and let M1 and M be the projections onto C(X1) and C(X). The extra sum of squares of X2 given X1 is SS(X2∣X1)=y⊤(M−M1)y=SSE(X1)−SSE(X), with rank(X)−rank(X1) degrees of freedom. Here SSE(⋅) is the residual sum of squares of the model with the given model matrix.

The two expressions agree by Eq. 6.5.2. One also writes 𝛃𝛃SS(β2∣β1), or SS(B∣μ,A) in factor models; some books write R, for “reduction”. Four properties follow from Theorem 6.5.1 and Theorem 6.7.1.

  1. It depends only on the two column spaces. Rescaling, recoding, centring, replacing X2 by X2+X1B, and rank deficiency change nothing.

  2. It is nonnegative, and zero iff My=M1y, that is, iff adding X2 leaves the fitted values unchanged.

  3. It obeys a chain rule: SS(X2,X3∣X1)=SS(X2∣X1)+SS(X3∣X1,X2), because the projections telescope.

  4. It is the sum of squares of a residualized regression. The next lemma makes this precise.

Lemma 9.2.1 (Extra sums of squares by residualizing)

For any X1 and X2, let X~2=(I−M1)X2. Then C([X1,X2])=C(X1)⊕C(X~2) with the two summands orthogonal, so M=M1+PC(X~2) and SS(X2∣X1)=‖PC(X~2)y‖2=y⊤X~2(X~2⊤X~2)−X~2⊤y.

Proof. The argument for part (a) of Lemma 6.6.1 uses no rank condition, so it applies here: the columns of X~2 lie in C(X) and are orthogonal to C(X1), and every X1b1+X2b2 equals X~2b2+(X1b1+M1X2b2). By Theorem 6.3.7, M=M1+PC(X~2). The formula is Theorem 6.3.6 applied to X~2.

9.2.2. Adding regressors to a fitted model

Suppose 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ has been fitted and we add the columns of an n×t matrix Z, giving 𝛃𝛄E(Y)=Xβ+Zγ with model matrix W=[X,Z]. Only a t×t matrix has to be inverted.

Theorem 9.2.2 (Adding regressors)

Let W=[X,Z] have full column rank p+t, let C=(X⊤X)−1, 𝛃β^=CX⊤y, and R=I−XCX⊤. Then:

  • (a) Z⊤RZ is positive definite;

  • (b) the least squares coefficients of the enlarged model are 𝛄𝛃𝛃𝛄γ^=(Z⊤RZ)−1Z⊤Ry,β^W=β^−Lγ^,L=CX⊤Z;

  • (c) the residual sum of squares falls by 𝛄𝛄𝛄SS(Z∣X)=y⊤RZ(Z⊤RZ)−1Z⊤Ry=γ^⊤Z⊤Ry=γ^⊤(Z⊤RZ)γ^;

  • (d) if Cov(Y)=σ2I, then 𝛄Cov(γ^)=σ2(Z⊤RZ)−1, 𝛃𝛄𝛄Cov(β^W,γ^)=−LCov(γ^) and 𝛃Cov(β^W)=σ2[C+L(Z⊤RZ)−1L⊤].

Proof.

  • (a) R is the symmetric idempotent residual projection of the model X, so a⊤Z⊤RZa=‖RZa‖2≥0. If RZa=0, then Za∈C(X), say Za=Xb, so W(−b⊤,a⊤)⊤=0 and full column rank forces a=0.

  • (b) Apply the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem (Theorem 6.6.2) with X1=Z and X2=X. Then M2=I−R and X~1=RZ, so part (a) of that theorem gives 𝛄γ^=(Z⊤RRZ)−1Z⊤Ry, which is the stated formula because R is symmetric and idempotent. Part (c) of the theorem gives 𝛃𝛄𝛃𝛄β^W=CX⊤(y−Zγ^)=β^−Lγ^.

  • (c) By Lemma 9.2.1, the extra sum of squares is the squared length of the projection of y onto C(RZ), which is y⊤RZ(Z⊤RZ)−1Z⊤Ry by Theorem 6.3.6. Substituting (b) gives the other two forms.

  • (d) Both 𝛄γ^ and 𝛃β^ are linear in y, and by Theorem 2.2.1 𝛄𝛃𝛄Cov(γ^)=σ2(Z⊤RZ)−1Z⊤RRZ(Z⊤RZ)−1=σ2(Z⊤RZ)−1,Cov(β^,γ^)=σ2CX⊤RZ(Z⊤RZ)−1=0, since X⊤R=0. With 𝛃Cov(β^)=σ2C (Theorem 5.5.1), the formula 𝛃𝛃𝛄β^W=β^−Lγ^ gives 𝛃𝛄𝛄Cov(β^W,γ^)=−LCov(γ^) and 𝛃𝛃𝛄Cov(β^W)=Cov(β^)+LCov(γ^)L⊤.

Since L(Z⊤RZ)−1L⊤ is nonnegative definite, part (d) shows that adding regressors never decreases the variances of the coefficients already in the model, when both models are correct (compare Exercise (B2), and see Exercise (C2)). Chapter 26 measures this price of adjustment as variance inflation (Definition 26.2.1). In computation one updates a QR factorization instead of using these formulas (Chapter 10).

Example (Adding poverty and urbanization)

In the state data, fit the murder rate on single parenthood first: y^=−8.2146+0.5147xsingle, with residual sum of squares 121.92. Now add Z=[xpoverty,xurban]. The listing computes 𝛄γ^=(0.2538,0.0046) from the old residual projection alone, and corrects the old coefficients to −9.0946 and 0.3980. These agree with a fresh fit of the enlarged model. The residual sum of squares falls by 19.96, on 2 degrees of freedom, to 101.96. Single parenthood’s coefficient drops by almost a quarter once poverty is allowed to take its share, the pattern seen in Example (Order matters).

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data.drop(index="District of Columbia")
y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)
one = np.ones(n)
cols = {"poverty": data["poverty"].to_numpy(), "single": data["single"].to_numpy(),
        "urban": data["urban"].to_numpy()}

X = np.column_stack([one, cols["single"]])                 # the model already fitted
Z = np.column_stack([cols["poverty"], cols["urban"]])       # the regressors to add
XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
beta_X = XtX_inv @ X.T @ y
R = np.eye(n) - X @ XtX_inv @ X.T                           # residual projection of the old model

gamma = np.linalg.solve(Z.T @ R @ Z, Z.T @ R @ y)          # new coefficients
beta_new = beta_X - XtX_inv @ X.T @ Z @ gamma               # old coefficients, corrected
extra_ss = gamma @ Z.T @ R @ y                              # SS(Z | X) = SSE_X - SSE_W
print("gamma =", gamma.round(4), " corrected beta =", beta_new.round(4))
print(f"SSE_X = {y @ R @ y:.2f},  extra SS = {extra_ss:.2f}")

9.2.3. One column at a time

With t=1, Theorem 9.2.2 links the extra sum of squares of a single regressor to its coefficient and standard error.

Corollary 9.2.3 (The extra sum of squares of one column)

Let X have full column rank p, write cjj for the jth diagonal entry of (X⊤X)−1, and let X(j) be X without its jth column. Then SS(xj∣X(j))=β^j2cjj=tj2s2,tj=β^jscjj,s2=SSEn−p. Consequently, the ratio of this one-degree-of-freedom sum of squares to the residual mean square is tj2.

Proof. Permute the columns so that xj comes last; this carries cjj to the last diagonal entry of the inverse. Write X=[X(j),xj] and R=I−X(j)(X(j)⊤X(j))−1X(j)⊤ for the residual projection of X(j). By Theorem 1.6.4 the last diagonal entry of (X⊤X)−1 is the inverse of the Schur complement xj⊤xj−xj⊤X(j)(X(j)⊤X(j))−1X(j)⊤xj=xj⊤Rxj, so xj⊤Rxj=1/cjj. Now apply Theorem 9.2.2 with X(j) as the old model and Z=xj: then γ^=β^j, and part (c) gives SS(xj∣X(j))=β^j2xj⊤Rxj=β^j2/cjj. The definition of tj gives the last form.

For poverty in the three-regressor model, β^=0.2538 and cjj=0.003935, so the extra sum of squares is 0.25382/0.003935=16.37. This is exactly the last entry for poverty in Order B of Example (Order matters). The t statistic is 2.718, and t2s2=7.386×2.217=16.37.

Xf = np.column_stack([one, cols["poverty"], cols["single"], cols["urban"]])
fit = sm.OLS(y, Xf).fit()
C = np.linalg.inv(Xf.T @ Xf)
j = 1                                                       # poverty
partial_ss = fit.params[j] ** 2 / C[j, j]
print(f"beta = {fit.params[j]:.4f}, c_jj = {C[j, j]:.6f}, beta^2/c_jj = {partial_ss:.2f}")
print(f"t = {fit.tvalues[j]:.3f},  t^2 * s^2 = {fit.tvalues[j] ** 2 * fit.scale:.2f}")

9.2.4. Sums of squares for linear hypotheses

Reduced models often come as constraints: two coefficients are equal, a set of coefficients vanishes, a contrast among group means is zero. Section 6.5 identified the test space of such a constraint (Theorem 6.5.2). The next theorem gives its sum of squares in terms of the least squares estimate, with no reduced model fitted.

Theorem 9.2.4 (Sum of squares of a linear hypothesis)

Let Λ be a p×q matrix of rank q such that each entry of 𝛃Λ⊤β is estimable, so that Λ=X⊤T for some n×q matrix 𝛒𝛒T=[ρ1,…,ρq] (Definition 8.2.1, Theorem 6.4.4). Let G be any generalized inverse of X⊤X, let 𝛃β^ be any least squares estimate, and let d∈Rq. Then:

  • (a) 𝛃Λ⊤β^ and Λ⊤GΛ do not depend on the choices of 𝛃β^ and G, and Λ⊤GΛ is positive definite;

  • (b) the subspace S0={Xb:Λ⊤b=0} of C(X) has orthogonal complement in C(X) equal to C(MT), of dimension q, and the projection onto C(MT) is MT(Λ⊤GΛ)−1T⊤M;

  • (c) the minimum of ‖y−Xb‖2 over {b:Λ⊤b=d} exceeds SSE by

    𝛃𝛃SSH=(Λ⊤β^−d)⊤(Λ⊤GΛ)−1(Λ⊤β^−d).
    (9.2.1)

Proof.

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃Λ⊤β^=T⊤Xβ^=T⊤My and Λ⊤GΛ=T⊤XGX⊤T=T⊤MT by Theorem 6.3.6, and neither expression involves the choices. Since X⊤M=X⊤, we have X⊤(MT)=Λ, which has rank q, so the q columns of MT are linearly independent. Hence T⊤MT=(MT)⊤(MT) is positive definite.

  • (b) Λ⊤b=T⊤Xb, so S0=C(X)∩N(T⊤). Theorem 6.5.2 identifies its orthogonal complement in C(X) as C(MT). The columns of MT are independent, so Theorem 6.3.6 gives the projection MT(T⊤MT)−1T⊤M, and T⊤MT=Λ⊤GΛ by (a).

  • (c) First let d=0. Minimizing over Λ⊤b=0 is minimizing 𝛍‖y−μ‖2 over 𝛍μ∈S0, so by Eq. 6.5.2 the excess over SSE is the squared length of the projection of y onto C(X)⊖S0=C(MT), where ⊖ denotes the orthogonal complement within C(X). By (b) this is y⊤MT(Λ⊤GΛ)−1T⊤My, and 𝛃T⊤My=Λ⊤β^. For general d, the rows of Λ⊤ are independent, so there is a b0 with Λ⊤b0=d. Substituting b=b0+b∗ turns the problem into minimizing ‖y∗−Xb∗‖2 subject to Λ⊤b∗=0, with y∗=y−Xb0. The data y∗ have the same residual sum of squares as y, and 𝛃β^−b0 is a least squares estimate for them, since 𝛃X(β^−b0)=My∗. Applying the case d=0 to y∗ gives Eq. 9.2.1, because 𝛃𝛃Λ⊤(β^−b0)=Λ⊤β^−d.

In Eq. 9.2.1, 𝛃Λ⊤β^−d measures how far the estimate is from the hypothesis, and σ2Λ⊤GΛ is the covariance of 𝛃Λ⊤β^ (Chapter 7), so SSH/σ2 is the squared Mahalanobis length of the discrepancy (Proposition 3.2.3). No reduced model has to be fitted.

In the full-rank case the constrained minimizer itself has a closed form, which recovers the classical restricted least squares estimator.

Theorem 9.2.5 (Restricted least squares)

Let X have full column rank, C=(X⊤X)−1, and let Λ (p×q) have rank q. For any d∈Rq, the unique minimizer of ‖y−Xb‖2 subject to Λ⊤b=d is 𝛃𝛃𝛃β^H=β^−CΛ(Λ⊤CΛ)−1(Λ⊤β^−d), and 𝛃𝛃𝛃‖y−Xβ^H‖2=SSE+‖X(β^−β^H)‖2, where the last term equals SSH of Eq. 9.2.1 with G=C.

Proof. Λ⊤CΛ is positive definite because C is and Λ has full column rank, and 𝛃𝛃𝛃Λ⊤β^H=Λ⊤β^−(Λ⊤β^−d)=d. For any b, Eq. 6.3.1 with u=Xb gives 𝛃‖y−Xb‖2=SSE+‖X(β^−b)‖2. Every feasible b has the form 𝛃β^H+v with Λ⊤v=0, and then 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃‖X(β^−b)‖2=‖X(β^−β^H)‖2−2(β^−β^H)⊤X⊤Xv+‖Xv‖2. The cross term vanishes: 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃(β^−β^H)⊤X⊤Xv=(Λ⊤β^−d)⊤(Λ⊤CΛ)−1Λ⊤CX⊤Xv=(Λ⊤β^−d)⊤(Λ⊤CΛ)−1Λ⊤v=0. So the objective is 𝛃𝛃SSE+‖X(β^−β^H)‖2+‖Xv‖2, minimized exactly when Xv=0, that is, v=0. Finally 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃‖X(β^−β^H)‖2=(Λ⊤β^−d)⊤(Λ⊤CΛ)−1Λ⊤CX⊤XCΛ(Λ⊤CΛ)−1(Λ⊤β^−d), which simplifies to SSH.

The same estimator comes out of Lagrange multipliers (Proposition 1.11.3 with A=X⊤X). The proof above shows instead that the constrained fit is the feasible point nearest to 𝛃β^ in the geometry of X⊤X.

Example (Equal effects of poverty and single parenthood)

Both poverty and single parenthood are percentages of the population. Is one percentage point of either associated with the same change in the murder rate? The hypothesis is 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=0 with 𝛌λ=(0,1,−1,0)⊤. Formula Eq. 9.2.1 gives SSH=1.807 on one degree of freedom, identical to the drop in residual sum of squares when the two regressors are replaced by their sum. Its ratio to the residual mean square is 0.815, so the data are entirely compatible with equal effects. For a hypothesis with a nonzero right-hand side, βsingle=0.5, Theorem 9.2.5 gives the constrained coefficients 0.1803 for poverty and −0.0044 for urbanization, and a sum of squares of 3.357, both confirmed by a direct fit of the reduced model.

def hypothesis_ss(X, y, Lam, d=None):
    """(L'b - d)' [L' (X'X)^- L]^{-1} (L'b - d) for estimable L'beta = d."""
    G = np.linalg.pinv(X.T @ X)
    b = G @ X.T @ y
    u = Lam.T @ b - (0 if d is None else d)
    return u @ np.linalg.solve(Lam.T @ G @ Lam, u)

def sse(Z):
    b, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Z, y, rcond=None)
    e = y - Z @ b
    return e @ e

Lam_equal = np.array([[0.0, 1.0, -1.0, 0.0]]).T             # beta_poverty = beta_single
ss_equal = hypothesis_ss(Xf, y, Lam_equal)
X_equal = np.column_stack([one, cols["poverty"] + cols["single"], cols["urban"]])
print(f"H: equal slopes   formula {ss_equal:.3f}   SSE_0 - SSE {sse(X_equal) - sse(Xf):.3f}")

9.2.5. Constraints that are not estimable

Without estimability, the reduced model S0={Xb:Λ⊤b=0} is still defined, but it may be larger than the number of constraints suggests.

Proposition 9.2.6 (How much a constraint restricts)

For any p×q matrix Λ, the subspace S0={Xb:Λ⊤b=0} has dimension r−dim⁡(C(Λ)∩C(X⊤)). So the extra sum of squares for the constraint has dim⁡(C(Λ)∩C(X⊤)) degrees of freedom. It is identically zero iff no nonzero linear combination of the constrained functions is estimable.

Proof. S0 is the image of N(Λ⊤) under b↦Xb, and the kernel of this map restricted to N(Λ⊤) is N(Λ⊤)∩N(X), the null space of the stacked matrix [Λ,X⊤]⊤. By rank–nullity (Theorem 1.2.2), dim⁡S0=dim⁡N(Λ⊤)−dim⁡(N(Λ⊤)∩N(X))=(p−rankΛ)−(p−rank[Λ,X⊤]). The dimension formula for a sum of subspaces (Section 6.2) gives rank[Λ,X⊤]=dim⁡(C(Λ)+C(X⊤))=rankΛ+r−dim⁡(C(Λ)∩C(X⊤)). Substituting, dim⁡S0=r−dim⁡(C(Λ)∩C(X⊤)). The degrees of freedom of the extra sum of squares are r−dim⁡S0. A combination Λa is estimable iff it lies in C(X⊤) (Theorem 6.4.4), which gives the last statement.

In the one-way layout E(Yij)=μ+αi, the constraint α1=α2 has one degree of freedom. The constraint α1=0 has none: it does not restrict the mean vector, and only picks one of the least squares solutions (Theorem 8.4.2). Software asked to “test” a nonestimable hypothesis either refuses or silently tests its estimable part. Chapter 17 meets this with empty cells (Theorem 17.4.1).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Prove the chain rule SS(X2,X3∣X1)=SS(X2∣X1)+SS(X3∣X1,X2), and deduce SS(X3∣X1,X2)≤SS(X2,X3∣X1). Show by an example from Example (Adding poverty and urbanization) or its listing that SS(X3∣X1,X2)≤SS(X3∣X1) can fail.

Solution. Let M1, M12 and M123 be the projections onto the nested column spaces. Then M123−M1=(M12−M1)+(M123−M12), and the chain rule follows by taking y⊤(⋅)y. Both terms are nonnegative, which gives the inequality. For the failure, the listing of Section 9.3 reports SS(xurban∣1)=6.30 but SS(xurban∣1,xpoverty)=29.26. Adjusting for poverty more than quadruples the sum of squares for urbanization.

Exercise (B2)

In Theorem 9.2.5 with Cov(Y)=σ2I, show that 𝛃Cov(β^H)=σ2[C−CΛ(Λ⊤CΛ)−1Λ⊤C], that 𝛃𝛃Cov(β^)−Cov(β^H) is nonnegative definite, and that 𝛃𝛃E(β^H)=β when the constraint is true. What is 𝛃E(β^H) when it is false?

Solution. Write K=CΛ(Λ⊤CΛ)−1, so 𝛃𝛃β^H=(I−KΛ⊤)β^+Kd. By Theorem 2.2.1 and 𝛃Cov(β^)=σ2C, 𝛃Cov(β^H)=σ2(I−KΛ⊤)C(I−ΛK⊤). Expanding and using Λ⊤CΛ(Λ⊤CΛ)−1=I, the two cross terms and the last term all equal KΛ⊤C=CΛ(Λ⊤CΛ)−1Λ⊤C, with signs −,−,+, which gives the formula. The difference σ2CΛ(Λ⊤CΛ)−1Λ⊤C is of the form BAB⊤ with A positive definite, hence nonnegative definite. Finally 𝛃𝛃𝛃E(β^H)=β−K(Λ⊤β−d), which is 𝛃β iff 𝛃Λ⊤β=d. A false constraint buys the smaller variance at the price of a bias.

Exercise (B3)

In the one-way layout with model matrix X=[1,z1,…,zg] and group sizes n1,…,ng, use the generalized inverse G=diag(0,1/n1,…,1/ng) in Eq. 9.2.1 to show that the sum of squares for α1=α2 is (y¯1−y¯2)2/(1/n1+1/n2). Verify with Proposition 9.2.6 that the constraint α1=0 has zero degrees of freedom.

Solution. G is a generalized inverse because X⊤X has the block form with first row (n,n1,…,ng) and lower right block diag(n1,…,ng), and a direct multiplication gives X⊤XGX⊤X=X⊤X. With 𝛌λ=(0,1,−1,0,…,0)⊤, GX⊤y=(0,y¯1,…,y¯g)⊤, so 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^=y¯1−y¯2 and 𝛌𝛌λ⊤Gλ=1/n1+1/n2. For α1=0, 𝛌λ=(0,1,0,…,0)⊤ is not in C(X⊤) (Example (Four answers, one fit)), and 𝛌C(λ) is a line, so 𝛌C(λ)∩C(X⊤)={0}.

9.6  Expected mean squares

This section computes the expectations of sums of squares when the data are random, using only two moments, and their distributions under normality. Each mean square estimates σ2 plus a term that vanishes exactly when its hypothesis is true, while the residual mean square estimates σ2 alone. That is the structure of the F ratio, on which Part III builds.

9.6.1. Expected sums of squares for a decomposition

In this section 𝛍E(Y)=μ and Cov(Y)=σ2I. We do not assume at first that 𝛍μ∈C(X), because the effect of a wrong model on the mean squares is part of the story.

Theorem 9.6.1 (Expected mean squares)

Let V1,…,Vk be an analysis of variance decomposition of C(X), with projections Pi and dimensions ri, and let 𝛍E(Y)=μ, Cov(Y)=σ2I.

  • (a) 𝛍E(Y⊤PiY)=σ2ri+‖Piμ‖2, so the mean square of Vi has expectation

    𝛍E(MSi)=σ2+‖Piμ‖2ri.
    (9.6.1)

  • (b) 𝛍E(SSE)=σ2(n−r)+‖(I−M)μ‖2, so E(s2)=σ2 iff 𝛍μ∈C(X).

  • (c) If 𝛍𝛃μ=Xβ with X=[X0,…,Xk] partitioned into terms, the partial sum of squares of Xj has 𝛃𝛃ESS(Xj∣X(−j))=σ2dj+βj⊤Xj⊤(I−M(−j))Xjβj, with dj=rank(M−M(−j)), and the sequential sum of squares of Xj has 𝛃ESS(Xj∣X0,…,Xj−1)=σ2dj′+‖(Mj−Mj−1)Xβ‖2, with dj′=rank(Mj−Mj−1).

  • (d) If moreover Y is normal, then 𝛍Var(Y⊤PiY)=2σ4ri+4σ2‖Piμ‖2, and the sums of squares of different subspaces, including the error space, are uncorrelated.

Proof.

  • (a) By Theorem 2.3.1 with A=Pi and Σ=σ2I, 𝛍𝛍E(Y⊤PiY)=σ2tr(Pi)+μ⊤Piμ. The trace is ri (Proposition 6.3.4), and 𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍μ⊤Piμ=μ⊤Pi⊤Piμ=‖Piμ‖2. Divide by ri.

  • (b) The same with A=I−M, of trace n−r. The second term vanishes iff 𝛍μ∈C(X).

  • (c) For the partial sum of squares, 𝛃(M−M(−j))Xlβl=0 for l≠j, because 𝛃Xlβl lies in C(X(−j)) and is fixed by both projections. And 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃(M−M(−j))Xjβj=Xjβj−M(−j)Xjβj=(I−M(−j))Xjβj. Its squared length is 𝛃𝛃βj⊤Xj⊤(I−M(−j))Xjβj, since I−M(−j) is symmetric and idempotent. The sequential case is (a) for the chain decomposition (Definition 9.3.1).

  • (d) By Theorem 4.2.3(b) with Σ=σ2I, 𝛍𝛍𝛍Var(Y⊤PiY)=2tr{(σ2Pi)2}+4σ2μ⊤PiPiμ=2σ4ri+4σ2‖Piμ‖2. By part (d) of the same theorem the covariance of two forms is 𝛍𝛍2σ4tr(PiPj)+4σ2μ⊤PiPjμ=0 when PiPj=0.

Parts (a) to (c) use nothing beyond second moments. They hold for skewed or heavy-tailed errors, provided the errors are uncorrelated with a common variance. Part (d) uses normality, and without it both the variances and the zero correlations fail in general (Exercise (C1)).

Part (c) confirms Proposition 9.3.1. For full-rank X the term 𝛃‖(I−M(−j))Xjβj‖2 is positive unless 𝛃βj=0, and it is large only if Xj has a large part orthogonal to the other terms. A nearly collinear term has a small expected partial sum of squares even when its coefficients are substantial.

Under normality the expectations become full distributions.

Corollary 9.6.2 (Distributions of the mean squares)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,σ2I) with 𝛍μ∈C(X), and let V1,…,Vk be an analysis of variance decomposition of C(X). Then the sums of squares Y⊤P1Y,…,Y⊤PkY and SSE are mutually independent, 𝛍Y⊤PiYσ2∼χ2(ri, γi),γi=‖Piμ‖2σ2,SSEσ2∼χ2(n−r), and Fi=MSi/s2∼F(ri,n−r,γi), which is central iff 𝛍Piμ=0. The same holds for a single projection P onto any subspace of C(X), such as a partial sum of squares, paired with SSE.

Proof. The projections P1,…,Pk,I−M are mutually orthogonal (Theorem 9.1.1(a)), so Theorem 4.4.4 gives independence and the noncentral chi-squared laws, with 𝛍‖(I−M)μ‖2=0 for the error space. The ratio of two independent chi-squared variables, each divided by its degrees of freedom and the numerator noncentral, is the noncentral F (Definition 4.1.2). A single projection P onto a subspace of C(X) satisfies P(I−M)=0, and the same argument applies to the pair.

The residual part is the familiar Theorem 7.5.1; the rest applies the same argument to the other pieces.

9.6.2. The logic of the F ratio

By Eq. 9.6.1 every mean square estimates σ2 plus 𝛍‖Piμ‖2/ri, the squared size per dimension of the mean’s component in Vi, while under a correct model the residual mean square estimates σ2 alone. So the ratio Fi=MSis2 compares two estimates of σ2 that agree in expectation exactly when 𝛍Piμ=0. This is the logic of the analysis of variance: choose a denominator whose expectation equals that of the numerator when the hypothesis holds. Independence gives the distribution exactly, and the noncentrality 𝛍γi=‖Piμ‖2/σ2 governs the power. The probability that Fi exceeds any fixed critical value increases strictly with γi (Theorem 4.1.4). The mean of the ratio follows from independence and E(1/V)=1/(ν−2) for V∼χ2(ν), ν>2:

E(Fi)=n−rn−r−2⋅ri+γiri.
(9.6.2)

The principle is more general than the choice of s2. With random effects the expected mean squares contain variance components, and the right denominator may be another mean square (Exercise (B1); Chapters 32 and 33, Proposition 32.1.1). Also, when the model is wrong, Theorem 9.6.1(b) shows that the denominator itself is inflated, by 𝛍‖(I−M)μ‖2/(n−r) (compare Example (Residual sum of squares under a wrong mean)). Omitted terms inflate the denominator of every F ratio, which pushes the ratios towards zero, and they can also shift the numerators, because the omitted part of 𝛍μ may have components in the Vi (Exercise (B2)).

Chapter 11 derives the F test from the likelihood ratio principle (Theorem 11.4.1) and studies its power (Theorem 11.5.1).

9.6.3. Expected mean squares in an unbalanced layout

In the two-way layout of Section 9.3, Eq. 9.6.1 makes the hypotheses of Theorem 9.3.2 concrete. For the sequential sum of squares of A entered first, 𝛍(PA−P0)μ has entry μ¯iw−μ¯w on each of the ni⋅ observations of row i, where μ¯w=∑ini⋅μ¯iw/n. So EMS(A∣μ)=σ2+1a−1∑ini⋅(μ¯iw−μ¯w)2. For the Type III sum of squares, the noncentrality is the sum of squares Eq. 9.2.1 evaluated at the true means, divided by σ2 (Exercise (B3)): 𝛍𝛍γ=(Λ⊤μc)⊤(Λ⊤N−1Λ)−1(Λ⊤μc)σ2, where 𝛍μc is the vector of ab cell means, N=diag(nij), and the columns of Λ express the a−1 contrasts of unweighted row means. In the first, the design enters through the weights nij/ni⋅, which also define the hypothesis. In the second, the hypothesis does not depend on the design, which enters only through the precision matrix N−1 of the cell means.

Example (Checking expected mean squares by simulation)

Take the design of Example (Party, education and political self-placement): n=944 respondents in the nine cells of party by education, with the observed counts. Set the true cell means equal to the observed cell means, and σ2=1.21. The script generates 40,000 data sets with normal errors and 40,000 with centred exponential errors of the same variance, which are strongly skewed. For each it computes the sequential decomposition (party, then education, then interaction), the residual, and the Type III sum of squares for party, and averages the mean squares:

The averages agree with the expectations under both error distributions, as parts (a) and (b) promise. The variances are another matter. Under skewed errors the variance of the residual mean square is 0.0124, 3.97 times the normal-theory value, because the variance of a quadratic form involves the fourth moment of the errors (Theorem 2.3.3). And the correlation between the interaction and residual mean squares, 0.008 under normal errors (zero in theory), is 0.020 under skewed errors, against a theoretical 0.025 from the third and fourth moments: the two mean squares are no longer independent.

For the interaction, the noncentrality γ=29.60 is large. By Eq. 9.6.2, the F ratio has mean 8.417, and the simulated mean is 8.427. Its 5% critical value is 2.381, and the power is 0.9967 in theory and 0.9967 in the simulation. Figure 9.6.1 shows the simulated distribution with the noncentral F density.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
d = sm.datasets.anes96.load_pandas().data
party = pd.cut(d["PID"], [-1, 1.5, 4.5, 6], labels=["Dem", "Ind", "Rep"])
educ = pd.cut(d["educ"], [0, 3.5, 5.5, 7], labels=["HS", "College", "Graduate"])
cell = party.cat.codes.to_numpy() * 3 + educ.cat.codes.to_numpy()
n = len(cell)

def basis(*blocks):
    Z = np.column_stack(blocks)
    U, s, _ = np.linalg.svd(Z, full_matrices=False)
    return U[:, s > 1e-10 * s[0]]

one = np.ones((n, 1))
A = np.eye(3)[party.cat.codes.to_numpy()]
B = np.eye(3)[educ.cat.codes.to_numpy()]
Cell = np.eye(9)[cell]
U0, UA, UAB, UC = basis(one), basis(one, A), basis(one, A, B), basis(Cell)

def complement(U_big, U_small):
    """Orthonormal basis of C(U_small)-perp within C(U_big)."""
    R = U_big - U_small @ (U_small.T @ U_big)
    return basis(R)

pieces = {"mean": U0, "party": complement(UA, U0),
          "educ | party": complement(UAB, UA), "interaction": complement(UC, UAB)}
Ub = basis(one, B, np.column_stack([(A[:, [i]] - A[:, [0]]) * (B[:, [j]] - B[:, [0]])
                                     for i in (1, 2) for j in (1, 2)]))
pieces["party (Type III)"] = complement(UC, Ub)       # unweighted-means hypothesis

sigma = 1.1
mu = d.groupby(cell)["selfLR"].mean().to_numpy()[cell]  # true means: observed cell means
for name, U in pieces.items():
    r = U.shape[1]
    gamma = np.sum((U.T @ mu) ** 2) / sigma ** 2        # noncentrality ||P mu||^2 / sigma^2
    print(f"{name:17s} rank {r}  gamma = {gamma:9.2f}"
          f"  E(MS) = {sigma ** 2 * (1 + gamma / r):9.3f}")
rng = np.random.default_rng(99)
reps, chunk = 40_000, 4_000
dfe = n - 9
sums = {"normal": [], "skewed": []}
for kind in sums:
    out = []
    for _ in range(reps // chunk):
        if kind == "normal":
            E = sigma * rng.normal(size=(n, chunk))
        else:                                             # centred exponential, variance sigma^2
            E = sigma * (rng.exponential(size=(n, chunk)) - 1.0)
        Y = mu[:, None] + E
        ms = {name: np.sum((U.T @ Y) ** 2, axis=0) / U.shape[1] for name, U in pieces.items()}
        ms["residual"] = (np.sum(Y ** 2, axis=0) - np.sum((UC.T @ Y) ** 2, axis=0)) / dfe
        out.append(pd.DataFrame(ms))
    sums[kind] = pd.concat(out, ignore_index=True)
print(pd.DataFrame({k: v.mean() for k, v in sums.items()}).round(3))

The simulation never forms an n×n matrix: each sum of squares comes from the coordinates of Y in an orthonormal basis of its subspace (Theorem 9.1.1(d)).

9.6.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In simple regression with E(Y)=β01+β1x, show that the regression mean square has expectation σ2+β12Sxx, and that the F ratio of the corrected table has noncentrality β12Sxx/σ2 under normality.

Exercise (A2)

In a one-way layout with g groups, group sizes ni and means μi, show that the between-groups mean square has expectation σ2+∑ini(μi−μ¯)2/(g−1), where μ¯=∑iniμi/n. Compare with Example (The one-way layout).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In a balanced one-way layout with g groups of m observations, let Yij=μ+ai+εij, where the ai and εij are uncorrelated random variables with mean zero and variances σa2 and σ2. Show that Cov(Y)=σ2I+σa2ZZ⊤, with Z the matrix of group indicators, and use Theorem 2.3.1 to show that E(MSbetween)=σ2+mσa2,E(MSwithin)=σ2. Which ratio tests σa2=0, and what unbiased estimator of σa2 do the two mean squares suggest?

Solution. 𝛆Y=μ1+Za+ε, so E(Y)=μ1 and Cov(Y)=Z(σa2Ig)Z⊤+σ2I by Theorem 2.2.1. The between-groups projection is M−P1, where M=m−1ZZ⊤ averages within groups, and the within-groups projection is I−M. The mean μ1 is annihilated by both. By Theorem 2.3.1, E(Y⊤(M−P1)Y)=tr{(M−P1)(σ2I+σa2ZZ⊤)}. Now ZZ⊤=mM, and (M−P1)M=M−P1, so the trace is σ2(g−1)+σa2m(g−1). For the within-groups projection, (I−M)Z=0, so the trace is σ2(n−g). Dividing by the degrees of freedom gives the two expectations. The ratio MSbetween/MSwithin has expectations that agree iff σa2=0, and (MSbetween−MSwithin)/m is unbiased for σa2 (though it can be negative).

Exercise (B2)

Suppose 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ+zδ but the model 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ is fitted, with 1∈C(X). Show that 𝛃E(SSE)=σ2(n−r)+δ2‖(I−M)z‖2,E(SSR)=σ2(r−1)+‖(M−P1)(Xβ+zδ)‖2. Give an example in which 𝛃β has all slopes zero but the regression mean square is inflated.

Exercise (B3)

In the setting of Theorem 9.2.4 with d=0, show that the expected sum of squares of the hypothesis 𝛃Λ⊤β=0 is 𝛃𝛃σ2q+(Λ⊤β)⊤(Λ⊤GΛ)−1Λ⊤β, so the noncentrality is obtained by evaluating Eq. 9.2.1 at the true parameter.

Solution. By Theorem 9.2.4(b), the sum of squares is Y⊤KY with K=MT(Λ⊤GΛ)−1T⊤M, a projection of rank q, where T is the n×q matrix with Λ=X⊤T. By Theorem 9.6.1(a) its expectation is 𝛃𝛃σ2q+β⊤X⊤KXβ. Since 𝛃𝛃𝛃T⊤MXβ=T⊤Xβ=Λ⊤β, the second term is 𝛃𝛃(Λ⊤β)⊤(Λ⊤GΛ)−1Λ⊤β.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let the errors be independent with mean zero, variance σ2 and fourth moment μ4=E(εi4), and let 𝛍μ∈C(X). Use Theorem 2.3.3 to show that Var(SSE)=(μ4−3σ4)∑i(1−hii)2+2σ4(n−r), where hii are the leverages. For the centred exponential errors of Example (Checking expected mean squares by simulation), μ4=9σ4. Show that Var(s2)≈8σ4/(n−r) when all leverages are small, and compare with the simulated variance of the residual mean square.

Solution. With A=I−M and 𝛉𝛍θ=μ, the terms of Theorem 2.3.3 involving 𝛉θ vanish because 𝛍(I−M)μ=0. The diagonal of I−M is 1−hii, and tr{(I−M)2}=n−r, which gives the formula. If the leverages are small, ∑i(1−hii)2≈n−r (it equals n−2r+∑ihii2), so Var(SSE)≈(6+2)σ4(n−r) and Var(s2)≈8σ4/(n−r), four times the normal value 2σ4/(n−r). This is the factor seen in the simulation.

For the Type I sum of squares of A entered first in a two-way layout, show that its noncentrality can be positive when μij=βj depends on B only, and find the condition on the counts nij under which it is zero for all such 𝛍μ. Relate your answer to Theorem 9.4.3.

9.1  Decomposing the total sum of squares

Every sum of squares in a linear model is the squared length of a projection of the data. Chapter 6 showed this for the residual sum of squares, ‖(I−M)y‖2, and for the drop in residual sum of squares between nested models, ‖(M−M0)y‖2 (Eq. 6.5.2). This section turns these observations into a general framework. An analysis of variance is a choice of mutually orthogonal subspaces that together fill the model space. Once that choice is made, the whole table follows from Pythagoras: the sums of squares add up because the pieces are orthogonal, and the degrees of freedom add up because dimensions of orthogonal subspaces add.

Throughout the chapter the model is 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ with Cov(Y)=σ2I (Definition 5.3.1). X is n×p of rank r, which may be less than p, and M is the orthogonal projection onto C(X). The identities of the chapter hold for every data vector y. Probability enters only where a covariance, an expectation or a distribution is stated, and systematically in Section 9.6.

9.1.1. Orthogonal decompositions of the model space

Definition 9.1.1 (Analysis of variance decomposition)

An analysis of variance decomposition of the model space is a list of subspaces V1,…,Vk of C(X) that are mutually orthogonal and whose sum is C(X): C(X)=V1⊕V2⊕⋯⊕Vk,Vi⊥Vj (i≠j). The subspace Vk+1=C(X)⊥ is the error space. Writing Pi for the orthogonal projection onto Vi, the sum of squares for Vi is ‖Piy‖2=y⊤Piy, its degrees of freedom are ri=dim⁡Vi, and its mean square is ‖Piy‖2/ri.

The same space can be decomposed in many ways, and the choice is the statistician’s: the constants, a regressor of interest, a block of nuisance regressors, the linear and quadratic parts of a trend. The theorem collects what is true of all decompositions.

Theorem 9.1.1 (The analysis of variance identity)

Let V1,…,Vk be an analysis of variance decomposition of C(X), with projections P1,…,Pk and dimensions r1,…,rk. Then:

  • (a) PiPj=0 for i≠j, P1+⋯+Pk=M, and I=P1+P2+⋯+Pk+(I−M), a sum of k+1 mutually orthogonal projections;

  • (b) for every y∈Rn the vectors P1y,…,Pky,(I−M)y are mutually orthogonal and

    ‖y‖2=‖P1y‖2+⋯+‖Pky‖2+‖(I−M)y‖2;
    (9.1.1)

  • (c) ri=rank(Pi)=tr(Pi), r1+⋯+rk=r, and the error space has dimension tr(I−M)=n−r;

  • (d) if the columns of Qi (n×ri) form an orthonormal basis of Vi, then ‖Piy‖2=‖Qi⊤y‖2 is a sum of ri squares, and together with an orthonormal basis Qk+1 of the error space the matrix U=[Q1,…,Qk+1] is orthogonal;

  • (e) the subspaces Wj=V1⊕⋯⊕Vj form a chain W1⊆⋯⊆Wk=C(X) with projections Nj=P1+⋯+Pj, and Pj=Nj−Nj−1 (with N0=0). Conversely, every chain {0}=W0⊆W1⊆⋯⊆Wk=C(X) gives an analysis of variance decomposition with Vj=Wj−1⊥∩Wj.

Proof.

  • (a) For any u, Pju∈Vj⊆Vi⊥, so PiPju=0 by Proposition 6.3.4(c). Applying Theorem 6.3.8 repeatedly, the sum P1+⋯+Pk is the orthogonal projection onto V1⊕⋯⊕Vk=C(X), and a projection is determined by its range, so the sum is M. Each Pi satisfies MPi=Pi, because its columns lie in C(X), and therefore (I−M)Pi=0. The projection I−M onto C(X)⊥ (Theorem 6.3.3) is thus orthogonal to every Pi.

  • (b) Multiply I=∑iPi+(I−M) by y. For two different pieces, (Piy)⊤(Pjy)=y⊤PiPjy=0, using symmetry and (a). Pythagoras gives the identity.

  • (c) Rank equals trace for projections (Proposition 6.3.4(b)), and the trace is additive, so ∑iri=tr(M)=r by Theorem 6.3.7, and tr(I−M)=n−r.

  • (d) By Proposition 6.3.2, Pi=QiQi⊤, so y⊤Piy=‖Qi⊤y‖2. Columns taken from different Qi are orthogonal because the subspaces are, so U has orthonormal columns. It has r1+⋯+rk+(n−r)=n of them, so it is square and hence orthogonal.

  • (e) The Nj are projections onto Wj by Theorem 6.3.8, and the chain is nested by construction. Conversely, given a chain, Theorem 6.5.1(b) shows that Nj−Nj−1 is the projection onto Vj=Wj−1⊥∩Wj. For i<j we have Vi⊆Wi⊆Wj−1, which is orthogonal to Vj. The projections telescope to Nk=M, so by Theorem 6.3.8 the orthogonal sum of the Vj is C(M)=C(X).

Part (e) says that decompositions and chains are the same thing. Listing the rows of an analysis of variance table from the top is the same as fitting a sequence of ever larger models and recording what each step adds. Section 9.3 builds on this.

9.1.2. Degrees of freedom are dimensions

“Degrees of freedom” can seem like a rule of thumb (“subtract one for every parameter estimated”). Part (d) of Theorem 9.1.1 gives the phrase an exact meaning. Rotate the data into the orthonormal coordinates z=U⊤y. The rotation preserves length, and it splits z into consecutive blocks Q1⊤y,…,Qk+1⊤y of sizes r1,…,rk,n−r. The sum of squares for Vi is the sum of the squares of the ri coordinates in its block. A sum of squares has ri degrees of freedom because it is a sum of ri squares of orthonormal linear combinations of the data, and it cannot be written with fewer.

The rotation also explains why degrees of freedom govern the size of a sum of squares. If Cov(Y)=σ2I, the coordinates U⊤Y are uncorrelated with variance σ2 (Theorem 2.2.1), so a block of ri of them has expected sum of squares riσ2 plus the squared length of its mean (Section 9.6). The divisor n−1 of the sample variance is the case Rn=span(1)⊕1⊥: the n deviations yi−y¯ lie in an (n−1)-dimensional subspace, and the Helmert matrix of Example (Helmert matrices) writes their sum of squares as n−1 squared orthonormal contrasts.

9.1.3. The regression analysis of variance table

The most common decomposition comes from the chain span(1)⊆C(X), available whenever 1∈C(X), which is the case for every model with an intercept. Write P1=n−111⊤ for the projection onto the constant vectors (Example (Projections onto a line)). The chain gives the three-way decomposition of Eq. 6.5.1, I=P1+(M−P1)+(I−M), with ranks 1, r−1 and n−r. The squared lengths are ‖P1y‖2=ny¯2,SSR=‖(M−P1)y‖2=∑i(y^i−y¯)2,SSE=‖(I−M)y‖2=∑i(yi−y^i)2. They are usually arranged as follows.

Source df Sum of squares Mean square
Mean 1 ny¯2 ny¯2
Regression (after the mean) r−1 SSR SSR/(r−1)
Residual n−r SSE s2=SSE/(n−r)
Total n y⊤y

This is the uncorrected table. Its total is the raw sum of squares ∑iyi2, with n degrees of freedom. The row for the mean is rarely of interest, because it records only that the average response is not zero. Deleting it gives the corrected table, whose total is SST=∑i(yi−y¯)2=‖(I−P1)y‖2 on n−1 degrees of freedom. The corrected identity SST=SSR+SSE is Eq. 6.7.1. “Correcting for the mean” is thus a projection. We first project the data onto 1⊥ and then decompose what is left.

The ratio of the two mean squares, F=SSR/(r−1)SSE/(n−r), compares the variation along the regression directions with the variation in the error space. Under normality and when no regressor matters, it has the F(r−1,n−r) distribution (Theorem 4.4.6). Section 9.6 explains why this ratio is the right comparison, and Chapter 11 develops the test.

A few formulas make the table easy to compute from any least squares solution.

Proposition 9.1.2 (Computing the sums of squares)

Let 𝛃β^ be any least squares estimate. Then:

  • (a) 𝛃‖My‖2=β^⊤X⊤y and 𝛃SSE=y⊤y−β^⊤X⊤y;

  • (b) if 1∈C(X), then 𝛃SSR=β^⊤X⊤y−ny¯2;

  • (c) if X=[1,X1] has full column rank and X~1=(I−P1)X1 is the matrix of centred regressors, then 𝛃𝛃𝛃SSR=β^1⊤X~1⊤X~1β^1=β^1⊤X~1⊤y, where 𝛃β^1 holds the slopes.

Proof.

  • (a) 𝛃Xβ^=My (Theorem 6.4.1), so 𝛃𝛃‖My‖2=y⊤My=y⊤Xβ^=β^⊤X⊤y. The second formula follows from SSE=y⊤y−y⊤My (Proposition 6.4.3). (b) M−P1 is a projection (Theorem 6.5.1), so SSR=y⊤My−y⊤P1y=‖My‖2−ny¯2; now use (a). (c) By Lemma 6.6.1 with X2=1, M−P1 is the projection onto C(X~1), and by Theorem 6.6.2 the slopes are the coefficients of (M−P1)y in terms of the columns of X~1. So 𝛃(M−P1)y=X~1β^1 and 𝛃SSR=‖X~1β^1‖2. The last equality uses 𝛃X~1⊤y=X~1⊤(M−P1)y=X~1⊤X~1β^1.

In (a), 𝛃β^⊤X⊤y is the same for every solution of the normal equations, even though 𝛃β^ is not unique when r<p. That is no accident. It equals ‖My‖2, which depends only on the model space.

Example (An analysis of variance table for murder rates)

Return to the regression of the 2009 murder rate of the 50 US states on poverty, single parenthood and urbanization, with an intercept (Example (Poverty and murder rates)). Here r=4 and y¯=4.514. The listing computes every sum of squares from the coordinates of y in an orthonormal basis of C(X), as in Theorem 9.1.1(d): the first coordinate gives the mean, the next three the regression.

Source df Sum of squares Mean square
Mean 1 1018.81 1018.81
Regression 3 182.80 60.93
Residual 46 101.96 2.217
Total (uncorrected) 50 1303.57
Total (corrected) 49 284.76

The regression mean square is 27.49 times the residual mean square. If none of the three regressors were related to the murder rate and the errors were normal, a ratio this large would occur with probability about 2.5×10−10. The proportion of the corrected total that the regression accounts for is 182.80/284.76=0.6419, the R2 of Theorem 6.7.2.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats
data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data.drop(index="District of Columbia")

y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), data["poverty"], data["single"], data["urban"]])
r = np.linalg.matrix_rank(X)

Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(X)                 # orthonormal basis of C(X); first column spans 1
z = Q.T @ y                            # coordinates of My in that basis
ss_mean = z[0] ** 2                    # n * ybar^2
ss_reg = np.sum(z[1:] ** 2)            # ||(M - P1) y||^2, r - 1 squared coordinates
ss_res = y @ y - np.sum(z ** 2)        # ||(I - M) y||^2

rows = [("mean", 1, ss_mean), ("regression", r - 1, ss_reg),
        ("residual", n - r, ss_res), ("total", n, y @ y)]
for name, df, ss in rows:
    print(f"{name:11s} df = {df:3d}   SS = {ss:9.2f}   MS = {ss / df:8.3f}")
F = (ss_reg / (r - 1)) / (ss_res / (n - r))
print(f"corrected total = {ss_reg + ss_res:.2f},  F = {F:.2f}")

The QR factorization supplies the orthonormal basis of Theorem 9.1.1(d); its first column is ±1/n because Gram–Schmidt starts with the intercept (Proposition 6.2.2). No n×n projection matrix is formed.

9.1.4. Models without an intercept

The corrected table depends on 1∈C(X). Without it, the chain span(1)⊆C(X) is not available, and the corrected “identity” fails.

Proposition 9.1.3

For any model matrix X, with Y^=My and 𝛆ε^=(I−M)y, ∑i(yi−y¯)2=∑i(y^i−y¯)2+∑iε^i2−2y¯∑iε^i. The last term vanishes for every y iff 1∈C(X).

Proof. Write 𝛆y−y¯1=(Y^−y¯1)+ε^ and expand the squared length. The cross term is 𝛆𝛆𝛆𝛆2(Y^−y¯1)⊤ε^=2Y^⊤ε^−2y¯1⊤ε^=−2y¯1⊤ε^, since 𝛆Y^⊥ε^. If 1∈C(X), then 𝛆1⊤ε^=0 (Proposition 6.4.3(b)). If 1∉C(X), take y=1. Then y¯=1 and 𝛆1⊤ε^=1⊤(I−M)1=‖(I−M)1‖2>0, so the last term is not zero.

Without an intercept the valid decomposition is the uncorrected one, ‖y‖2=‖My‖2+SSE, whose regression row has r degrees of freedom. That is appropriate only when the mean response is known to vanish when every regressor is zero.

Example (Forcing the murder regression through the origin)

Refit the model of Example (An analysis of variance table for murder rates) without the intercept. The residual sum of squares rises from 101.96 to 177.73, so the fit is much worse. Yet the residuals now sum to −8.332, and the would-be corrected identity is off by −2y¯∑iε^i=75.22: the corrected total 284.76 is not 31.81+177.73=209.54. The uncentred ratio ‖Y^‖2/‖y‖2, which some software reports as R2 for such models, is 0.8637, much larger than the 0.6419 of the better model with an intercept (Exercise (B1)).

X0 = X[:, 1:]                                        # drop the intercept column
b0, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X0, y, rcond=None)
e0 = y - X0 @ b0
ybar = y.mean()
sst = np.sum((y - ybar) ** 2)
ssr_star = np.sum((X0 @ b0 - ybar) ** 2)
sse0 = e0 @ e0
print(f"SST = {sst:.2f}  but  SSR* + SSE = {ssr_star + sse0:.2f}")
print(f"sum of residuals = {e0.sum():.3f},  -2 ybar * sum = {-2 * ybar * e0.sum():.2f}")

9.1.5. Coarsening and refining

Coarsening merges rows: replacing V1 and V2 by V1⊕V2 gives another decomposition, whose sums of squares and degrees of freedom are sums of the old ones. The regression row is a coarsening of the table that gives each regressor its own row (Section 9.3). Refining splits a row: any subspace of dimension ri can be cut into ri orthogonal lines, each carrying one degree of freedom. A refinement is useful only when its lines answer questions of interest. Section 9.4 chooses them with orthogonal contrasts and orthogonal polynomials.

Idea. An analysis of variance table is a list of mutually orthogonal subspaces that fill Rn. Its sums of squares are the squared lengths of the projections of y, its degrees of freedom are the dimensions, and both columns add up because of Pythagoras.

9.1.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For simple regression, X=[1,x] with x not constant, show that SSR=β^12Sxx=Sxy2/Sxx and that the F ratio of the corrected table equals t2, where t=β^1Sxx/s.

Exercise (A2)

A regression with an intercept has a corrected table in which the regression row has 3 degrees of freedom and sum of squares 120, the residual mean square is 2.5, and the corrected total sum of squares is 172.5. Find n, the residual degrees of freedom, the F ratio and R2.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let U=[Q1,…,Qk+1] be the orthogonal matrix of Theorem 9.1.1(d) and z=U⊤Y. Suppose 𝛍E(Y)=μ∈C(X) and Cov(Y)=σ2I. Show that the coordinates of z are uncorrelated with variance σ2, that the last n−r of them have mean zero, and deduce E(SSE)=(n−r)σ2. Show that if in addition Y is normal, the coordinates are independent.

Solution. By Theorem 2.2.1, Cov(z)=U⊤(σ2I)U=σ2I and 𝛍E(z)=U⊤μ. The last block is 𝛍Qk+1⊤μ, and 𝛍μ∈C(X) is orthogonal to the error space, so it is zero. Then SSE=‖Qk+1⊤Y‖2 is a sum of n−r squares of variables with mean zero and variance σ2, so its expectation is (n−r)σ2. Under normality z is normal (Theorem 3.3.1) with diagonal covariance, and uncorrelated jointly normal variables are independent (Theorem 3.4.1).

Exercise (B2)

For a model without an intercept, the quantity 1−SSE/SST is sometimes reported as R2. Show that it is negative iff the model fits worse than the intercept-only model. Construct a data set with n=4 and one regressor for which it is below −1.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let V be a subspace with projection P. Show that ‖Py‖2=maxv∈V, v≠0(v⊤y)2v⊤v, with the maximum attained at v=Py when Py≠0. Interpret: the sum of squares of a row with ri degrees of freedom is the largest one-degree-of-freedom sum of squares obtainable by refining that row. Chapter 13 uses this fact to build Scheffé’s simultaneous intervals.

Solution. For v∈V, v⊤y=v⊤Py, so by Cauchy–Schwarz (Proposition 1.1.1) (v⊤y)2≤v⊤v‖Py‖2, with equality iff v is a multiple of Py. Taking v=Py∈V attains the bound. A line span(v) inside V carries the one-degree-of-freedom sum of squares (v⊤y)2/v⊤v (Example (Projections onto a line)), so no refinement of the row gives a line with a larger sum of squares than the whole row.

9.5  The coefficient of determination and its limits

The coefficient of determination is the most widely reported summary of a regression, and probably the most widely misread. It is a ratio of two rows of the corrected table, R2=SSRSST=1−SSESST, defined when 1∈C(X) and y is not constant. Section 6.7 showed that it is a squared cosine (Theorem 6.7.2) that cannot decrease as the model space grows. This section adds R2 as a maximal correlation, its null distribution, the adjusted and partial versions, and what it does not measure.

9.5.1. A maximal correlation

The square root R=R2 is called the multiple correlation coefficient. The name comes from the following property, the sample counterpart of the population result Proposition 2.5.3.

Theorem 9.5.1 (R is the largest correlation with a linear combination)

Suppose 1∈C(X) and y is not constant. For every b such that Xb is not constant, corr(y,Xb)2≤R2. If R2>0, equality holds iff Xb=aY^+c1 for some a≠0 and some c, and the correlation corr(y,Xb) is maximized, with value R, by the fitted values themselves. If R2=0, every such correlation is zero.

Proof. Let u=y−y¯1 and V=C(X)∩1⊥, whose projection is M−P1 (Theorem 6.5.1), and let v=Xb−Xb―1. Since 1∈C(X), v∈V, so u⊤v=u⊤(M−P1)v=((M−P1)u)⊤v. The sample correlation is u⊤v/(‖u‖‖v‖), and by Cauchy–Schwarz (Proposition 1.1.1) corr(y,Xb)2=(((M−P1)u)⊤v)2‖u‖2‖v‖2≤‖(M−P1)u‖2‖u‖2=SSRSST, with equality iff v and (M−P1)u=Y^−y¯1 are linearly dependent. If R2>0 the second vector is nonzero, and since v≠0 dependence means v=a(Y^−y¯1) with a≠0, which is the stated condition; for a>0 the correlation itself is +R. If R2=0, then (M−P1)u=0 and u⊤v=0.

Among all linear combinations of the regressors, the fitted values are the one most correlated with the response. When the regressors are pairwise uncorrelated, R2 is the sum of the squared simple correlations (Exercise (A3)). In general it can be smaller or larger than that sum (Exercise (C2)).

9.5.2. R2 when nothing is going on

Since R2 never decreases as regressors are added, a model with many regressors has a sizeable R2 even if none of them matters. The theory of Chapter 4 says how sizeable.

Theorem 9.5.2 (The distribution of R2)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,σ2I) with 𝛍μ∈C(X), 1∈C(X) and 1<r=rank(X)<n. Let F=[SSR/(r−1)]/[SSE/(n−r)] and 𝛍γ=‖(M−P1)μ‖2/σ2. Then:

  • (a) R2=(r−1)F(r−1)F+n−r, and F∼F(r−1,n−r,γ);

  • (b) if 𝛍μ∈span(1), so that no regressor matters, then R2∼Beta(r−12,n−r2) and E(R2)=r−1n−1.

Proof.

  • (a) R2/(1−R2)=SSR/SSE=(r−1)F/(n−r), and solving for R2 gives the formula. The projections M−P1 and I−M are orthogonal with ranks r−1 and n−r, and 𝛍(I−M)μ=0, so by Theorem 4.4.4 SSR/σ2∼χ2(r−1,γ) and SSE/σ2∼χ2(n−r), independently. The ratio F is therefore F(r−1,n−r,γ) (Definition 4.1.2).

  • (b) Now γ=0, and R2=U/(U+V) with U=SSR/σ2∼χ2(r−1) and V=SSE/σ2∼χ2(n−r) independent. By Exercise (B4) this is Beta(r−12,n−r2), whose mean is r−12/n−12=(r−1)/(n−1).

Part (b) answers the exercise Exercise (C1) of Chapter 6. With k=r−1 regressors of pure noise, R2 is on average k/(n−1). A model with 10 useless regressors fitted to 30 observations has expected R2 about one third. Figure 9.5.1 compares the Beta densities with 100,000 simulated data sets with n=30 and k=1,5,10 regressors drawn at random. The simulated means are 0.0345, 0.1725 and 0.3445, against the exact 0.0345, 0.1724 and 0.3448. The upper 5% points of the three null distributions are 0.130, 0.353 and 0.556, so with ten regressors an R2 of one half is unremarkable. Part (a) shows that R2 and F carry the same information: testing R2 against its null distribution is the overall F test of the regression.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(20260919)
n, reps = 30, 100_000
results = {}
for k in (1, 5, 10):                                   # number of regressors besides 1
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), rng.normal(size=(n, k))])
    r = k + 1
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(X)
    Y = 3.0 + rng.normal(size=(n, reps))               # no regressor matters
    Z = Q.T @ Y
    ssr = np.sum(Z[1:] ** 2, axis=0)
    sst = np.sum(Y ** 2, axis=0) - Z[0] ** 2
    R2 = ssr / sst
    R2adj = 1 - (1 - R2) * (n - 1) / (n - r)
    print(f"k = {k:2d}: mean R^2 = {R2.mean():.4f} (theory {k / (n - 1):.4f}),"
          f" mean adjusted R^2 = {R2adj.mean():+.4f}")
    results[k] = R2

9.5.3. Adjusted R2

The bias of R2 under the null suggests a correction. Write s2=SSE/(n−r) and sy2=SST/(n−1) for the unbiased estimators of the error variance in the fitted model and in the intercept-only model. Replacing sums of squares by these mean squares defines the adjusted coefficient of determination

R¯2=1−s2sy2=1−(1−R2)n−1n−r.
(9.5.1)
Proposition 9.5.3 (Properties of the adjusted R2)

Let 1∈C(X), 1≤r=rank(X)<n, and let F be the overall F ratio of Theorem 9.5.2.

  • (a) R¯2≤R2, with equality iff r=1 or R2=1.

  • (b) For r>1, R¯2<0 iff F<1.

  • (c) If a model with rank r0 is enlarged to one of rank r=r0+q containing it, then R¯2 increases iff the F ratio of the added terms, [SS(added∣old)/q]/s2, exceeds 1. For a single added column this means |t|>1.

  • (d) Among models for the same response, R¯2 is largest for the model with the smallest residual mean square s2.

  • (e) Under the normal model with 𝛍μ∈span(1), E(R¯2)=0.

Proof.

  • (a) 1−R¯2=(1−R2)(n−1)/(n−r), and (n−1)/(n−r)≥1 with equality iff r=1, so 1−R¯2≥1−R2, with equality iff r=1 or 1−R2=0.

  • (b) R¯2<0 iff s2>sy2, that is, SSE(n−1)>(SSR+SSE)(n−r), that is, SSE(r−1)>SSR(n−r), which for r>1 is F<1.

  • (c) sy2 does not depend on the model, so R¯2 increases iff s2 decreases. Write SSE0=SSE+D with D the extra sum of squares. Then SSE/(n−r)<(SSE+D)/(n−r0) iff SSE(n−r0)<(SSE+D)(n−r), iff qSSE<D(n−r), iff (D/q)/s2>1. For one column the ratio is t2 (Corollary 9.2.3).

  • (d) is immediate from Eq. 9.5.1.

  • (e) By Theorem 9.5.2(b), E(1−R2)=1−(r−1)/(n−1)=(n−r)/(n−1), so E(1−R¯2)=1.

Part (e) is the point of the adjustment. In the simulation the averages of R¯2 are 0.0000, 0.0001 and −0.0005, but R¯2 is negative in a proportion 0.674 of the data sets with one regressor: it is not a proportion of anything. Part (c) shows what selection by R¯2 amounts to: a regressor is admitted when |t|>1, far below the threshold near two of a 5% test, so the chosen models tend to be too large (Proposition 29.2.3).

Example (Adjusted R2 for the state data)

For all seven models built from poverty, single parenthood and urbanization (each with an intercept):

Regressors R2 R¯2
poverty 0.3599 0.3466
single 0.5719 0.5629
urban 0.0221 0.0017
poverty, single 0.6410 0.6257
poverty, urban 0.4626 0.4398
single, urban 0.5844 0.5668
all three 0.6419 0.6186

R2 is largest for the full model, as it must be. R¯2 prefers poverty and single parenthood without urbanization. Adding urbanization to that model raises R2 slightly but lowers R¯2, because its t statistic in the full model is only 0.349, so its F ratio is 0.121<1.

9.5.4. Partial R2

Applied to what a reduced model leaves unexplained, the idea of R2 gives a measure for a term.

Definition 9.5.1 (Partial coefficient of determination)

Let C(X1)⊆C(X) with 1∈C(X1). The partial coefficient of determination of X over X1 is RX∣X12=SSE(X1)−SSE(X)SSE(X1)=SS(X∣X1)SSE(X1), the proportion of the residual variation of the reduced model that the larger model explains.

Theorem 9.5.4 (Partial R2, F and t)

With the notation of Definition 9.5.1, let r1=rank(X1), r=rank(X), q=r−r1>0, s2=SSE(X)/(n−r), and let F=[SS(X∣X1)/q]/s2. Then:

  • (a) 1−R2=(1−R12)(1−RX∣X12), where R2 and R12 belong to the two models;

  • (b) RX∣X12=qFqF+n−r;

  • (c) if X=[X1,xj] adds one column, then RX∣X12=tj2tj2+n−r, and it equals the squared sample correlation between the residuals (I−M1)y and (I−M1)xj;

  • (d) for a chain C(1)=C(X0)⊆C(X1)⊆⋯⊆C(Xk), 1−Rk2=∏j=1k(1−RXj∣Xj−12).

Proof.

  • (a) 1−R2=SSE(X)/SST=(SSE(X1)/SST)(SSE(X)/SSE(X1)), and the two factors are 1−R12 and 1−RX∣X12.

  • (b) Write D=SS(X∣X1)=qFs2 and SSE(X)=(n−r)s2. Then RX∣X12=D/(D+SSE(X))=qF/(qF+n−r).

  • (c) With q=1, F=tj2 by Corollary 9.2.3, and (b) gives the first formula. For the correlation, let y~=(I−M1)y and x~=(I−M1)xj. Then SSE(X1)=‖y~‖2, and by Lemma 9.2.1 SS(X∣X1)=(x~⊤y~)2/‖x~‖2. Their ratio is the squared cosine of the angle between y~ and x~. Both have mean zero because 1∈C(X1), so the cosine is their sample correlation (Exercise (B3)).

  • (d) Apply (a) repeatedly along the chain, with R02=0 for the intercept-only model.

Part (c) identifies the partial R2 of one regressor with a squared sample partial correlation, the sample counterpart of Definition 3.6.1; Chapter 14 studies its sampling distribution. Within one model, ranking regressors by |t| and by partial R2 is the same thing.

Example (Partial R2 in the state data)

In the three-regressor model, with n−r=46:

Regressor t partial R2 R2 without it
poverty 2.718 0.1383 0.5844
single 4.799 0.3337 0.4626
urban 0.349 0.0026 0.6410

Each partial R2 equals t2/(t2+46), and each row satisfies part (a): for poverty, 1−0.6419=(1−0.5844)(1−0.1383). Poverty explains 14% of the variation that single parenthood and urbanization leave unexplained, although on its own it explains 36% of the total (R2=0.3599). For the pair poverty and urbanization over single parenthood, the F ratio on 2 and 46 degrees of freedom is 4.502 and the partial R2 is 0.1637.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data.drop(index="District of Columbia")

y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
names = ["poverty", "single", "urban"]
n = len(y)

def fit(cols):
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n)] + [data[c].to_numpy() for c in cols])
    return sm.OLS(y, X).fit()

full = fit(names)
df_res = full.df_resid
for j, name in enumerate(names, start=1):
    reduced = fit([c for c in names if c != name])
    partial_r2 = (reduced.ssr - full.ssr) / reduced.ssr
    t = full.tvalues[j]
    print(f"{name:8s} partial R^2 = {partial_r2:.4f},  t = {t:6.3f},"
          f"  t^2/(t^2 + df) = {t ** 2 / (t ** 2 + df_res):.4f}")
print(f"R^2 = {full.rsquared:.4f}, adjusted R^2 = {full.rsquared_adj:.4f}")

9.5.5. What R2 does not measure

A high R2 is often read as “a good model” and a low one as “a useless model”; neither reading is safe.

It is not a property of the relationship alone. R2 depends on the spread of the regressors, which is a property of the design. In simple regression with fixed x values, the expectations of the two sums of squares are (Exercise (B1)) E(SSR)=σ2+β12Sxx,E(SST)=(n−1)σ2+β12Sxx, so R2 is roughly β12Sxx/(β12Sxx+(n−1)σ2). In Figure 9.5.2 the line E(y)=2+0.5x and the same 50 errors with σ=1 are combined with x values spread evenly over [4,6] and over [0,10]. The two designs span the same C(X), so the residuals are identical and the residual mean square is 1.101 in both fits, but R2 is 0.026 in one case and 0.632 in the other. The approximate values from the formula are 0.081 and 0.689. Restricting the range of a regressor lowers R2 without changing the relationship.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
n, beta0, beta1, sigma = 50, 2.0, 0.5, 1.0
eps = sigma * rng.normal(size=n)                        # the same errors in both designs

def fit(x):
    y = beta0 + beta1 * x + eps
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])
    b, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
    sse = np.sum((y - X @ b) ** 2)
    r2 = 1 - sse / np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2)
    return y, b, sse / (n - 2), r2

for label, x in [("narrow", np.linspace(4, 6, n)), ("wide", np.linspace(0, 10, n))]:
    y, b, s2, r2 = fit(x)
    Sxx = np.sum((x - x.mean()) ** 2)
    expected = beta1 ** 2 * Sxx / (beta1 ** 2 * Sxx + (n - 1) * sigma ** 2)
    print(f"{label:6s}: slope {b[1]:.3f}, s^2 {s2:.3f}, R^2 {r2:.3f}, design value {expected:.3f}")

It is not a test of the model. A line fitted to points on a smooth curve can have a high R2 while missing the curvature; residual plots and lack-of-fit tests (Theorem 14.6.2) reveal it. Conversely, a correct model has a low R2 whenever the error variance is large, and its coefficients can still be precisely estimated and important.

It is not comparable across responses. Models for y and log⁡y, or for individuals and for group averages, have different denominators SST. Averaging replicates inflates R2 (Exercise (B2)).

It is not comparable without an intercept (Proposition 9.1.3, Example (Forcing the murder regression through the origin)).

It is not a measure of prediction or of cause. Computed on the fitting data, it overstates predictive accuracy for new data (Chapter 29), and variation “explained” is linear association, not the effect of changing a regressor (Chapter 25).

Exercise (B1)

In simple regression with fixed x values, E(Y)=β01+β1x and Cov(Y)=σ2I, use Theorem 2.3.1 to show that E(SSR)=σ2+β12Sxx and E(SST)=(n−1)σ2+β12Sxx. Show that the ratio of these expectations tends to 1 as Sxx→∞ with n fixed, and to 1/(n−1) as Sxx→0, and interpret the second limit.

Solution. SSR=y⊤(M−P1)y, where M−P1 projects onto the line spanned by x−x¯1 (Example (Intercept inside simple regression)) and has trace 1. With 𝛍μ=β01+β1x, 𝛍(M−P1)μ=β1(x−x¯1), whose squared length is β12Sxx. Theorem 2.3.1 gives E(SSR)=σ2+β12Sxx. Similarly SST=y⊤(I−P1)y, with trace n−1 and 𝛍(I−P1)μ=β1(x−x¯1). The ratio (σ2+β12Sxx)/((n−1)σ2+β12Sxx) tends to 1 as Sxx→∞ and to 1/(n−1) as Sxx→0. The second limit is the null value of E(R2) for r=2 (Theorem 9.5.2): with almost no spread in x, the slope contributes almost nothing and the regression sum of squares is almost pure noise.

Exercise (B2)

Suppose each of g distinct values x1,…,xg carries m observations. Show that the least squares line fitted to all gm observations is the same as the line fitted to the g group means, and that its R2 for the individual data is at most its R2 for the means, with equality iff every observation equals its group mean.

Solution. The model space for the individual data is spanned by 1 and x, both constant within groups, so it lies inside the space of group-constant vectors. By Theorem 6.5.1, projecting y onto it equals projecting the vector of group means (repeated m times) onto it, which is the fit to the means with each point weighted equally. Write SSRg and SSTg for the sums over the g means, and W for the within-group sum of squares. For the individual data SSR=mSSRg and SST=mSSTg+W. Hence R2=mSSRg/(mSSTg+W)≤SSRg/SSTg, with equality iff W=0.

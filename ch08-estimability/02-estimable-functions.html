8.2  Estimable functions

Identifiability asks whether a quantity is determined by the distribution of the data. Estimation asks for more. We want a statistic whose distribution is centred on the quantity, however the parameter is set. For linear functions of β, the natural candidates are linear functions of Y, and the natural centring requirement is unbiasedness. This leads to the classical notion of an estimable function.

8.2.1. Linear unbiased estimators

Here and below the model is E(Y)=Xβ with X an n×p matrix of rank r; no assumption on Cov(Y) is needed until variances are computed.

Definition 8.2.1 (Estimable function)

A linear function λ⊤β, λ∈Rp, is estimable if there is a vector a∈Rn such that E(a⊤Y)=λ⊤βfor every β∈Rp. A vector Λ⊤β of linear functions, with Λ a p×q matrix, is estimable if each of its q components is. Equivalently, there is an n×q matrix A with E(A⊤Y)=Λ⊤β for all β.

The statistic a⊤Y is a linear unbiased estimator of λ⊤β. The requirement “for every β” is essential. For a single fixed β, almost any statistic can be made unbiased by adding a constant. What the definition demands is one statistic that is centred correctly whatever the true coefficients are. Chapter 7 asked which linear unbiased estimator is best (Definition 7.1.1). Here we ask only whether there is one.

One might allow an affine estimator a0+a⊤Y. This gains nothing, as Chapter 7 already showed.

Lemma 8.2.1

The statistic a0+a⊤Y is unbiased for λ⊤β, for every β, iff a0=0 and X⊤a=λ.

Proof. This is Proposition 7.1.1, proved in Chapter 7 by evaluating the expectation a0+a⊤Xβ at β=0 and at the coordinate vectors.

So the linear unbiased estimators of λ⊤β correspond exactly to the solutions a of the linear system X⊤a=λ. There is at least one iff the system is consistent, and when it is, the solutions form the affine subspace a0+N(X⊤) (Theorem 1.4.1).

8.2.2. Estimable is the same as identifiable

Theorem 8.2.2

For λ∈Rp the following are equivalent:

  • (a) λ⊤β is estimable;

  • (b) λ⊤β is identifiable;

  • (c) λ∈C(X⊤), that is, λ⊤=ρ⊤X for some ρ∈Rn.

For a p×q matrix Λ, Λ⊤β is estimable iff Λ=X⊤P for some n×q matrix P, that is, iff C(Λ)⊆C(X⊤).

Proof. (a)⇔(c) is Lemma 8.2.1: an unbiased a⊤Y exists iff X⊤a=λ has a solution, which is the statement λ∈C(X⊤). (b)⇔(c) is Proposition 8.1.4. The matrix version applies the vector version to each column of Λ and collects the vectors ρ as the columns of P.

The theorem connects two different ideas. Identifiability is about what the model could reveal with unlimited data, and it involves no estimator. Estimability is about a concrete property of a concrete statistic. For linear functions they are the same thing, and both reduce to membership of λ in the row space of X. The row space is spanned by the rows x(1)⊤,…,x(n)⊤, so the theorem says:

A linear function of β is estimable iff it is a linear combination of the expected responses E(yi)=x(i)⊤β.

This reading is the most useful one in practice. To show that λ⊤β is estimable, write it as a combination of the means of observations that were actually made. To show that it is not, find a direction v with Xv=0 and λ⊤v≠0.

Corollary 8.2.3 (The estimable space)

The set of λ for which λ⊤β is estimable is the subspace C(X⊤) of dimension r=rank(X). In particular:

  • (a) every expected response x(i)⊤β, and hence the whole mean vector Xβ, is estimable;

  • (b) every entry of X⊤Xβ is estimable;

  • (c) at most r estimable functions can be linearly independent, and a set of r linearly independent ones determines all the others as linear combinations;

  • (d) if r=p, every linear function of β is estimable.

Proof. The first sentence is Theorem 8.2.2. For (a) take ρ=ei, the ith coordinate vector. For (b), X⊤Xβ=X⊤(Xβ) and the rows of X⊤X lie in C(X⊤). Part (c) is the dimension count, and (d) is the case C(X⊤)=Rp.

8.2.3. The mean at a new point

A frequent use of a fitted model is to estimate the mean response at a setting x0 of the regressors, which may or may not have been observed. The quantity x0⊤β is a linear function of β, so the theorem applies. It is estimable iff x0∈C(X⊤). In a full-rank model this is automatic, and the only danger in predicting at a new point is extrapolation, meaning that the model may not hold there. In a rank-deficient model there is a sharper limit. Some settings are algebraically out of reach. The data contain no information about the mean there, even if the model is exactly true.

Example (A fertilizer trial with a fixed ratio)

Ten plots receive nitrogen at 0,20,40,60,80 kg/ha (two plots each), and phosphorus is always applied at half the nitrogen rate, as in a commercial blend. The model E(y)=β0+β1N+β2P is natural agronomically, but in this design xP=12xN, so v=(0,1,−2)⊤ spans N(X) and rank(X)=2. A linear function is estimable iff its coefficient vector satisfies λ1−2λ2=0. So:

  • β0, the mean yield without fertilizer, is estimable. Plots with N=P=0 were observed.
  • The mean yield at (N,P)=(40,20) is estimable, since 40−2⋅20=0, and so is the mean at any dose of the blend, observed or not, such as (50,25).
  • The mean yield at (40,0), nitrogen alone, is not estimable, since 40−0≠0.
  • Neither β1 nor β2 is estimable, but β1+12β2, the gain per kilogram of nitrogen when it comes with its phosphorus, is.

The listing computes three least squares solutions: the minimum-norm one, the one that omits P, and the one that omits N. They give intercepts 2.9579, 2.9579 and 2.9579, all equal as they must be. The pairs of slopes (0.0296,0.0148), (0.0370,0.0000) and (0.0000,0.0740) all disagree. At the blend dose (40,20) all three predict 4.439 t/ha. At the nitrogen-only dose (40,0) they predict 4.143, 4.439 and 2.958. The third value is just β^0, because that solution puts the whole fertilizer effect on phosphorus. Each of these is a legitimate least squares answer, and none of them estimates anything.

The two kinds of prediction differ in kind, not in degree. Nothing in this experiment says what nitrogen does without phosphorus. A larger experiment of the same design would not help. What is needed is at least one plot off the line P=N/2 (Exercise (B3)).

import numpy as np

N = np.array([0, 0, 20, 20, 40, 40, 60, 60, 80, 80], dtype=float)   # kg/ha nitrogen
P = N / 2                                                           # phosphorus, always half
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(N.size), N, P])
rng = np.random.default_rng(80)
y = 3.0 + 0.020 * N + 0.030 * P + rng.normal(scale=0.25, size=N.size)   # yield, t/ha
print("rank(X) =", np.linalg.matrix_rank(X), "  null vector (0, 1, -2):", X @ [0, 1, -2])
b_min = np.linalg.pinv(X) @ y                                  # minimum-norm solution
b_noP = np.append(np.linalg.lstsq(X[:, :2], y, rcond=None)[0], 0.0)     # drop P
b_noN = np.insert(np.linalg.lstsq(X[:, [0, 2]], y, rcond=None)[0], 1, 0.0)  # drop N
new_points = {"N=40, P=20 (on the line)": [1, 40, 20],
              "N=40, P=0  (off the line)": [1, 40, 0]}
for name, x0 in new_points.items():
    print(name, [round(float(np.dot(x0, b)), 3) for b in (b_min, b_noP, b_noN)])

8.2.4. Many unbiased estimators, one least squares estimator

An estimable function usually has many linear unbiased estimators. In Example (A fertilizer trial with a fixed ratio), the mean at the blend dose (40,20) is estimated without bias by the average of the two plots that received exactly that dose, a1⊤Y with a1 putting weight 12 on each. It is also estimated without bias by the value of any least squares fit at that dose. If Cov(Y)=σ2I, the first estimator has variance σ2‖a1‖2=0.500σ2, while the least squares estimator has variance 0.100σ2, five times smaller, because it uses all ten plots.

The least squares estimator can be written in the same form. If a is any solution of X⊤a=λ, then Theorem 6.4.4 gives

λ⊤β^=a⊤Xβ^=a⊤My=(Ma)⊤y,
(8.2.1)

for every least squares solution β^. So the least squares estimator is the linear unbiased estimator whose coefficient vector is the projection Ma of any one coefficient vector onto C(X). It is unbiased, because X⊤Ma=X⊤a=λ. Every solution of X⊤a=λ has the form Ma+w with w∈N(X⊤)=C(X)⊥, and Pythagoras gives ‖Ma+w‖2=‖Ma‖2+‖w‖2. This is the Gauss–Markov theorem in its simplest form: among linear unbiased estimators of an estimable function, least squares has the smallest variance when Cov(Y)=σ2I. Chapter 7 states and proves it in general (Theorem 7.2.1).

8.2.5. What estimability does not depend on

Three remarks keep the concept in its place.

It does not depend on the covariance. Estimability involves only E(Y)=Xβ, so it is the same whether the errors are uncorrelated, correlated, heteroscedastic or normal, provided Cov(Y) is free of β. Generalized least squares in Part VII has the same estimable functions as ordinary least squares.

It is a property of the design, not the response. Whether λ⊤β is estimable can be decided before any response is measured, from X alone. This makes estimability a planning tool. If a comparison matters, the design must contain the rows that make it estimable.

It is a statement about linear functions. A turning point −β1/(2β2) or a quotient of two estimable quantities is not “estimable” in the technical sense, and usually has no unbiased estimator of any kind. Yet when it is identifiable it is a perfectly sensible target, to be estimated by other means. The line that must not be crossed is identifiability, not linearity. In this chapter “nonestimable” is used only for linear functions, and for them it means the same as “not identifiable”.

Warning. Software fitted to a rank-deficient model reports a number for every coefficient and every requested combination, estimable or not. For a nonestimable function the number is an artefact of the particular solution the software chose, as the three predictions at (40,0) show. It comes with a standard error that is equally arbitrary (Section 8.3).

8.2.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In Example (A fertilizer trial with a fixed ratio), decide which of the following are estimable: β1−2β2; 2β1+β2; the mean yield at (N,P)=(100,50); the mean yield at (0,10); the difference between the mean yields at (60,30) and (20,10). For the estimable ones give a vector a with X⊤a=λ.

Exercise (A2)

In simple linear regression E(yi)=β0+β1xi, show that β1 is estimable iff the xi are not all equal. If all xi=c, which linear functions are estimable, and what is their least squares estimator?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let λ⊤β be estimable and let a∗=Ma as in Eq. 8.2.1. Show that a∗ does not depend on which solution a of X⊤a=λ is used, that a∗=XGλ for any generalized inverse G of X⊤X, and that ‖a∗‖2=λ⊤Gλ.

Solution. Two solutions differ by a vector of N(X⊤)=C(X)⊥, which M sends to 0, so Ma is the same for both. With M=XGX⊤ (Theorem 6.3.7), Ma=XGX⊤a=XGλ. Finally, ‖Ma‖2=a⊤Ma=a⊤XGX⊤a=λ⊤Gλ.

Exercise (B2)

Show that Λ⊤β is estimable iff Λ⊤b1=Λ⊤b2 whenever Xb1=Xb2, and that in that case Λ⊤β^=P⊤My for any P with X⊤P=Λ. Show also that P⊤M does not depend on the choice of P.

Exercise (B3)

A study will compare three treatments, A, B and C, in the additive model E(y)=μ+τtrt+δsite. Two sites are available, and each site can host two of the three treatments. Which allocations make every difference τs−τt estimable? Show that putting {A,B} at both sites leaves τA−τC nonestimable, while {A,B} at one site and {B,C} at the other makes all three differences estimable.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Suppose the distribution of ε does not depend on β. Show that if some statistic f(Y), linear or not, satisfies Ef(Y)=g(β) for every β, then g is identifiable. Conclude that no statistic whatever is unbiased for a nonestimable linear function.

Solution. The distribution of Y=Xβ+ε depends on β only through Xβ. If Xβ1=Xβ2, then Y has the same distribution under both, so g(β1)=Eβ1f(Y)=Eβ2f(Y)=g(β2). A linear function with an unbiased estimator is therefore identifiable, hence estimable by Theorem 8.2.2.

8.7  Contrasts

In the one-way model the individual effects αk are not estimable, but their differences are. The general form of such a difference is a contrast. Contrasts are the natural targets of inference about a factor. They are estimable, they do not depend on the side condition or coding, and they are what a scientific question about a factor usually asks: does treatment beat control, is there a trend across doses, do two groups of levels differ? This section characterizes them, shows how an orthogonal set of contrasts splits the factor’s sum of squares, and constructs the Helmert and polynomial families.

8.7.1. Definition and estimability

Throughout, the one-way model is E(ykj)=μ+αk for observation j at level k, with nk≥1 observations at level k=1,…,g, level means μk=μ+αk and sample means y¯k.

Definition 8.7.1 (Contrast)

In a factor model, a contrast in the effects of a factor is a linear function ∑kckαk whose coefficients satisfy ∑kck=0, not all zero. In the one-way model it equals ∑kckμk, and we call either form a contrast in the level means. Two contrasts are orthogonal (for the given design) if ∑kckdk/nk=0.

The two forms agree because ∑kckμk=μ∑kck+∑kckαk. This is where the condition ∑kck=0 comes from: it makes the unidentified μ drop out. Simple differences αk−αl, comparisons of one level with the average of others such as α1−12(α2+α3), and trend coefficients are all contrasts.

Theorem 8.7.1 (Estimable functions in the one-way model)

In the one-way model with every nk≥1, the function λ0μ+∑kλkαk is estimable iff λ0=∑kλk. In particular, a linear function of the effects alone, ∑kckαk, is estimable iff it is a contrast (or zero). For a contrast, the least squares estimator and its variance under Cov(Y)=σ2I are

ψ^=∑kcky¯k,Var(ψ^)=σ2∑kck2nk.
(8.7.1)

Proof. The null space of X=[1,Z] is spanned by v=(1,−1,…,−1)⊤ (Example (A rank-deficient layout)). By Theorem 8.3.1(c), the function is estimable iff λ0−∑kλk=0. For an estimable function, λ0μ+∑kλkαk=∑kλk(μ+αk). Every least squares solution has μ^+α^k=y¯k, since the fitted values are the level means (Example (The one-way layout)). So the estimate is ∑kλky¯k. The level means are uncorrelated with variances σ2/nk, by Theorem 2.2.1, which gives the variance.

The estimator in Eq. 8.7.1 is the same whatever coding or side condition was used to fit the model. Proposition 8.3.2 guarantees this, and it is the reason contrasts, rather than coefficients, should be reported.

8.7.2. Sums of squares and orthogonal contrasts

Each contrast defines a direction in the observation space, and its estimate is the component of y along that direction. With D=diag(n1,…,ng) and Z the indicator matrix, put

uc=ZD−1c,
(8.7.2)

the vector whose entries equal ck/nk for the observations at level k.

Proposition 8.7.2 (Contrasts as projections)

Let c and d be contrasts, and write 𝛍ψ^c=c⊤μ^. Then:

  • (a) ψ^c=uc⊤y, and uc is a nonzero vector in C(Z)∩1⊥;

  • (b) the sum of squares for the contrast, SS(c)=ψ^c2∑kck2/nk, equals ‖Py‖2, where P is the orthogonal projection onto the line spanned by uc;

  • (c) Cov(ψ^c,ψ^d)=σ2∑kckdk/nk=σ2uc⊤ud when Cov(Y)=σ2I, so orthogonal contrasts have uncorrelated estimates and orthogonal vectors u;

  • (d) if c1,…,cg−1 are pairwise orthogonal contrasts, then ∑j=1g−1SS(cj)=∑k=1gnk(y¯k−y¯)2, the sum of squares between levels.

Proof.

  • (a) 𝛍μ^=D−1Z⊤y (Eq. 8.5.1), so 𝛍c⊤μ^=(ZD−1c)⊤y. Clearly uc∈C(Z), and 1⊤uc=1⊤ZD−1c=(n1,…,ng)D−1c=∑kck=0. It is nonzero because ZD−1 has full column rank and c≠0.
  • (b) By Example (Projections onto a line), ‖Py‖2=(uc⊤y)2/‖uc‖2, and ‖uc‖2=c⊤D−1Z⊤ZD−1c=c⊤D−1c=∑kck2/nk.
  • (c) Theorem 2.2.1 gives Cov(uc⊤Y,ud⊤Y)=σ2uc⊤ud, and the same computation as in (b) gives uc⊤ud=c⊤D−1d.
  • (d) The space C(Z)∩1⊥ has dimension g−1 (Theorem 6.5.1(c), with C(1)⊆C(Z)). By (a) and (c) the vectors uc1,…,ucg−1 are g−1 nonzero, mutually orthogonal vectors in it, so they form an orthogonal basis. By Theorem 6.3.8 the sum of the projections onto the lines they span is the projection onto that space, which is M−n−111⊤ (Theorem 6.5.1(b)). Applying it to y and using Pythagoras gives ∑jSS(cj)=‖(M−n−111⊤)y‖2. The vector (M−n−111⊤)y has entry y¯k−y¯ at every observation of level k.

So a complete set of orthogonal contrasts decomposes the between-level sum of squares into g−1 one-dimensional pieces. Under normality the pieces are independent, each is σ2 times a noncentral χ2 with one degree of freedom, and each is independent of the residual sum of squares (Theorem 4.4.4). Each contrast can therefore be tested on its own. Chapter 9 places this decomposition in the general theory of orthogonal sums of squares, and Chapter 15 uses it for the one-way analysis of variance. In a balanced design, with all nk equal, orthogonality reduces to the ordinary ∑kckdk=0. In an unbalanced design, contrasts that look orthogonal on paper need not be orthogonal for the data.

8.7.3. Helmert contrasts

The Helmert matrix of Example (Helmert matrices) has rows proportional to hk=(1,…,1⏟k−1,−(k−1),0,…,0),k=2,…,g. The contrast 𝛍hk⊤μ compares level k with the average of the levels before it, since it is (k−1) times 1k−1∑l<kμl−μk. The hk are mutually orthogonal in the ordinary sense, so in a balanced design they are orthogonal contrasts. They suit factors whose levels are added in a meaningful order, such as successive refinements of a treatment, where each new level is compared with everything before it.

8.7.4. Polynomial contrasts

When the levels of a factor are ordered and equally spaced, such as doses 0,10,20,30 or the seven points of a party scale, the natural questions are about the shape of the sequence of means. Is there a linear trend? Curvature? Give the levels scores sk=k. Apply Gram–Schmidt (Proposition 6.2.2) to the vectors 1,s,s2,…,sg−1 in Rg, where sj has entries skj. This gives orthogonal vectors p0,…,pg−1, and pj is a polynomial of degree j in the score. For j≥1, pj⊥1, so pj is a contrast. Rescaled to integers, the first three for g=7 are linear (−3,−2,−1,0,1,2,3),quadratic (5,0,−3,−4,−3,0,5),cubic (−1,1,1,0,−1,−1,1). By construction pj is orthogonal to every polynomial of degree less than j. So if the level means follow a polynomial of degree d in the score, every contrast of degree above d is zero. The linear contrast measures the trend, the quadratic the curvature beyond it, and so on. This is the reason for their use.

With unequal group sizes the classical integer contrasts are no longer orthogonal for the design. The fix follows Proposition 8.7.2(c). Orthogonality is needed in the inner product ∑kckdk/nk, which suggests setting cj=Dpj, where now the pj are orthogonal polynomials in the weighted inner product ∑knkpkqk. Then ∑kcjk=∑knkpjk=0, and ∑kcjkclk/nk=∑knkpjkplk=0 for j≠l. In observation space, ucj=Zpj is the polynomial of degree j in each observation’s score, orthogonalized over the observations. The weighted linear contrast gives exactly the regression sum of squares of y on the score (Exercise (C1)).

Example (Trend in ideology across party identification)

Return to the seven party-identification groups of Example (Party identification and ideology). The between-group sum of squares is 772.87. The classical linear contrast has estimate ψ^=∑k(k−4)y¯k=11.175, variance 0.1652σ2 by Eq. 8.7.1, and sum of squares 755.85, which is 97.8 percent of the between-group sum of squares. The quadratic and cubic contrasts have sums of squares 10.15 and 2.58. Ideology rises almost linearly across the seven-point party scale, with some upward curvature (Figure 8.7.1).

The group sizes range from 37 to 200, so the six classical contrasts are not orthogonal for this design. Their sums of squares add to 784.02, which exceeds the between-group total. Part of the variation is counted twice. The weighted orthogonal polynomial contrasts decompose the total exactly: 746.57 for the linear trend and 26.30 for the five higher-degree contrasts together.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

anes = sm.datasets.anes96.load_pandas().data
y = anes["selfLR"].to_numpy()
level = anes["PID"].to_numpy().astype(int)       # 0 = strong Dem. ... 6 = strong Rep.
g = 7
n_k = np.bincount(level).astype(float)
means = np.bincount(level, weights=y) / n_k
def orthogonal_polynomials(scores, weights):
    """Columns 1..g-1: polynomials in the scores, orthogonal in sum_k w_k u_k v_k."""
    V = np.vander(scores, len(scores), increasing=True)      # 1, s, s^2, ...
    W = np.sqrt(weights)[:, None]
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(W * V)                               # weighted Gram-Schmidt
    P = Q / W                                                # back to the original scale
    P = P / np.abs(P).max(axis=0)                            # cosmetic rescaling
    return P[:, 1:]                                          # drop the constant column

def contrast_ss(c, means, n_k):
    """Estimate c'mu-hat and its sum of squares (c'mu-hat)^2 / sum(c_k^2 / n_k)."""
    psi = c @ means
    return psi, psi ** 2 / np.sum(c ** 2 / n_k)

scores = np.arange(g, dtype=float)
C_unw = orthogonal_polynomials(scores, np.ones(g))           # the classical table (equal n)
C_wtd = n_k[:, None] * orthogonal_polynomials(scores, n_k)   # c_k = n_k p(k)
ss_between = np.sum(n_k * (means - y.mean()) ** 2)
for name, C in [("unweighted", C_unw), ("weighted", C_wtd)]:
    ss = [contrast_ss(C[:, j], means, n_k)[1] for j in range(g - 1)]
    print(f"{name:10s} SS by degree: {np.round(ss, 2)}  total {sum(ss):.2f}",
          f"(between groups {ss_between:.2f})")

8.7.5. Coding matrices are not contrast matrices

Section 8.5 coded a factor by an intercept and the columns ZC. When the columns of C are contrasts, as for Helmert or polynomial coding, it is tempting to read the coefficient of column j as the estimate of the contrast 𝛍cj⊤μ. That reading is wrong in general. By Proposition 8.5.1(b) the coefficients are the rows of K−1, with K=[1,C], applied to 𝛍μ^. The rows of K−1 and the columns of K are related simply only when the columns are orthogonal.

Proposition 8.7.3 (Orthogonal codings)

If the columns of K=[1g,c1,…,cg−1] are mutually orthogonal, then the coefficients of the coded model are 𝛍γ^0=1g∑ky¯k,γ^j=cj⊤μ^cj⊤cj,j=1,…,g−1, whatever the group sizes.

Proof. With orthogonal columns, K⊤K=diag(g,c1⊤c1,…,cg−1⊤cg−1), so K−1=(K⊤K)−1K⊤. Apply Proposition 8.5.1(b).

The orthogonality required here is the plain one, cj⊤cl=0, not the design orthogonality of Definition 8.7.1, because 𝛄𝛍γ^=K−1μ^ is pure algebra and does not involve the nk. Helmert coding, in the usual software convention, uses the columns cj=−hj+1, which compare level j+1 with the levels before it, and cj⊤cj=j(j+1). In the party data the first three Helmert coefficients are 0.1408, 0.0698 and 0.1271, while the contrasts themselves are 0.2817, 0.4187 and 1.5253, larger by the factors 2, 6 and 12. For a non-orthogonal coding there is no such shortcut. Reference coding has cj=ej+1, yet its coefficients are μj+1−μ1, not μj+1.

def helmert(g):
    """Coding matrix whose column k compares level k+1 with the mean of levels 1..k."""
    C = np.zeros((g, g - 1))
    for k in range(1, g):
        C[:k, k - 1] = -1.0
        C[k, k - 1] = k
    return C

H = helmert(g)
K = np.column_stack([np.ones(g), H])
gamma = np.linalg.solve(K, means)                  # Helmert-coded coefficients
psi = H.T @ means                                  # the contrasts c_k' mu-hat
print("coefficients:", np.round(gamma[1:], 4))
print("contrasts:   ", np.round(psi, 4))
print("ratio:       ", np.round(psi / gamma[1:], 1))   # = c_k' c_k = k(k+1)

Warning. A coefficient from a contrast-coded regression estimates a contrast only up to a scale factor, and only when the coding columns are orthogonal. To test or report a contrast, compute 𝛍c⊤μ^ and its variance Eq. 8.7.1 directly, or read it from the coded fit using K−1. Do not read it off the coefficient table.

8.7.6. Contrasts in the two-way additive model

In the additive model μ+αi+βj of Section 8.6, a row contrast is ∑iciαi with ∑ici=0. It is a combination of the differences αi−α1, so in a connected design it is estimable (Theorem 8.6.1). In a balanced design, with m≥1 observations in every one of the ab cells, the least squares fit is y¯i⋅⋅+y¯⋅j⋅−y¯⋅⋅⋅. This is shown in Example (Additive two-factor model, one observation per cell) for m=1, and the averaging argument there is unchanged for m>1. Hence ∑iciαi^=∑ici(y¯i⋅⋅+y¯⋅j⋅−y¯⋅⋅⋅)=∑iciy¯i⋅⋅,Var=σ2bm∑ici2, since the terms not depending on i are multiplied by ∑ici=0. The row contrast is estimated from the row means alone, as if factor B were absent. In an unbalanced design this is false. The row means are then contaminated by the column effects (Exercise (B2)), and the least squares estimate has to adjust for them. That adjustment is the subject of Chapter 17. Interaction contrasts, and their estimability when cells are empty, were treated in Proposition 8.6.2.

8.7.7. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Find the linear, quadratic and cubic orthogonal polynomial contrasts for g=4 equally spaced levels, scaled to integers. Check that they are mutually orthogonal and that the quadratic contrast vanishes when μk=a+bk.

Exercise (A2)

Show that the Helmert contrasts h2,…,hg are orthogonal for the design iff n1=n2=⋯=ng−1, whatever ng is. Hint: compute ∑khjkhlk/nk for j<l.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that for every contrast c, SS(c)≤∑knk(y¯k−y¯)2, with equality when ck=nk(y¯k−y¯). This fact is the basis of Scheffé’s method in Chapter 13 (Theorem 13.2.2).

Solution. By Proposition 8.7.2(b), SS(c)=‖Py‖2 with P projecting onto a line inside S=C(Z)∩1⊥. Let PS=M−n−111⊤. Because the line lies in S, Py=PPSy (Theorem 6.5.1(a)), and so ‖Py‖2≤‖PSy‖2=∑knk(y¯k−y¯)2. Equality holds when the line contains PSy, whose entries are y¯k−y¯ at level k. That vector is uc for ck=nk(y¯k−y¯), and this c is a contrast because ∑knk(y¯k−y¯)=0.

Exercise (B2)

In the additive two-way model with nij observations in cell (i,j), show that E(y¯i⋅⋅)=μ+αi+∑jnijβj/ni⋅. Deduce that ∑iciy¯i⋅⋅ is unbiased for every row contrast and every 𝛃β iff the proportions nij/ni⋅ do not depend on i, that is, iff the cell counts are proportional, nij=ni⋅n⋅j/n.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let x be the n-vector giving each observation’s score sk. Show that the sum of squares of the weighted linear contrast c1=Dp1 equals the regression sum of squares ‖(M1−n−111⊤)y‖2 of the simple regression of y on x with intercept, where M1 projects onto C([1,x]). Conclude that the remaining weighted contrasts together measure the lack of fit of the straight line to the level means.

Solution. In the weighted inner product, p1 is s minus its weighted mean, s−s¯w1 with s¯w=∑knksk/n, up to scale. Then uc1=ZD−1Dp1=Zp1, whose entries are sk−s¯w. That is x−x¯1, the centred score vector. By Proposition 8.7.2(b), SS(c1) is the squared length of the projection of y onto the line spanned by x−x¯1. By Example (Intercept inside simple regression) this line is C([1,x])∩1⊥, and the projection onto it is M1−n−111⊤. The remaining weighted contrasts span the orthogonal complement of that line in C(Z)∩1⊥, which is C(Z)∩C([1,x])⊥. Their total sum of squares is ‖(M−M1)y‖2, the reduction in residual sum of squares from the straight-line model to the model with a separate mean for each level.

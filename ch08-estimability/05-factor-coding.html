8.5  Indicator variables and the coding of a factor

Most regressors in practice are not measurements on a continuous scale. They record a category: treatment arm, region, occupation, party, a yes-or-no answer. Such a variable is a factor, and its possible values are its levels. A factor enters a linear model through indicator variables (also called dummy variables), columns of zeros and ones that mark membership of a level. There are many ways to build these columns, and they give quite different coefficient tables. This section shows that they all describe the same fitted model. Each choice of coding is a choice of which linear functions of the level means the coefficients report. It gives a single formula that says exactly which ones.

8.5.1. One binary regressor

Suppose each observation belongs to one of two groups, and let di=1 if observation i is in group 1 and di=0 if it is in group 0. The model E(yi)=β0+β1di says that the mean is μ0=β0 in group 0 and μ1=β0+β1 in group 1. So β1=μ1−μ0 is the difference between the two group means. The least squares fit sets the fitted value in each group to that group’s mean (the model space is the set of vectors constant within groups, Example (The one-way layout)), so β^0=y¯0 and β^1=y¯1−y¯0. The t statistic for β1=0 is the pooled two-sample t statistic (Exercise (A1)). The familiar two-sample comparison is a regression on one indicator.

It is tempting to add a second indicator, 1−di, for group 0. Then X=[1,d,1−d] has three columns, but they satisfy 1−d−(1−d)=0, so rank(X)=2. This is the dummy variable trap: an intercept together with an indicator for every level produces an exactly collinear model matrix. By Section 8.1, none of the three coefficients is then identifiable. The trap is not a mistake in the model, which still describes two means, but in the coordinates.

8.5.2. A factor with g levels

Let a factor have levels 1,…,g, with nk≥1 observations at level k. Its indicator matrix is the n×g matrix Z=[z1,…,zg] with zik=1 if observation i is at level k and 0 otherwise. Each row of Z contains a single 1, so

Z1g=1n,Z⊤Z=D=diag(n1,…,ng),Z⊤y=(y1⋅,…,yg⋅)⊤,
(8.5.1)

where yk⋅ is the total of the responses at level k. The columns have disjoint supports, so Z has full column rank g, and C(Z) is the set of vectors that are constant within levels.

The cell-means model 𝛍E(Y)=Zμ, 𝛍μ=(μ1,…,μg)⊤, gives each level its own mean. It has full rank, and by Eq. 8.5.1 𝛍μ^=D−1Z⊤y=(y¯1,…,y¯g)⊤, the vector of level means. Any other model containing C(Z) and nothing more has the same fit. The overparameterized model [1,Z] of Example (A rank-deficient layout) is one example. The full-rank codings below are others.

Definition 8.5.1 (Coding of a factor)

A coding of a factor with g levels is a nonsingular g×g matrix K. The coded model matrix is ZK, and the model is 𝛄E(Y)=ZKγ. When the first column of K is 1g, so that K=[1g,C], the coded matrix is [1n,ZC]: an intercept and the g−1 columns ZC. The g×(g−1) matrix C is then called the coding matrix of the factor.

Row k of K lists the values that the coded columns take for an observation at level k. In the software of Section 8.4 the matrix C is what one specifies (in R, the “contrasts” attribute of a factor), and the program builds the columns ZC from it.

Proposition 8.5.1 (What a coding estimates)

Let K be a coding and 𝛍μ^=D−1Z⊤y the vector of level means. Then:

  • (a) C(ZK)=C(Z), so every coding has the same fitted values, residuals and residual sum of squares;

  • (b) the coefficients are 𝛄𝛍γ=K−1μ, and their least squares estimates are 𝛄𝛍γ^=K−1μ^;

  • (c) [1g,C] is nonsingular iff the columns of C are linearly independent and 1g∉C(C);

  • (d) to make the coefficients estimate prescribed linear functions 𝛍Lμ of the level means, with L a nonsingular g×g matrix, use the coding K=L−1.

Proof.

  • (a) The columns of ZK lie in C(Z), and rank(ZK)=rank(Z)=g because K is nonsingular (Proposition 1.2.3(b)). A subspace of C(Z) of the same dimension is C(Z) itself. The fit depends only on the column space (Theorem 6.7.1).
  • (b) The two models describe the same means iff 𝛄𝛍ZKγ=Zμ, and since Z has full column rank this means 𝛄𝛍Kγ=μ. For the estimates, 𝛄𝛍ZKγ^=My=Zμ^, and the same cancellation gives 𝛄𝛍Kγ^=μ^.
  • (c) A square matrix is nonsingular iff its columns are linearly independent. The columns 1g,c1,…,cg−1 are independent iff the cj are independent and 1g is not in their span.
  • (d) With K=L−1, (b) gives 𝛄𝛍γ=Lμ.

Part (b) is the whole story. Whatever the coding, the coefficients are fixed linear combinations of the level means, and these combinations are the rows of K−1, not the columns of K. Each such combination is estimable in the overparameterized model, since it is a function of the means. Part (d) reverses the logic, and in practice it is the useful direction. Decide which questions the coefficients should answer, write them as the rows of L, and invert.

8.5.3. The standard codings

For a factor with g levels, the codings in common use are the following. For each we give the meaning of the intercept γ0 and of the other coefficients γ1,…,γg−1, that is, the rows of K−1.

  • Reference (treatment) coding. C=(0⊤Ig−1): the columns are the indicators of levels 2,…,g. Then γ0=μ1, and γj=μj+1−μ1 is the difference between level j+1 and the reference level 1. The level chosen as reference is arbitrary.
  • Sum-to-zero (deviation or effect) coding. C=(Ig−1−1⊤). Level k<g has mean γ0+γk and level g has mean γ0−∑j<gγj. Summing over the g levels gives γ0=μ¯=g−1∑kμk, the unweighted average of the level means. Then γk=μk−μ¯ is the deviation of level k from that average. The deviation of the last level is −∑j<gγj.
  • Weighted effect coding. C=(Ig−1−(n1,…,ng−1)/ng). Now ∑knkμk=nγ0, so γ0=∑knkμk/n, the size-weighted average, estimated by the overall mean y¯. The coefficient γk=μk−γ0 is the deviation from it.
  • Successive differences. With L having first row g−11⊤ and rows ej+1⊤−ej⊤, part (d) gives a coding in which γ0=μ¯ and γj=μj+1−μj. This is natural for ordered levels.
  • Helmert and orthogonal polynomial codings, whose columns are contrasts, are treated in Section 8.7.

The first three are the side conditions of Example (Five side conditions, one fit) in another language. In the overparameterized model μ+αk, reference coding is the side condition α1=0, sum-to-zero coding is ∑kαk=0, and weighted effect coding is ∑knkαk=0. In each case the constrained (μ,α2,…,αg) or (μ,α1,…,αg−1) equals 𝛄γ (Exercise (C1)).

Example (Party identification and ideology)

An extract of the 1996 American National Election Study, distributed with statsmodels as a public-domain data set, contains 944 respondents who placed themselves on a seven-point scale from extremely liberal (1) to extremely conservative (7). It also records their party identification in seven levels from strong Democrat to strong Republican. The level means and sizes are

party identification strong Dem weak Dem ind. Dem independent ind. Rep weak Rep strong Rep
nk 200 180 108 37 94 150 175
y¯k 3.335 3.617 3.685 4.054 4.957 5.033 5.691

Here are the coefficients of four codings of the same factor (γ1,…,γ6 refer to the columns of C in the order defined above; for the two deviation codings they belong to the first six levels):

coding γ^0 γ^1 γ^2 γ^3 γ^4 γ^5 γ^6
reference (strong Dem) 3.335 0.282 0.350 0.719 1.622 1.698 2.356
sum to zero 4.339 -1.004 -0.722 -0.654 -0.285 0.618 0.694
weighted effect 4.325 -0.990 -0.709 -0.640 -0.271 0.632 0.708
successive differences 4.339 0.282 0.069 0.369 0.903 0.076 0.658

Each row is 𝛍K−1μ^ for its own K, as Proposition 8.5.1(b) says, and all four have residual sum of squares 1178.3. The intercepts are the reference mean 3.335, the unweighted mean of the seven means 4.339, the overall mean y¯=4.325 and again 4.339. Figure 8.5.1 shows the three reference points. The successive differences show that the largest step in ideology, 0.903, falls between pure independents and independents leaning Republican, and the step between weak and strong Republicans is 0.658. The reference coding cannot show this directly. The two deviation codings nearly agree here because the groups are of similar size, apart from the small group of pure independents.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

anes = sm.datasets.anes96.load_pandas().data
y = anes["selfLR"].to_numpy()        # 1 = very liberal ... 7 = very conservative
level = anes["PID"].to_numpy().astype(int)    # 0 = strong Dem. ... 6 = strong Rep.
g = 7
Z = np.eye(g)[level]                          # n x 7 indicator (cell-means) matrix
n_k = Z.sum(axis=0)
cell_means = (Z.T @ y) / n_k
print("group sizes:", n_k.astype(int))
print("cell means: ", np.round(cell_means, 3))
def coding_matrices(n_k):
    """Each coding is X = Z K for a nonsingular g x g K; the coefficients are K^{-1} mu."""
    g = len(n_k)
    one = np.ones((g, 1))
    reference = np.vstack([np.zeros(g - 1), np.eye(g - 1)])            # level 0 is baseline
    deviation = np.vstack([np.eye(g - 1), -np.ones(g - 1)])            # sum-to-zero
    weighted = np.vstack([np.eye(g - 1), -n_k[:-1] / n_k[-1]])         # sum n_k * effect = 0
    successive = np.array([[(j + 1 - g if i <= j else j + 1) / g for j in range(g - 1)]
                           for i in range(g)])                         # backward differences
    return {"cell means": np.eye(g),
            "reference": np.hstack([one, reference]),
            "sum to zero": np.hstack([one, deviation]),
            "weighted effect": np.hstack([one, weighted]),
            "successive differences": np.hstack([one, successive])}

for name, K in coding_matrices(n_k).items():
    Xc = Z @ K
    gamma = np.linalg.lstsq(Xc, y, rcond=None)[0]
    print(f"{name:23s}", np.round(gamma, 3))

8.5.4. Choosing a coding

Since every coding gives the same fit, the choice is about communication and, to a lesser extent, precision. Four points are worth keeping in mind.

Anything that depends on the fit is invariant. This includes fitted values, residuals, R2, σ^2, and the F test that all levels have the same mean. That test compares C(Z) with C(1), and the coding does not enter it (Theorem 11.1.1).

Coefficient tests answer different questions. The t test for a reference-coded coefficient tests whether level k differs from the reference level. The t test for a sum-coded coefficient tests whether level k differs from the average of all levels, a question that changes if a level is added. Neither question is wrong, but they are different questions. A table of coefficients from an unfamiliar coding should never be read as if it came from the familiar one.

Precision depends on the reference. In reference coding Var(γ^j)=σ2(1/nj+1+1/n1). A small reference group inflates the variance of every coefficient. In Example (Party identification and ideology), comparing each of the other five levels with the 37 pure independents instead of the 200 strong Democrats would multiply the standard errors by factors between 1.55 and 1.76. A common default is to use the largest group, or a natural control condition, as the reference.

With other regressors, coefficients are adjusted comparisons. When the model also contains other variables, a reference-coded coefficient compares level k with the reference level at equal values of those variables. That is the “holding the others fixed” reading of Definition 5.7.1, with all its caveats. Section 8.6 gives an example in which adjustment changes the answer a great deal.

Idea. A coding is a choice of coordinates for the g-dimensional space of level means. The coefficients are 𝛍K−1μ^. To get the comparisons you want, write them down first as the rows of a nonsingular matrix L, then code with K=L−1.

For an ordered factor, a useful instance of this principle is the cumulative coding, with columns 1{level≥k} for k=2,…,g. Then K is lower triangular with ones on and below the diagonal. Its inverse takes successive differences, so the intercept is μ1 and the coefficient of the kth column is μk−μk−1 (Exercise (B2)). An observation’s mean is built up as a sum of steps, which is often how an ordered factor is best described.

8.5.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In the model E(yi)=β0+β1di, with n0 observations in group 0 (level 1) and n1 in group 1 (level 2), write down the coding matrix K with 𝛍𝛃μ=Kβ. Use Proposition 8.5.1(b), not an inverse of X⊤X, to find 𝛃β^. Then show that Var(β^1)=σ2(1/n0+1/n1) and that β^1/se(β^1) is the pooled two-sample t statistic, where s2=SSE/(n−2) replaces σ2.

Solution. The two means are μ0=β0 and μ1=β0+β1, so K=(1011) and K−1=(10−11). By Proposition 8.5.1(b), 𝛃𝛍β^=K−1μ^=(y¯0,y¯1−y¯0)⊤. The level means are the estimates of the full-rank cell-means model, with 𝛍Cov(μ^)=σ2D−1=σ2diag(1/n0,1/n1) (Theorem 5.5.1), so Var(β^1)=(−1,1)σ2D−1(−1,1)⊤=σ2(1/n0+1/n1). The residuals are deviations from the group means (Proposition 8.5.1(a)), so s2 is the pooled variance, and the ratio is (y¯1−y¯0)/{s1/n0+1/n1}.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let X have full column rank, let ei be the ith coordinate vector with ei∉C(X), and fit [X,ei] with coefficients 𝛃(β,δ). Write e^i and hii for the residual and the leverage (Definition 6.8.1) of observation i in the fit of X alone.

  • (a) Use Theorem 6.6.2, with ei as the regressor of interest and X as the one partialled out, to show that δ^=e^i/(1−hii), and explain why hii<1.

  • (b) Show that the enlarged fit reproduces yi exactly, and that its 𝛃β^ is the estimate 𝛃β^(i) without observation i.

  • (c) Deduce the deletion identity 𝛃yi−x(i)⊤β^(i)=e^i/(1−hii): the error in predicting yi from the other observations is the ordinary residual inflated by 1/(1−hii).

Solution.

  • (a) Apply Theorem 6.6.2(a) with X1=ei and X2=X, and let M project onto C(X). Then δ^ is the coefficient in the regression of y on (I−M)ei: δ^=ei⊤(I−M)y/ei⊤(I−M)ei=e^i/(1−hii). The denominator is ‖(I−M)ei‖2, which is positive because ei∉C(X).
  • (b) The residual vector of the enlarged fit is orthogonal to every column, in particular to ei, so its ith entry is zero. For fixed 𝛃β, the residual sum of squares 𝛃𝛃∑j≠i(yj−x(j)⊤β)2+(yi−x(i)⊤β−δ)2 is minimized over δ by making the last term zero. What remains is the deletion criterion, and its minimizer 𝛃β^(i) is unique: if Xb=cei with b≠0, then c≠0 by full rank, and ei∈C(X). So 𝛃𝛃β^=β^(i).
  • (c) By (b), 𝛃δ^=yi−x(i)⊤β^(i); compare with (a). The t statistic for δ is the externally studentized residual of Chapter 20.

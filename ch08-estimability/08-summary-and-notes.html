8.8  Summary and Notes

8.8.1. Summary

Idea.

  1. The data determine the mean vector 𝛃Xβ and nothing more. A function of 𝛃β is identifiable iff it is a function of 𝛃Xβ, and 𝛃β itself is identifiable iff rank(X)=p (Definition 8.1.1, Theorem 8.1.1 and Proposition 8.1.2).

  2. A single coefficient is identifiable iff its column is not a combination of the others (Proposition 8.1.3). Rank deficiency comes from construction, from identities among variables (age, period and cohort), or from the design (Example (Age, period and cohort)).

  3. A linear function 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β has a linear unbiased estimator iff it is identifiable iff 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤) (Definition 8.2.1 and Theorem 8.2.2). The estimable functions form a space of dimension rank(X) (Corollary 8.2.3).

  4. Eight equivalent tests of estimability, including 𝛌λ⊥N(X), 𝛌𝛌λ⊤GX⊤X=λ⊤, and invariance over least squares solutions (Theorem 8.3.1). The estimate is 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ for any solution, with variance 𝛌𝛌σ2λ⊤Gλ for any generalized inverse (Proposition 8.3.2).

  5. For a nonestimable 𝛌λ, a generalized-inverse solution estimates a different, estimable function 𝛌𝛃λ⊤GX⊤Xβ, and the reported variance can be any number (Proposition 8.3.3 and Exercise (C1)). Decide estimability with the SVD of X, and treat near-nonestimable functions as unestimated (Example (Estimable in principle, not in practice)).

  6. Full-rank reparameterizations are choices of basis for the estimable space (Theorem 8.4.1). Side conditions Tb=0 select a unique solution iff rank(T)=p−r and no combination of them is estimable (Theorem 8.4.2). The constrained coefficients are then particular estimable functions (Proposition 8.4.3). The minimum-norm solution is itself a side condition (Proposition 8.4.4).

  7. A coding of a factor is a nonsingular matrix K, and its coefficients estimate 𝛍K−1μ, the rows of K−1 applied to the level means (Definition 8.5.1 and Proposition 8.5.1).

  8. In the additive two-way model the rank is a+b minus the number of connected components of the design, and a difference of levels is estimable iff the levels are connected (Theorem 8.6.1). In the interaction model, exactly the combinations of occupied cell means are estimable (Proposition 8.6.2).

  9. With a covariate, adjusted differences between levels subtract the slope times the difference in covariate means, the slope being estimated within levels (Proposition 8.6.3).

  10. Contrasts are the estimable functions of a factor’s effects (Definition 8.7.1 and Theorem 8.7.1). Orthogonal contrasts, in the design inner product ∑kckdk/nk, decompose the between-level sum of squares (Proposition 8.7.2). Coding coefficients equal scaled contrasts only for orthogonal codings (Proposition 8.7.3).

8.8.2. Notes and sources

Identifiability and estimability. Christensen (2020, §2.1) develops estimability from identifiability, as this chapter does, and stresses that the estimable functions are exactly the identifiable linear ones. Agresti (2015, §1.4) gives a short treatment aimed at generalized linear models, where the same definitions apply to the linear predictor. Rencher and Schaalje (2008, ch. 12) is a detailed textbook account of the non-full-rank model: estimable functions and their tests, the estimators r⊤X⊤y and 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^, reparameterization and side conditions, with small worked layouts. The notion of an estimable function goes back to R. C. Bose’s work on the design of experiments in the 1940s. Scheffé (1959) and Searle (1971) made it standard, and Rao (1973) treats it through generalized inverses. The characterization Theorem 8.3.1 collects conditions that are scattered through these sources. That a generalized-inverse solution estimates the estimable function 𝛌𝛃λ⊤GX⊤Xβ (Proposition 8.3.3) follows at once from 𝛃E(GX⊤Y)=GX⊤Xβ, but its consequence for software output deserves more emphasis than it usually gets.

Side conditions and software. The exact conditions of Theorem 8.4.2, and the formula (X⊤X+T⊤T)−1X⊤y, are standard (see Rencher and Schaalje 2008, §12.6, and Searle 1971). The coding matrices, called “contrasts”, that S and R use for factors are described by Chambers and Hastie (1992), and Venables and Ripley (2002) discuss the treatment, sum, Helmert and polynomial codings of R. The minimum-norm solution rests on the Moore–Penrose inverse (Penrose 1955), and column-pivoted QR is described in Golub and Van Loan (2013).

Indicator variables. Sen and Srivastava (1990, ch. 4) is a practical account of indicator variables: the two-sample comparison as a regression, polychotomous factors, hierarchical codings, factors with continuous covariates, broken-line regression and aggregated indicators as responses. Where this chapter meets those themes it takes its own route: the two-sample comparison through the coding proposition (Exercise (A1)), the indicator of a single observation through the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem (Exercise (B1)), and count regressors without intercept in a new setting (Exercise (B3)).

Connectedness, empty cells and cohorts. Connectedness of two-way designs, and its role in estimability, is treated at length by Searle (1971, 1987). Searle (1987) is also the standard reference for unbalanced data and empty cells, which Chapter 17 takes up. The age–period–cohort problem of Example (Age, period and cohort) is a well-known instance of a dependency among variables. Holford (1983) showed which functions of the age, period and cohort effects are estimable, among them the second differences of Exercise (C1).

Data. The survey examples use the extract of the 1996 American National Election Study distributed with statsmodels (Seabold and Perktold 2010) as a public-domain data set. The other examples use simulated data, generated by the scripts in code/ch08/.

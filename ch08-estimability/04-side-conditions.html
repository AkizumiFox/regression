8.4  Reparameterization and side conditions

A rank-deficient model can be handled in three ways. One can work with it as it stands, estimating only estimable functions, which is what the last two sections did. One can reparameterize, replacing X by a full-rank matrix with the same column space. Or one can impose side conditions, linear restrictions on 𝛃β that select a single coefficient vector from each set 𝛃β+N(X). This section shows that the second and third are two descriptions of the same act: choosing coordinates for the mean vector. Neither changes any estimable quantity. It also records what the most common software does by default.

8.4.1. Full-rank reparameterizations

By Corollary 8.2.3, at most r estimable functions are linearly independent. A set of exactly r of them can serve as a new parameter vector.

Theorem 8.4.1 (Reparameterizing to full rank)

Let rank(X)=r. Let U be an r×p matrix of rank r whose rows lie in C(X⊤), so that 𝛄𝛃γ=Uβ is a vector of r linearly independent estimable functions, and put

Z=XU⊤(UU⊤)−1.
(8.4.1)

Then:

  • (a) X=ZU, Z has full column rank r, and C(Z)=C(X);

  • (b) the models 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ, 𝛃β∈Rp, and 𝛄E(Y)=Zγ, 𝛄γ∈Rr, describe the same set of mean vectors, with 𝛄𝛃γ=Uβ;

  • (c) the least squares estimate in the new model is 𝛄𝛃γ^=(Z⊤Z)−1Z⊤y=Uβ^ for every least squares solution 𝛃β^ of the old one;

  • (d) 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β is estimable iff 𝛌λ=U⊤c for some c∈Rr, which is then unique, and in that case 𝛌𝛃𝛄λ⊤β^=c⊤γ^;

  • (e) conversely, if Z is any n×r matrix of full column rank with C(Z)=C(X), then X=ZU for a unique U, and U has rank r and estimable rows.

Proof. Since C(U⊤)⊆C(X⊤) and both have dimension r, C(U⊤)=C(X⊤). The matrix Q=U⊤(UU⊤)−1U is symmetric and idempotent with column space C(U⊤), so it is the orthogonal projection onto C(X⊤) (Theorem 6.3.3). Hence I−Q projects onto C(X⊤)⊥=N(X), and X(I−Q)=0, that is, X=XQ=ZU. (a) Now r=rank(X)=rank(ZU)≤rank(Z)≤r, so Z has full column rank. C(Z)⊆C(X) because Z=X(⋅), and C(X)⊆C(Z) because X=ZU. (b) 𝛃𝛃Xβ=Z(Uβ), and 𝛃𝛃β↦Uβ maps Rp onto Rr because U has full row rank. So both models describe C(X). (c) 𝛄𝛃𝛃Zγ^=My=Xβ^=Z(Uβ^) by Theorem 6.4.1, and Z has full column rank. (d) 𝛌λ is estimable iff 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤)=C(U⊤). The vector c is unique because U⊤ has full column rank, and 𝛌𝛃𝛃𝛄λ⊤β^=c⊤Uβ^=c⊤γ^. (e) Each column of X lies in C(Z), so X=ZU with U=(Z⊤Z)−1Z⊤X, unique because Z has full column rank. Then 𝛃𝛃Uβ=(Z⊤Z)−1Z⊤(Xβ) is a linear function of 𝛃Xβ, so each row of U is estimable. Finally r=rank(X)≤rank(U)≤r.

So reparameterization is the same thing as choosing a basis for the estimable space. Theorem 6.7.1 of Chapter 6 already showed that fitted values and residuals do not care which basis is chosen. Part (d) adds that estimable functions do not care either: each is a fixed linear combination of the new parameters, and its estimate is the same combination of their estimates. Everything in the full-rank theory of Chapter 5 now applies to 𝛄γ^, including Theorem 5.5.1 and the variance estimator of Theorem 5.6.1, with p replaced by r.

For the one-way layout with 𝛃β=(μ,α1,…,αg)⊤, two choices of U stand out. The rows (1,ek⊤), k=1,…,g, give 𝛄γ=(μ+α1,…,μ+αg)⊤, the cell means, and Z is the indicator matrix. The rows (1,e1⊤) and (0,ek⊤−e1⊤), k=2,…,g, give the first group’s mean and the differences from it. This is the reference coding of Section 8.5.

8.4.2. Side conditions

The other classical device leaves X alone and adds equations. A side condition is a set of linear restrictions Tb=0, with T a q×p matrix, imposed on the solutions of the normal equations. The aim is to single out one solution. Examples in the one-way layout are α1=0, ∑kαk=0 and μ=0. The theorem says exactly which restrictions achieve this.

Theorem 8.4.2 (Side conditions)

Let X be n×p of rank r and T be q×p. The following are equivalent:

  • (a) for every y∈Rn there is exactly one b with X⊤Xb=X⊤y and Tb=0;

  • (b) for every 𝛍μ∈C(X) there is exactly one b with 𝛍Xb=μ and Tb=0;

  • (c) Rp=N(X)⊕N(T);

  • (d) rank(XT)=p and C(T⊤)∩C(X⊤)={0};

  • (e) rank(T)=p−r, and no nonzero linear combination of the rows of T is estimable.

When these hold, X⊤X+T⊤T is nonsingular, the solution in (a) is

𝛃β^T=(X⊤X+T⊤T)−1X⊤y,
(8.4.2)

and (X⊤X+T⊤T)−1 is a generalized inverse of X⊤X.

Proof. (a)⇔(b). The solutions of the normal equations for y are the b with Xb=My (Theorem 6.4.2), and My runs over all of C(X) as y does.

(b)⇔(c). The set 𝛍{b:Xb=μ} is a coset b0+N(X), and every b0∈Rp arises (take 𝛍μ=Xb0). So (b) says that every coset of N(X) meets N(T) in exactly one point. Some point b0+v of the coset lies in N(T) iff b0∈N(T)+N(X), so existence for every coset means Rp=N(X)+N(T). Two points of the same coset that both lie in N(T) differ by an element of N(X)∩N(T), and a nonzero element of that intersection added to one such point gives a second. So uniqueness means N(X)∩N(T)={0}. Together, these say the sum is direct.

(c)⇔(d). By Lemma 6.2.1 and Lemma 6.2.5, (N(X)+N(T))⊥=C(X⊤)∩C(T⊤),(N(X)∩N(T))⊥=C(X⊤)+C(T⊤). So N(X)+N(T)=Rp iff C(X⊤)∩C(T⊤)={0}, and N(X)∩N(T)={0} iff C(X⊤)+C(T⊤)=Rp. The last space is the row space of the stacked matrix, so this holds iff that matrix has rank p.

(d)⇔(e). A vector in C(T⊤) is a combination of the rows of T, and it is estimable iff it lies in C(X⊤). So the second halves of (d) and (e) say the same thing. When they hold, the sum C(X⊤)+C(T⊤) is direct, with dimension r+rank(T). This equals p iff rank(T)=p−r.

For the formula, suppose (d). If (X⊤X+T⊤T)v=0, then 0=v⊤X⊤Xv+v⊤T⊤Tv=‖Xv‖2+‖Tv‖2, so v lies in N(X)∩N(T)={0}. Hence the matrix is nonsingular. The solution b of (a) satisfies (X⊤X+T⊤T)b=X⊤y+T⊤0, which gives Eq. 8.4.2. Finally, write GT=(X⊤X+T⊤T)−1. For every y, GTX⊤y solves the normal equations, so X⊤XGTX⊤=X⊤. Multiplying on the right by X gives X⊤XGTX⊤X=X⊤X.

Condition (e) is the familiar rule of thumb, made exact: a side condition must supply exactly as many restrictions as the rank deficiency, and none of them, singly or in combination, may be estimable. The reason for the second requirement is clear from (d). An estimable restriction constrains the mean vector itself, and so it changes the model instead of merely choosing coordinates.

Remark (Existence and uniqueness separately). The proof shows more than the theorem states. The condition C(T⊤)∩C(X⊤)={0} alone guarantees that the restricted normal equations always have at least one solution. The condition rank(XT)=p alone guarantees at most one. A constraint that is too weak, such as a single restriction when p−r=2, leaves many solutions. One with an estimable part leaves none for most y (Exercise (C2)).

8.4.3. What the constrained coefficients mean

Under a side condition every coefficient becomes identifiable, and it acquires a meaning.

Proposition 8.4.3 (Constrained coefficients are estimable functions)

Suppose T satisfies the conditions of Theorem 8.4.2, and let PT=(X⊤X+T⊤T)−1X⊤X. For each 𝛃β, 𝛃PTβ is the unique vector b with 𝛃Xb=Xβ and Tb=0. The matrix PT is idempotent, with range N(T) and null space N(X). Every row of PT lies in C(X⊤). So each constrained coefficient 𝛃ej⊤PTβ is an estimable function of the original parameters, and 𝛃β^T estimates it without bias.

Proof. With 𝛃y=Xβ, the vector 𝛃𝛃PTβ=GTX⊤(Xβ) is the unique solution of Theorem 8.4.2(a) for that y. It satisfies 𝛃𝛃Xb=MXβ=Xβ and Tb=0. The matrix PT is HG of Eq. 8.3.1 for the generalized inverse GT, so Proposition 8.3.3 gives idempotence, the null space N(X) and rows in C(X⊤). Its range lies in N(T). The range has dimension r, and so does N(T), because rank(T)=p−r. So the two coincide. Unbiasedness is Proposition 8.3.3(c).

In words, PT is the projection of Rp onto N(T) along N(X), generally an oblique one. It sends the true 𝛃β to the unique representative of 𝛃β+N(X) that satisfies the side condition. That representative’s coordinates are functions of the mean, so they can be estimated. Their meaning is fixed by T. In the one-way layout, the condition ∑kαk=0 makes μ the unweighted average of the g cell means and αk the deviation of cell k from it. The condition ∑knkαk=0 makes μ the size-weighted average instead. The condition α1=0 makes μ the first cell mean. The data do not choose among these. The analyst chooses, by deciding which question the coefficients should answer.

One side condition deserves special mention, because it is the one many programs impose without saying so.

Proposition 8.4.4 (Minimum norm as a side condition)

Let the columns of the p×(p−r) matrix V0 be a basis of N(X). Then T=V0⊤ satisfies the conditions of Theorem 8.4.2, and the resulting solution 𝛃β^T is the minimum-norm least squares solution X+y.

Proof. C(T⊤)=C(V0)=N(X), which has dimension p−r and meets C(X⊤)=N(X)⊥ only in 0. So (d) holds. The least squares solutions are 𝛃β^T+N(X), and 𝛃β^T∈N(T)=C(V0)⊥=N(X)⊥. For v∈N(X), Pythagoras gives 𝛃𝛃‖β^T+v‖2=‖β^T‖2+‖v‖2, so 𝛃β^T has the smallest norm. By Proposition 6.10.1 this is X+y.

In the one-way layout the null space is spanned by (1,−1,…,−1)⊤, so the minimum-norm solution is the one satisfying μ=α1+⋯+αg. No one would choose this restriction on purpose, and its coefficients have no natural interpretation.

Example (Five side conditions, one fit)

Eighteen simulated observations fall into four groups of sizes 5,3,6,4, with group means 11.111, 15.204, 10.558 and 13.624. In the model E(ykj)=μ+αk, p−r=1, so a side condition is a single nonestimable restriction. The listing solves Eq. 8.4.2 for five of them and computes the minimum-norm solution.

Side condition μ^ α^1 α^2 α^3 α^4
α1=0 11.111 0.000 4.093 -0.553 2.513
α4=0 13.624 -2.513 1.580 -3.066 0.000
∑kαk=0 12.624 -1.513 2.580 -2.066 1.000
∑knkαk=0 12.167 -1.056 3.037 -1.609 1.457
μ=0 0.000 11.111 15.204 10.558 13.624
minimum norm (μ=∑kαk) 10.100 1.012 5.105 0.459 3.525

Every row solves the normal equations. In every row μ^+α^k is the kth group mean, and α^1−α^2 is the same. The value of μ^ is, in turn, the first group mean, the last group mean, the average of the four group means (12.624), the overall mean y¯=12.167, zero, and an uninterpretable 10.100.

Compare a restriction that is estimable, α1=α2. It is not a side condition. Imposing it (by solving the Lagrange system for least squares under a linear restriction) forces the first two group means to be equal. The residual sum of squares rises from 14.665 to 46.078. The fit has changed, which is what testing the hypothesis α1=α2 in Chapter 11 measures.

import numpy as np

sizes = [5, 3, 6, 4]
groups = np.repeat(np.arange(4), sizes)
rng = np.random.default_rng(84)
y = np.array([12.0, 15.0, 11.0, 14.0])[groups] + rng.normal(scale=1.0, size=groups.size)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(groups.size), np.eye(4)[groups]])   # (mu, alpha_1..alpha_4)
A, c = X.T @ X, X.T @ y
n_k = np.array(sizes, dtype=float)
side = {"alpha_1 = 0":         [0, 1, 0, 0, 0],
        "alpha_4 = 0":         [0, 0, 0, 0, 1],
        "sum alpha_k = 0":     [0, 1, 1, 1, 1],
        "sum n_k alpha_k = 0": [0, *n_k],
        "mu = 0":              [1, 0, 0, 0, 0]}
solutions = {}
for name, t in side.items():
    T = np.array([t], dtype=float)
    G = np.linalg.inv(A + T.T @ T)          # (X'X + T'T)^{-1}: a g-inverse of X'X
    b = G @ c
    solutions[name] = b
    print(f"{name:20s} b = {np.round(b, 3)}   T b = {(T @ b)[0]:.1e}")
b_mn = np.linalg.pinv(X) @ y
print(f"{'minimum norm':20s} b = {np.round(b_mn, 3)}",
      f"  mu - sum(alpha) = {b_mn[0] - b_mn[1:].sum():.1e}")
# "alpha_1 = alpha_2" is a restriction on an estimable function: it changes the model
T_bad = np.array([[0, 1, -1, 0, 0]], dtype=float)
K = np.block([[A, T_bad.T], [T_bad, np.zeros((1, 1))]])        # Lagrange (bordered) system
b_bad = np.linalg.lstsq(K, np.append(c, 0.0), rcond=None)[0][:5]
sse_free = np.sum((y - X @ solutions["alpha_1 = 0"]) ** 2)
sse_bad = np.sum((y - X @ b_bad) ** 2)
print(f"SSE without restriction {sse_free:.3f};  with alpha_1 = alpha_2 {sse_bad:.3f}")

Reparameterization and side conditions are two languages for the same choice. Deleting the column of α1 from X and fitting the full-rank remainder gives exactly the solution with α1=0. More generally, deleting a set of columns whose removal leaves a full-rank matrix with the same column space is the side condition that sets those coefficients to zero, and it satisfies Theorem 8.4.2(c) (Exercise (B1)).

8.4.4. What software does

Programs treat a rank-deficient X differently.

  • Code the factor at full rank before fitting. Formula interfaces such as R’s lm() and statsmodels’ formula API expand a factor with g levels into an intercept and g−1 columns, by default the reference coding of Section 8.5. The overparameterized matrix never appears. This is a reparameterization in the sense of Theorem 8.4.1.
  • Drop columns found to be dependent. If the columns supplied are dependent anyway, R’s lm() computes a QR decomposition in which a column found to be (numerically) a combination of earlier ones is moved to the end and left out. Its coefficient is reported as NA. This is the side condition setting that coefficient to zero, and which column is dropped depends on the order of the columns. SAS’s GLM procedure similarly solves the normal equations with a generalized inverse that zeroes the coefficients of dependent columns, and it flags the resulting estimates as not unique.
  • Return the minimum-norm solution. numpy.linalg.lstsq returns X+y silently (it reports the numerical rank only as a second output), and statsmodels’ OLS, whose fit uses the pseudoinverse by default, returns the same vector with a SingularMatrixWarning. This is the side condition of Proposition 8.4.4, and it does not depend on the order of the columns.
  • Pivot greedily. A QR decomposition with column pivoting (Chapter 10, Theorem 10.7.2) chooses at each step the remaining column with the largest component orthogonal to those already chosen, and the columns left at the end are dropped. In Example (Five side conditions, one fit) it takes the intercept, then α3 (the largest group), α1 and α4, and drops α2, the smallest group, a choice that depends on the group sizes.
import scipy.linalg
import statsmodels.api as sm
b_sm = sm.OLS(y, X).fit().params                 # statsmodels default: method="pinv"
b_np = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]      # LAPACK gelsd: minimum-norm solution
_, R, piv = scipy.linalg.qr(X, mode="economic", pivoting=True)
print("statsmodels:", np.round(b_sm, 3))
print("numpy lstsq:", np.round(b_np, 3))
print("pivoted QR column order:", piv, " |R_kk| =", np.round(np.abs(np.diag(R)), 3))

The lesson of this section is that all these outputs are correct, and none is more correct than the others. They are different coordinate systems for one fitted mean vector. Any conclusion that changes when the software changes is a conclusion about a nonestimable function.

Warning. Before interpreting a coefficient from a rank-deficient fit, find out which side condition produced it. A coefficient reported as “the effect of level 3” may be a difference from level 1, a deviation from an average of cell means, a deviation from the overall mean, or, under the minimum-norm convention, a quantity with no interpretation at all.

8.4.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In the one-way model with four groups and 𝛃β=(μ,α1,…,α4)⊤, decide which of the following are side conditions in the sense of Theorem 8.4.2: (i) α2=0; (ii) μ=α3; (iii) α1−α2=0; (iv) α1=0 together with α2=0; (v) 2μ+α1+α2=0.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let X=[X1,X2], where X1 has full column rank and C(X1)=C(X). Show that the least squares fit using X1 alone, padded with zeros for the coefficients of X2, is the solution of the side condition T=[0,I]. Check the conditions of Theorem 8.4.2 directly.

Solution. The padded vector 𝛃(β^1⊤,0⊤)⊤ satisfies 𝛃𝛃X(β^1⊤,0⊤)⊤=X1β^1=My, because C(X1)=C(X). So it solves the normal equations and satisfies Tb=0. For the conditions: rank(T)=p2, and rank(X)=rank(X1)=p1, so rank(T)=p−r. If T⊤c=(0⊤,c⊤)⊤ were in C(X⊤), say 𝛒X1⊤ρ=0 and 𝛒X2⊤ρ=c, then 𝛒ρ⊥C(X1)=C(X)⊇C(X2), so c=0. Condition (e) holds.

Exercise (B2)

For the additive model E(yij)=μ+αi+βj with i=1,2, j=1,2,3 and one observation per cell, take U with rows giving μ+α1+β1, α2−α1, β2−β1 and β3−β1. Compute Z from Eq. 8.4.1, and check that it is the reference-coded model matrix.

Exercise (B3)

Let T satisfy the conditions of Theorem 8.4.2 and have full row rank p−r. Show that the bordered matrix (X⊤XT⊤T0) is nonsingular, that the solution of the bordered system with right-hand side (X⊤y,0) has first block 𝛃β^T and second block 0, and interpret the zero Lagrange multiplier. Relate this to Exercise (B2).

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Replace X by XD for a nonsingular diagonal D (a change of units). Show that the minimum-norm solution for XD, converted back to the original units, is the solution for the side condition V0⊤D−2b=0, which differs in general from V0⊤b=0. Conclude that the minimum-norm convention depends on the units of measurement, while no estimable function does.

Exercise (C2)

Suppose rank(T)=p−r but C(T⊤)∩C(X⊤)≠{0}. Show that for some y the restricted normal equations of Theorem 8.4.2(a) have no solution, and that whenever they have one they have infinitely many.

Solution. By the dimension count, C(X⊤)+C(T⊤) has dimension less than r+(p−r)=p. So N(X)∩N(T)≠{0}, and any solution can be moved along a nonzero vector of that intersection. For existence, the proof of Theorem 8.4.2 shows that a solution exists for every y iff N(X)+N(T)=Rp, which fails because its orthogonal complement C(X⊤)∩C(T⊤) is nonzero. So some coset b0+N(X) misses N(T), and the response y=Xb0 has no solution.

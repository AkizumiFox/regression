2.3  Expectation of quadratic forms

Sums of squares are quadratic forms in the response. The sample variance is (n−1)−1Y⊤(I−n−1J)Y. The residual sum of squares of a regression is Y⊤(I−M)Y, where M is the projection onto the column space of X (Chapter 6). Test statistics are ratios of such forms. Their expectations follow from a short trace argument that needs no distributional assumption.

2.3.1. The basic formula

Theorem 2.3.1 (Mean of a quadratic form)

Let Y be a p×1 random vector with finite second moments, 𝛍E(Y)=μ and Cov(Y)=Σ, and let A be a constant p×p matrix. Then 𝛍𝛍E(Y⊤AY)=tr(AΣ)+μ⊤Aμ.

Proof. A 1×1 matrix equals its trace, and tr(ab⊤)=b⊤a (Theorem 1.6.1), so Y⊤AY=tr(AYY⊤). By Proposition 2.1.1 and Proposition 2.1.2(b), 𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍E(Y⊤AY)=tr(AE(YY⊤))=tr(A(Σ+μμ⊤))=tr(AΣ)+μ⊤Aμ.\qedhere

The formula has two parts. The trace term is what the form picks up from random variation. The second term is the value of the form at the mean, which is what it would be without any randomness. The theorem does not require independence, normality or symmetry of A. Since Y⊤AY=Y⊤AsY with As=12(A+A⊤), and tr(AΣ)=tr(AsΣ) when Σ is symmetric, we may assume A symmetric whenever that is convenient.

Corollary 2.3.2

Under the conditions of Theorem 2.3.1:

  • (a) 𝛍𝛍E[(Y−b)⊤A(Y−b)]=tr(AΣ)+(μ−b)⊤A(μ−b) for any constant b;

  • (b) if Σ=σ2I, then 𝛍𝛍E(Y⊤AY)=σ2tr(A)+μ⊤Aμ;

  • (c) if Σ is positive definite, the squared Mahalanobis distance Eq. 2.2.2 has EΔ2(Y)=p;

  • (d) for random vectors U (p×1) and V (q×1) with finite second moments and a constant p×q matrix A, E(U⊤AV)=tr(ACov(V,U))+E(U)⊤AE(V).

Proof.

  • (a) applies the theorem to Y−b, which has covariance matrix Σ. (b) is immediate. (c) is (a) with 𝛍b=μ and A=Σ−1, so the trace is tr(Ip)=p. For (d), U⊤AV=tr(AVU⊤) and E(VU⊤)=Cov(V,U)+E(V)E(U)⊤.
Example (Sample variance with unequal means and variances)

Let Y1,…,Yn be uncorrelated with means μi and variances σi2, and let S2=(n−1)−1Y⊤CY with C=I−n−1J. Here Σ=diag(σ12,…,σn2), so tr(CΣ)=(1−1/n)∑iσi2, and 𝛍𝛍μ⊤Cμ=∑i(μi−μ¯)2. Therefore E(S2)=σ2―+1n−1∑i=1n(μi−μ¯)2,σ2―=1n∑i=1nσi2. With a common mean, S2 is unbiased for the average variance, even when the variances differ. Differences in the means inflate it. That is exactly why a residual mean square estimates σ2 only when the mean structure is right.

Example (Residual sum of squares under a wrong mean)

Let Cov(Y)=σ2In and 𝛉E(Y)=θ, where 𝛉θ need not lie in C(X). The residual sum of squares is Y⊤(I−M)Y, where M is symmetric and idempotent of rank r=rank(X), so tr(I−M)=n−r (Theorem 1.9.2). By Corollary 2.3.2(b),

𝛉E(RSS)=σ2(n−r)+‖(I−M)θ‖2.
(2.3.1)

If the model is right, 𝛉θ∈C(X), the second term vanishes and RSS/(n−r) is unbiased for σ2. If the model is wrong, the estimate is biased upwards by the squared distance from the true mean to the model space, divided by n−r. For instance, fit a straight line at x=1,…,6 when the true mean is the parabola θi=14(xi−3.5)2 and σ2=1. Then σ2(n−r)=4, 𝛉‖(I−M)θ‖2=2.333, and E(RSS)=6.333. Chapter 19 studies such departures systematically (Theorem 19.1.2).

The theorem uses only two moments, so it holds equally for skewed and heavy-tailed data. The listing checks it for a correlated vector 𝛍Y=μ+BU, where U has independent, centred exponential components, which are strongly skewed. With the matrices in the listing, tr(AΣ)=6.9 and 𝛍𝛍μ⊤Aμ=17.0, so E(Y⊤AY)=23.9. The average over 106 draws is 23.940, with Monte Carlo standard error 0.018. The script also verifies the formula exactly, by enumerating a discrete distribution with eight atoms.

import itertools
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(20260917)

mu = np.array([1.0, -1.0, 2.0])
B = np.array([[1.0, 0.0, 0.0],
              [0.5, 1.0, 0.0],
              [-0.5, 0.8, 0.6]])
Sigma = B @ B.T                                   # Cov(mu + B U) when Cov(U) = I
A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0, 0.0],
              [1.0, 1.0, -1.0],
              [0.0, -1.0, 3.0]])

theory = np.trace(A @ Sigma) + mu @ A @ mu        # Theorem: no normality needed

N = 1_000_000
U = rng.exponential(size=(N, 3)) - 1.0            # skewed, mean 0, variance 1
Y = mu + U @ B.T
Q = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Y, A, Y)             # Y_i' A Y_i for every draw
print(f"theory {theory:.4f}   simulation {Q.mean():.4f} ± {Q.std() / np.sqrt(N):.4f}")

2.3.2. The variance needs fourth moments

The mean of Y⊤AY involves only E(YiYj). Its second moment involves E(YiYjYkYl), so no formula for Var(Y⊤AY) can be written in terms of 𝛍μ and Σ alone. Two random vectors with the same mean and covariance matrix can give the same quadratic form very different variances. The next theorem handles independent components, the case that matters for residual sums of squares under the classical error assumptions. Chapter 4 gives the normal case with a general Σ (Theorem 4.2.3).

Theorem 2.3.3 (Variance of a quadratic form)

Let Y1,…,Yn be independent with means θ1,…,θn and common central moments σ2=E(Yi−θi)2, μ3=E(Yi−θi)3 and μ4=E(Yi−θi)4<∞. Let A be a constant symmetric n×n matrix, and let a=(a11,…,ann)⊤ be its diagonal. Then 𝛉𝛉𝛉Var(Y⊤AY)=(μ4−3σ4)a⊤a+2σ4tr(A2)+4σ2θ⊤A2θ+4μ3θ⊤Aa.

Proof. Write 𝛉𝛆Y=θ+ε, where the εi are independent with mean zero. Then 𝛉𝛉𝛆𝛆𝛆𝛉Y⊤AY=q+ℓ+θ⊤Aθ,q=ε⊤Aε,ℓ=2b⊤ε,b=Aθ, so Var(Y⊤AY)=Var(q)+Var(ℓ)+2Cov(q,ℓ). We compute the three terms.

The linear term. 𝛆Cov(ε)=σ2I, so 𝛉𝛉Var(ℓ)=4σ2b⊤b=4σ2θ⊤A2θ.

The cross term. Since E(ℓ)=0, Cov(q,ℓ)=E(qℓ)=2∑i,j,kaijbkE(εiεjεk). By independence and zero means, E(εiεjεk) vanishes unless i=j=k, when it equals μ3. So 𝛉Cov(q,ℓ)=2μ3∑iaiibi=2μ3θ⊤Aa.

The quadratic term. E(q2)=∑i,j,k,laijaklE(εiεjεkεl). The fourth-order moment is μ4 if all four indices agree. It is σ4 if the indices form two distinct pairs, which happens in three patterns: i=j≠k=l, i=k≠j=l and i=l≠j=k. Otherwise some index occurs exactly once, and the moment is zero. Using the symmetry of A, E(q2)=μ4∑iaii2+σ4[∑i≠kaiiakk+2∑i≠jaij2]=μ4a⊤a+σ4[(trA)2−a⊤a+2tr(A2)−2a⊤a], since ∑i,jaij2=tr(A2). By Theorem 2.3.1, E(q)=σ2trA, and subtracting its square gives Var(q)=(μ4−3σ4)a⊤a+2σ4tr(A2). Adding the three terms proves the formula.

For normal errors, μ3=0 and μ4=3σ4, and the formula collapses to 𝛉𝛉2σ4tr(A2)+4σ2θ⊤A2θ. Both extra terms describe how non-normality leaks into second-order behaviour. The kurtosis term depends on A only through its diagonal. The skewness term needs both a skewed error distribution and a mean that the form does not annihilate. For the residual sum of squares of a correct model, 𝛉𝛉Aθ=(I−M)θ=0, so the skewness term disappears but the kurtosis term remains.

Example (Skewed errors and a misspecified line)

Continue Example (Residual sum of squares under a wrong mean), now with independent centred exponential errors, for which σ2=1, μ3=2 and μ4=9. Theorem 2.3.3 gives Var(RSS)=29.80. Of this, the kurtosis term contributes 16.73 and the skewness term −4.27. Normal errors with the same mean and variance would give 17.33, so the exponential errors multiply the variance by 1.72 even though the mean of RSS is unchanged. Over 106 simulated data sets, the variance of RSS is 30.04. The script also confirms the formula exactly for a two-point error distribution.

se = Q.std() / np.sqrt(N)
vals, probs = np.array([-1.0, 2.0]), np.array([2 / 3, 1 / 3])
exact = 0.0
for idx in itertools.product(range(2), repeat=3):
    u = vals[list(idx)]
    y = mu + B @ u
    exact += np.prod(probs[list(idx)]) * (y @ A @ y)
x = np.arange(1.0, 7.0)                           # n = 6 design points
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])
M = X @ np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T)
A2 = np.eye(6) - M                                # RSS = Y'(I - M)Y for a straight line
theta = 0.25 * (x - 3.5) ** 2                     # true mean is curved: the line is wrong
a = np.diag(A2)

def var_quadform(A, theta, m2, m3, m4):
    """Var(Y'AY) for independent Y_i with means theta_i and common central moments."""
    a = np.diag(A)
    return ((m4 - 3 * m2**2) * a @ a + 2 * m2**2 * np.trace(A @ A)
            + 4 * m2 * theta @ A @ A @ theta + 4 * m3 * theta @ A @ a)

# A2 = I - M for a straight line at x = 1..6; theta = true (curved) mean
mean_rss = np.trace(A2) + theta @ A2 @ theta      # sigma^2 = 1
var_expo = var_quadform(A2, theta, 1.0, 2.0, 9.0) # centred exponential errors
var_norm = var_quadform(A2, theta, 1.0, 0.0, 3.0) # what normal errors would give

E = rng.exponential(size=(N, 6)) - 1.0
R = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", theta + E, A2, theta + E)
print(f"mean  {mean_rss:.3f} vs {R.mean():.3f}")
print(f"var   {var_expo:.3f} vs {R.var():.3f}   (normal-theory value {var_norm:.3f})")

2.3.3. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For random vectors U and V of the same size, show that E‖U−V‖2=trCov(U)+trCov(V)−2trCov(U,V)+‖EU−EV‖2.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let E(Yt)=μ and Cov(Yt,Ys)=σ2ϕ|t−s| for t,s=1,…,n, with |ϕ|<1. Show that the sample variance satisfies E(S2)=σ2[1−2n(n−1)∑k=1n−1(n−k)ϕk]. Is S2 biased up or down when ϕ>0? What happens as n→∞ with ϕ fixed?

Solution. With C=I−n−111⊤ and E(Y)=μ1, CE(Y)=0, so E(Y⊤CY)=tr(CΣ)=tr(Σ)−n−11⊤Σ1. Here tr(Σ)=nσ2, and 1⊤Σ1 sums all entries. There are n diagonal entries and 2(n−k) entries at lag k, so 1⊤Σ1=σ2[n+2∑k=1n−1(n−k)ϕk]. Dividing nσ2−n−11⊤Σ1 by n−1 gives the formula. For ϕ>0 the sum is positive, so S2 is biased downwards: positively correlated observations cluster, and the sample looks less variable than the process is. As n→∞ the sum is O(n), so the bias is O(1/n) and vanishes.

Exercise (B2)

Suppose 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ and Cov(Y)=σ2V. Show that E(RSS)=σ2tr((I−M)V). For the equicorrelated V=(1−ρ)I+ρ11⊤ and a model with 1∈C(X), show that E(RSS)/(n−r)=σ2(1−ρ).

Exercise (B3)

Let Y1,…,Yn be independent and identically distributed with variance σ2 and finite fourth central moment μ4. Use Theorem 2.3.3 to show that Var(S2)=(μ4−3σ4)/n+2σ4/(n−1). Compare with the normal case.

Solution. Take A=C/(n−1) and 𝛉θ=μ1. Then 𝛉Aθ=0, so the last two terms of Theorem 2.3.3 vanish. Every diagonal entry of A is (1−1/n)/(n−1)=1/n, so a⊤a=1/n, and tr(A2)=tr(C)/(n−1)2=1/(n−1). This gives the formula. Normal data have μ4=3σ4 and Var(S2)=2σ4/(n−1). Heavy tails (μ4>3σ4) add a term of the same order 1/n, so the normal-theory variance is wrong even in large samples.

Exercise (B4)

Under the conditions of Theorem 2.3.3 with 𝛉θ=0, show that Cov(Y⊤AY,Y⊤BY)=(μ4−3σ4)a⊤b+2σ4tr(AB), where a and b are the diagonals of the symmetric matrices A and B. Hint: apply the theorem to A+B.

Exercise (B5)

Let Y1,…,Yn be independent N(0,σ2). Among symmetric A with E(Y⊤AY)=σ2, show that Var(Y⊤AY) is minimized by A=n−1I. Hint: tr(A2)≥(trA)2/n.

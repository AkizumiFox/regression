2.2  Linear and affine transformations

Almost every statistic in a linear model is a linear function of the response: least squares coefficients, fitted values, residuals, contrasts. Their means and covariance matrices follow from one theorem.

2.2.1. The transformation rules

Theorem 2.2.1 (Moments of affine transformations)

Let Y be a p×1 random vector with finite second moments, E(Y)=μ and Cov(Y)=Σ. Let A be k×p, B be m×p, and b be k×1, all constant. Then

  • (a) E(AY+b)=Aμ+b;

  • (b) Cov(AY+b)=AΣA⊤;

  • (c) Cov(AY,BY)=AΣB⊤.

More generally, if U and V have finite second moments and A, B, b, c are constants of conformable sizes, then Cov(AU+b,BV+c)=ACov(U,V)B⊤.

Proof.

  • (a) is Proposition 2.1.1(a). For the general statement, the centred vectors are AU+b−E(AU+b)=A(U−EU) and similarly B(V−EV). Hence Cov(AU+b,BV+c)=E[A(U−EU)(V−EV)⊤B⊤]=AE[(U−EU)(V−EV)⊤]B⊤, again by Proposition 2.1.1(a). Parts (c) and (b) are the cases U=V=Y, and B=A in (b).

For a single linear combination, A=a⊤ is a row vector, and (b) and (c) give

Var(a⊤Y)=a⊤Σa=∑i=1p∑j=1paiajσij,Cov(a⊤Y,b⊤Y)=a⊤Σb.
(2.2.1)

Every variance and covariance enters Var(a⊤Y), weighted by the products of the coefficients. Conversely, Σ is determined by the variances of linear combinations, because σij=12[Var(Yi+Yj)−Var(Yi)−Var(Yj)].

Proposition 2.2.2 (Covariance of sums)

Let U,U2,V have finite second moments, with U and U2 of the same size in (a), and U and V of the same size in (b). Then

  • (a) Cov(U+U2,V)=Cov(U,V)+Cov(U2,V);

  • (b) Cov(U+V)=Cov(U)+Cov(U,V)+Cov(V,U)+Cov(V);

  • (c) if Y1,…,Yn are pairwise uncorrelated p×1 vectors and c1,…,cn are constants, then Cov(∑iciYi)=∑ici2Cov(Yi). In particular, if every Cov(Yi)=Σ, then Cov(Y¯)=Σ/n.

Proof.

  • (a) Centre each vector and use linearity of the outer product and of E. (b) Apply (a) in each argument; note that the two cross terms are transposes of each other, not equal.
  • (b) By (a) and (b), applied repeatedly, Cov(∑iciYi)=∑i∑jcicjCov(Yi,Yj), using Cov(cU,V)=cCov(U,V) from Theorem 2.2.1. The terms with i≠j vanish.
Example (Centring a sample)

Let Y be n×1 with Cov(Y)=σ2In, and write J=11⊤. The mean is Y¯=n−11⊤Y, and the vector of deviations is d=(I−n−1J)Y. The matrix I−n−1J is symmetric and idempotent, so Theorem 2.2.1 gives Cov(d)=σ2(I−n−1J),Cov(Y¯,d)=σ2n−11⊤(I−n−1J)=0⊤. The deviations have variances σ2(1−1/n) and are negatively correlated, with correlation −1/(n−1) between each pair, because they are forced to sum to zero. And each deviation is uncorrelated with the mean. Under normality, uncorrelated becomes independent (Example (Sample mean and deviations)), which is the first half of Corollary 4.4.5.

2.2.2. Covariance matrices are nonnegative definite

A variance cannot be negative, and by Eq. 2.2.1 this simple fact constrains the whole matrix. Recall from Definition 1.7.1 that a symmetric Σ is nonnegative definite if a⊤Σa≥0 for all a, and positive definite if in addition a⊤Σa>0 for a≠0.

Theorem 2.2.3 (Covariance matrices)

Let Y have finite second moments, mean μ and covariance matrix Σ.

  • (a) Σ is nonnegative definite.

  • (b) Conversely, every p×p nonnegative definite matrix is the covariance matrix of some random vector.

  • (c) For a constant a, Σa=0 iff a⊤Y=a⊤μ with probability one.

  • (d) Pr(Y−μ∈C(Σ))=1. If S is any subspace with Pr(Y−μ∈S)=1, then C(Σ)⊆S.

In particular, Σ is singular iff some nontrivial linear combination of Y is constant with probability one. Then Y lies, with probability one, in the affine subspace μ+C(Σ) of dimension rank(Σ)<p, and this is the smallest such flat.

Proof.

  • (a) Σ is symmetric, and a⊤Σa=Var(a⊤Y)≥0 by Eq. 2.2.1.

  • (b) Let Σ be nonnegative definite and let Σ1/2 be its symmetric nonnegative definite square root (Theorem 1.7.6). Let Z have independent components with mean 0 and variance 1, for instance independent standard normal variables, so that Cov(Z)=I. Then Cov(Σ1/2Z)=Σ1/2IΣ1/2=Σ.

  • (c) If Σa=0, the random variable a⊤(Y−μ) has mean zero and variance a⊤Σa=0, so it is zero with probability one. Conversely, if a⊤(Y−μ)=0 with probability one, then Σa=E[(Y−μ)(Y−μ)⊤a]=0.

  • (d) Let a1,…,as be a basis of N(Σ). By (c), each event {aj⊤(Y−μ)=0} has probability one, and so does their intersection. On that intersection Y−μ is orthogonal to N(Σ). Because Σ is symmetric, N(Σ) is the orthogonal complement of C(Σ), and so Y−μ∈C(Σ). Now let S be a subspace with Pr(Y−μ∈S)=1. For every a∈S⊥ we have a⊤(Y−μ)=0 with probability one, hence a∈N(Σ)=C(Σ)⊥ by (c). So S⊥⊆C(Σ)⊥, and taking orthogonal complements gives C(Σ)⊆S. The final statements follow, with dim⁡C(Σ)=rank(Σ). For minimality among all flats, suppose Pr(Y∈c+S)=1 for a vector c and a subspace S. Every a∈S⊥ has a⊤(Y−c)=0 with probability one, and taking expectations gives a⊤(μ−c)=0. Hence μ−c∈S, so c+S=μ+S, and the subspace case applies.

A covariance matrix is therefore positive definite exactly when no linear combination of the components is degenerate. Singular covariance matrices are not pathological. They arise whenever the components satisfy an exact linear constraint, as with the deviations of Example (Centring a sample), whose covariance matrix has rank n−1 because 1⊤d=0. The residual vector of a regression is another case: it is orthogonal to every column of X, and when Cov(Y)=σ2I its covariance matrix has rank n−rank(X) (Section 2.2, Chapter 6).

Example (Multinomial counts)

In m independent trials, each outcome falls into one of k categories with probabilities π=(π1,…,πk)⊤, where ∑jπj=1. Let Zt be the indicator vector of trial t, with a single 1 in the position of its category. Then E(Zt)=π, and ZtZt⊤=diag(Zt) because the indicator has only one nonzero entry. So Cov(Zt)=diag(π)−ππ⊤. The count vector Y=∑tZt is a sum of independent vectors, and Proposition 2.2.2(c) gives Σ=Cov(Y)=m(diag(π)−ππ⊤). Because Σ1=m(π−ππ⊤1)=0, the matrix is singular, and Theorem 2.2.3(c) recovers the constraint 1⊤Y=m. For m=20 and π=(0.5,0.3,0.2)⊤ the eigenvalues are 7.762, 4.638 and 0. All 5000 simulated count vectors lie in the plane μ+C(Σ)={y:1⊤y=m}.

Linear maps cannot increase the rank of a covariance matrix, since rank(AΣA⊤)≤rank(Σ) (Exercise (B2)). They can, however, discard degenerate directions. If Y=(Z,Z)⊤ with Var(Z)=1, then Σ has all entries equal to 1 and is singular, while A=(1,0) gives Var(AY)=1>0. By Theorem 2.2.1, the covariance matrix of AY is AΣA⊤. If Σ is positive definite and A has full row rank k≤p, then for b≠0 the vector A⊤b is nonzero and b⊤AΣA⊤b>0. So AΣA⊤ is positive definite. If k>p, then AΣA⊤ has rank at most p and is singular. A vector of more than p linear combinations of p variables always satisfies a linear constraint.

2.2.3. Standardization and Mahalanobis distance

A scalar with mean μ and variance σ2>0 is standardized by (Y−μ)/σ. The vector version must remove correlation as well as scale.

Proposition 2.2.4 (Whitening)

Let Σ=Cov(Y) be positive definite, with E(Y)=μ.

  • (a) Z=Σ−1/2(Y−μ) has E(Z)=0 and Cov(Z)=Ip.

  • (b) A p×p matrix W satisfies WΣW⊤=Ip iff W=QΣ−1/2 for some orthogonal Q. In particular, if Σ=LL⊤ is the Cholesky factorization, W=L−1 is such a matrix.

  • (c) For every W as in (b), ‖W(Y−μ)‖2=(Y−μ)⊤Σ−1(Y−μ).

Proof.

  • (a) By Theorem 2.2.1, Cov(Z)=Σ−1/2ΣΣ−1/2=I, since Σ−1/2 is symmetric and commutes with Σ. (b) If W=QΣ−1/2, then WΣW⊤=QQ⊤=I. Conversely, if WΣW⊤=I, put Q=WΣ1/2. Then QQ⊤=WΣW⊤=I, so the square matrix Q is orthogonal, and W=QΣ−1/2. For the Cholesky factor, L−1Σ(L⊤)−1=L−1LL⊤(L⊤)−1=I. (c) W⊤W=Σ−1/2Q⊤QΣ−1/2=Σ−1.

The quantity in (c),

Δ2(Y)=(Y−μ)⊤Σ−1(Y−μ),
(2.2.2)

is the squared Mahalanobis distance of Y from μ. It measures distance in units of the covariance structure. Its level sets are ellipsoids with axes along the eigenvectors of Σ, with half-lengths proportional to the square roots of the eigenvalues. Whitening maps these ellipsoids to spheres. Part (b) says that whitening is unique only up to a rotation or reflection. Different choices give different coordinates, but the same distances.

Example (Two whitenings)

Take μ=(3,1)⊤ and Σ=(42.42.42.25), so the standard deviations are 2 and 1.5 and the correlation is 0.8. The eigenvalues are 5.680 and 0.570. The symmetric and Cholesky whitening matrices are Σ−1/2=(0.717−0.425−0.4251.027),L−1=(0.5000−0.6671.111). The Cholesky version standardizes Y1 alone and then removes from Y2 its linear dependence on Y1, in the spirit of Gram–Schmidt. The symmetric version treats both coordinates alike and moves points as little as possible (Exercise (B3)). Figure 2.2.1 applies both to 400 simulated points. The two whitened clouds differ by a rotation through 22.5∘, and every point keeps its Mahalanobis distance.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)

mu = np.array([3.0, 1.0])
Sigma = np.array([[4.0, 2.4],
                  [2.4, 2.25]])                   # correlation 2.4 / (2 * 1.5) = 0.8

lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(Sigma)                    # spectral decomposition
W_sym = U @ np.diag(lam ** -0.5) @ U.T            # Sigma^{-1/2}
L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)                     # Sigma = L L', L lower triangular
W_chol = np.linalg.inv(L)                         # L^{-1}

n = 400
Y = mu + rng.standard_normal((n, 2)) @ L.T        # any draws with Cov = Sigma will do
Z_sym = (Y - mu) @ W_sym.T
Z_chol = (Y - mu) @ W_chol.T

Q = W_chol @ np.linalg.inv(W_sym)                 # L^{-1} Sigma^{1/2}
print("Q'Q =", np.round(Q.T @ Q, 12))

Whitening runs in both directions. If Cov(Z)=I, then μ+Σ1/2Z, or μ+LZ, has mean μ and covariance Σ. This is how correlated vectors are simulated, and with Z standard normal it is how Chapter 3 constructs Np(μ,Σ). The Mahalanobis distance then appears in the exponent of the density (Theorem 3.2.2). When Σ is singular, no matrix whitens all of Y, since a degenerate combination cannot be scaled to variance one. The best one can do is whiten within the support μ+C(Σ) using the Moore–Penrose inverse square root (Exercise (B4)).

2.2.4. Second moments in the linear model

Here is how the rules of this section enter regression. Suppose Y=Xβ+ε with X an n×p constant matrix of rank p, E(ε)=0 and Cov(ε)=σ2In. Adding the constant Xβ changes the mean but not the covariance, so E(Y)=Xβ and Cov(Y)=σ2In. The least squares estimator β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤Y is AY with A=(X⊤X)−1X⊤, so E(β^)=(X⊤X)−1X⊤Xβ=β,Cov(β^)=σ2(X⊤X)−1X⊤X(X⊤X)−1=σ2(X⊤X)−1. No distributional assumption was used, and none is needed for the Gauss–Markov theorem (Theorem 7.2.1). Fitted values and residuals are also linear in Y, and their covariance matrices are computed the same way in Chapter 5 and Chapter 6. If instead Cov(ε)=σ2V with V positive definite, then V−1/2Y=V−1/2Xβ+V−1/2ε is a model with Cov(V−1/2ε)=σ2I. Whitening the data turns correlated errors back into the standard case. This is generalized least squares, which Chapter 6 and Chapter 31 develop.

2.2.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let Cov(Y)=σ2I3. Find the covariance and correlation matrices of the successive differences (Y2−Y1,Y3−Y2)⊤, and of (Y2−Y1,Y3−Y2,Y3−Y1)⊤. Which is singular, and which linear combination is degenerate?

Exercise (A2)

For p=2 and Σ=LL⊤, show that the second component of L−1(Y−μ) is a multiple of Y2−μ2−(σ12/σ11)(Y1−μ1). Explain the connection with Theorem 2.5.2.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let ε0,…,εn be uncorrelated with variance σ2, and let Yt=εt+θεt−1 for t=1,…,n. Find Cov(Y), and show that it is positive definite for every real θ, including θ=±1.

Solution. Y=Aε, where A is n×(n+1) with θ in column t and 1 in column t+1 of row t (columns indexed by ε0,…,εn). So Cov(Y)=σ2AA⊤ is tridiagonal, with σ2(1+θ2) on the diagonal and σ2θ next to it. The last n columns of A form a lower bidiagonal matrix with unit diagonal, which is nonsingular. So A has full row rank n, and AA⊤ is positive definite (Section 2.2) whatever the value of θ.

Exercise (B2)

Show that C(AΣA⊤)=C(AΣ) for every nonnegative definite Σ and conformable A. Deduce that rankCov(AY)≤rankCov(Y).

Exercise (B3)

Let Σ be positive definite. Among all W with WΣW⊤=I, show that E‖W(Y−μ)−(Y−μ)‖2 is minimized uniquely by W=Σ−1/2. Hint: use Proposition 2.2.4(b) and show that tr(QΣ1/2)≤tr(Σ1/2) for orthogonal Q.

Solution. Write W=QΣ−1/2. By Corollary 2.3.2(a) with A=(W−I)⊤(W−I), E‖(W−I)(Y−μ)‖2=tr((W−I)Σ(W−I)⊤)=tr(WΣW⊤)−2tr(WΣ)+tr(Σ)=p+tr(Σ)−2tr(QΣ1/2). Let Σ1/2=ΓΛ1/2Γ⊤ and G=Γ⊤QΓ, which is orthogonal, so each |gii|≤1. Then tr(QΣ1/2)=tr(GΛ1/2)=∑igiiλi1/2≤∑iλi1/2. Since every λi>0, equality forces every gii=1. An orthogonal matrix with unit diagonal is I, because each column has norm one. So G=I, which means Q=I.

Exercise (B4)

Let Σ=Cov(Y) have rank r, with spectral decomposition Σ=ΓrΛrΓr⊤, where Γr⊤Γr=Ir and Λr holds the positive eigenvalues. Show that Z=Λr−1/2Γr⊤(Y−μ) has Cov(Z)=Ir and that Y=μ+ΓrΛr1/2Z with probability one. Show that (Y−μ)⊤Σ+(Y−μ)=‖Z‖2, where Σ+ is the Moore–Penrose inverse, and that its expectation is r.

Solution. By Theorem 2.2.1, Cov(Z)=Λr−1/2Γr⊤ΓrΛrΓr⊤ΓrΛr−1/2=Ir. By Theorem 2.2.3(d), Y−μ∈C(Σ)=C(Γr) with probability one, and on that event Y−μ=ΓrΓr⊤(Y−μ)=ΓrΛr1/2Z. The Moore–Penrose inverse is Σ+=ΓrΛr−1Γr⊤, so (Y−μ)⊤Σ+(Y−μ)=‖Λr−1/2Γr⊤(Y−μ)‖2=‖Z‖2, with expectation trCov(Z)=r.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In Y=Xβ+ε with X of full column rank, let Cov(ε)=σ2V with V positive definite. Find Cov(β^) for β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤Y and Cov(β~) for β~=(X⊤V−1X)−1X⊤V−1Y. Show that Cov(β^)−Cov(β~) is nonnegative definite. Hint: show that β^−β~ and β~ are uncorrelated.

Solution. By Theorem 2.2.1, Cov(β^)=σ2(X⊤X)−1X⊤VX(X⊤X)−1,Cov(β~)=σ2(X⊤V−1X)−1. Write β^−β~=DY with D=(X⊤X)−1X⊤−(X⊤V−1X)−1X⊤V−1, and note DX=I−I=0. Then Cov(β^−β~,β~)=σ2DVV−1X(X⊤V−1X)−1=0, so by Proposition 2.2.2(b), Cov(β^)=Cov(β~)+Cov(β^−β~), and the last term is nonnegative definite.

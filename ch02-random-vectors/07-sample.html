2.7  Sample moments and their computation

The population quantities of this chapter have sample analogues, and the operator rules give their expectations at once. Let y1,…,yn be p×1 observations, and stack them as the rows of the n×p data matrix Y=[y1,…,yn]⊤. The script letter keeps the data matrix apart from a single random vector Y. The rows keep the lowercase yi of observed data, but in Proposition 2.7.1 they are treated as random. With C=In−n−111⊤, the sample mean vector and the sample covariance matrix are y¯=1n∑i=1nyi=1nY⊤1,S=1n−1∑i=1n(yi−y¯)(yi−y¯)⊤=1n−1Y⊤CY. The second form of S holds because CY is the data matrix with its column means subtracted, and C is symmetric and idempotent. The sample correlation matrix is R^=D^−1SD^−1, where D^ holds the sample standard deviations.

Proposition 2.7.1 (Moments of sample moments)

Suppose the rows yi are pairwise uncorrelated random vectors with common mean 𝛍μ and common covariance matrix Σ. Then

  • (a) 𝛍E(y¯)=μ and Cov(y¯)=Σ/n;

  • (b) E(S)=Σ;

  • (c) S is nonnegative definite, and rank(S)≤min(p,n−1) for every realization.

Proof.

  • (a) is Proposition 2.2.2(c) with ci=1/n. For (b), let uj denote the jth column of Y, the n observations of variable j. The (j,k) entry of Y⊤CY is the bilinear form uj⊤Cuk. Because different rows are uncorrelated, Cov(uk,uj)=σkjIn, and E(uj)=μj1. By Corollary 2.3.2(d), E(uj⊤Cuk)=σkjtr(C)+μjμk1⊤C1=(n−1)σjk, since tr(C)=n−1 and C1=0. (c) For any a, a⊤Sa=‖CYa‖2/(n−1)≥0. The rank of Y⊤CY=(CY)⊤(CY) equals rank(CY), which is at most rank(C)=n−1 and at most p.

The divisor n−1 is the trace of C, the dimension of the space the centred data live in, exactly as n−r is for regression residuals in Example (Residual sum of squares under a wrong mean). Part (c) has a practical side. With n≤p, as in genomics or finance, the sample covariance matrix is always singular, even when Σ is positive definite. It has a linear combination with zero sample variance whether or not one exists in the population. Chapter 28 returns to this.

Computation. In practice C is never formed: one subtracts column means. The eigenvalues of S are the squared singular values of CY/n−1 (Theorem 1.8.1), which can be computed without forming a cross-product matrix (Chapter 10).

Example (Macroeconomic series)

Longley’s data give n=16 annual observations (1947–1962) on p=7 US series: total employment, the GNP deflator, GNP, unemployment, armed forces, population and the year. The eigenvalues of S run from 9.94×109 down to 1.07×10−2, a ratio of 9.3×1011. That spread says little about the structure of the data. It mostly reflects units: the standard deviation of GNP is 99395, that of the deflator 10.79.

The correlation matrix removes the units (Proposition 2.4.1(d)). Its eigenvalues sum to p=7. The largest is 5.533, so one direction carries about 79% of the standardized variation, and the smallest is 2.59×10−4. By Theorem 1.7.7, the unit vector v belonging to the smallest eigenvalue minimizes the sample variance of v⊤z over standardized combinations. Its two largest weights are 0.681 on year and −0.674 on GNP, and the combination has standard deviation 0.016, against 1 for each standardized variable. In these sixteen years GNP is almost an exact linear function of time (their correlation is 0.995). This is the sample version of Theorem 2.2.3(c): a nearly zero eigenvalue marks a nearly constant linear combination. For regression it signals collinearity, which Chapter 26 studies. Finally, the first 5 rows alone give a sample covariance matrix of rank 4, as Proposition 2.7.1(c) requires.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.longley.load_pandas().data
names = list(data.columns)

Y = data.to_numpy(dtype=float)                    # n x p data matrix, one row per year
n, p = Y.shape
C = np.eye(n) - np.ones((n, n)) / n               # centring matrix I - J/n
S = Y.T @ C @ Y / (n - 1)                         # sample covariance matrix
d = np.sqrt(np.diag(S))
R = S / np.outer(d, d)                            # D^{-1} S D^{-1}, D = diag(d)

lam_S = np.linalg.eigvalsh(S)
lam_R, V = np.linalg.eigh(R)                      # ascending eigenvalues
print("eigenvalues of S:", lam_S)
print("eigenvalues of R:", lam_R)
v = V[:, 0]                                       # direction of least variance
Z = (Y - Y.mean(0)) / d                           # standardized variables
print("sample variance of Z v:", np.var(Z @ v, ddof=1))

2.7.1. Exercises

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Suppose the rows of Y are observed in time order, with common mean 𝛍μ and covariance Σ, and that neighbouring rows are correlated: Cov(yi,yi+1)=ρΣ, while Cov(yi,yj)=0 for |i−j|≥2. Show that E(S)=(1−2ρ/n)Σ and Cov(y¯)=n−2[n+2(n−1)ρ]Σ. Which of the two effects disappears as n→∞?

Solution. As in the proof of Proposition 2.7.1, Cov(uk,uj)=σkjT, where T is the n×n tridiagonal matrix with ones on the diagonal and ρ next to it. Since C1=0, Corollary 2.3.2(d) gives E(uj⊤Cuk)=σkjtr(CT)=σkj[tr(T)−n−11⊤T1]. Here tr(T)=n and 1⊤T1=n+2(n−1)ρ, so the bracket is (n−1)−2(n−1)ρ/n, and dividing by n−1 gives E(S)=(1−2ρ/n)Σ. For the mean, y¯=n−1∑iyi and Proposition 2.2.2 gives n−2 times the sum of all Cov(yi,yj): n diagonal terms Σ and 2(n−1) neighbouring terms ρΣ. The bias of S is O(1/n) and disappears, but nCov(y¯)→(1+2ρ)Σ, not Σ. For ρ>0 the naive standard error sjj/n stays too small by a fixed factor however large the sample.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

  • (a) With n=2, show that S=12dd⊤ with d=y1−y2. Deduce that rank(S)=1 whenever y1≠y2, and that every sample correlation is ±1, whatever Σ is.

  • (b) With n=3 and p=3, show that det(S)=0, hence that the three sample correlations satisfy 1+2r^12r^13r^23−r^122−r^132−r^232=0 when the sample variances are positive. Conclude that r^23 is always one of the two endpoints of the interval allowed by Proposition 2.4.2, never an interior point.

  • (c) What does this say about reading a sample correlation matrix computed from few observations, and about the nearly singular R^ of Example (Macroeconomic series)?

Solution.

  • (a) Here y¯=12(y1+y2), so y1−y¯=12d and y2−y¯=−12d. Summing the two outer products and dividing by n−1=1 gives S=12dd⊤, of rank one. Then sjk=djdk/2 and r^jk=djdk/dj2dk2=sign(djdk), provided dj,dk≠0. The two observations determine one direction, and every pair of coordinates moves along it.

  • (b) By Proposition 2.7.1(c), rank(S)≤n−1=2<3, so det(S)=0. Writing S=D^R^D^ with D^ the diagonal matrix of sample standard deviations, which is nonsingular when all sample variances are positive, gives det(R^)=0. Expanding the 3×3 determinant gives the stated identity, which is a quadratic in r^23 with roots r^12r^13±(1−r^122)(1−r^132), the two endpoints in Proposition 2.4.2. A population correlation matrix hits these endpoints only when one variable is an exact linear function of the other two; three observations force it.

  • (c) With n≤p the sample correlation matrix is singular whatever the population is, so its smallest eigenvalues and any statement derived from them describe the sample size as much as the data. Longley’s R^ has n=16 and p=7, so its near singularity is not forced by the rank bound; it is a genuine feature of the series.

Exercise (C2)

Let S be positive definite and let λmin be its smallest eigenvalue.

  • (a) Show that λmin=min{a⊤Sa:‖a‖=1} (Theorem 1.7.7), while 1/(S−1)jj=min{a⊤Sa:aj=1} (Theorem 1.6.4).

  • (b) Deduce that λmin≤1/(S−1)jj for every j, with equality iff ej is an eigenvector of S belonging to λmin.

  • (c) For centred data, a⊤Sa=‖CYa‖2/(n−1), so 1/(S−1)jj is the sample variance, with divisor n−1, of the residuals of the least squares regression of variable j on the other p−1 variables, while λmin minimizes over all normalized combinations. Using the smallest eigenvalue 2.59×10−4 of the Longley correlation matrix in Example (Macroeconomic series), what can you say about how well any one standardized series is predicted by the other six?

Solution.

  • (a) The first statement is the Rayleigh quotient characterization. For the second, order the variables so that j=1 and partition a=(1,b⊤)⊤. Then a⊤Sa=s11+2b⊤S21+b⊤S22b, a positive definite quadratic in b, minimized at b=−S22−1S21 with value the Schur complement s11−S12S22−1S21, which by Theorem 1.6.4 is 1/(S−1)11.

  • (b) Any a with aj=1 has ‖a‖≥1, so a⊤Sa≥λmin‖a‖2≥λmin; minimizing over such a gives the inequality. Equality forces a minimizer with ‖a‖=1 and aj=1, hence a=ej, and ej attains the Rayleigh minimum only if it is an eigenvector for λmin. Conversely, if it is, both sides equal λmin.

  • (c) The residual sample variance of any one standardized series regressed on the other six is at least 2.59×10−4, so by Proposition 2.5.3 its multiple correlation with the others satisfies 1−R2≥2.59×10−4. Collinearity as measured by the smallest eigenvalue is therefore always at least as severe as collinearity measured one variable at a time: the direction of least variance need not be close to any coordinate direction, and in Longley’s data it is a contrast between year and GNP rather than a single series.

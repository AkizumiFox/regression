2.5  Moments of partitioned random vectors

Regression with random regressors studies a response vector Y (p×1) together with a regressor vector X (q×1) observed on the same unit. In this section X is a random vector, not a model matrix. We stack the two into one vector and read off what the blocks of its moments mean.

2.5.1. Blocks of the mean and covariance

Theorem 2.5.1 (Partitioned moments)

Let W=(Y⊤,X⊤)⊤ have finite second moments, and write 𝛍𝛍E(W)=(μYμX),Cov(W)=(ΣYYΣYXΣXYΣXX).

  • (a) 𝛍μY=E(Y), 𝛍μX=E(X), ΣYY=Cov(Y), ΣXX=Cov(X), ΣYX=Cov(Y,X) is p×q, and ΣXY=ΣYX⊤.

  • (b) For constants A, B, c of conformable sizes, Cov(AY,BX)=AΣYXB⊤, and Cov(AY+BX+c)=AΣYYA⊤+AΣYXB⊤+BΣXYA⊤+BΣXXB⊤.

  • (c) C(ΣXY)⊆C(ΣXX) and C(ΣYX)⊆C(ΣYY). Equivalently, for every generalized inverse ΣXX−, ΣXXΣXX−ΣXY=ΣXY,ΣYXΣXX−ΣXX=ΣYX, and similarly with the roles of Y and X exchanged.

Proof.

  • (a) The outer product (W−EW)(W−EW)⊤ has blocks 𝛍𝛍(Y−μY)(Y−μY)⊤, 𝛍𝛍(Y−μY)(X−μX)⊤, its transpose, and 𝛍𝛍(X−μX)(X−μX)⊤. Take expectations blockwise. (b) AY+BX+c=[A, B]W+c, and AY=[A, 0]W, BX=[0, B]W. Apply Theorem 2.2.1 and multiply out the blocks.

  • (b) Let a∈N(ΣXX). By Theorem 2.2.3(c), a⊤X is constant with probability one, so its covariance with Y vanishes: ΣYXa=Cov(Y,a⊤X)=0. Thus N(ΣXX)⊆N(ΣYX). Taking orthogonal complements, and using N(B)⊥=C(B⊤) and the symmetry of ΣXX, gives C(ΣXY)⊆C(ΣXX). The same argument with Y and X exchanged gives the second inclusion. A column inclusion C(ΣXY)⊆C(ΣXX) means ΣXY=ΣXXK⊤ for some K. Then ΣXXΣXX−ΣXY=ΣXXΣXX−ΣXXK⊤=ΣXY. Conversely, that identity exhibits every column of ΣXY as a combination of columns of ΣXX (Theorem 1.4.1). Transposing gives ΣYX=KΣXX and the second identity.

Part (c) looks technical, but it removes every difficulty with singular ΣXX in what follows. A direction in which X does not vary cannot covary with anything.

2.5.2. Linear prediction and residual covariance

The most important use of the blocks is to split Y into a part explained linearly by X and a part uncorrelated with X.

Theorem 2.5.2 (Best linear predictor)

In the setting of Theorem 2.5.1, fix a generalized inverse ΣXX− and put 𝛍𝛍B∗=ΣYXΣXX−,E=Y−μY−B∗(X−μX),ΣYY⋅X=ΣYY−ΣYXΣXX−ΣXY.

  • (a) E(E)=0 and Cov(E,X)=0.

  • (b) Cov(E)=ΣYY⋅X. This matrix is nonnegative definite and does not depend on the choice of generalized inverse. Neither does 𝛍B∗(X−μX), with probability one.

  • (c) For every constant p×q matrix B and p×1 vector c, E[(Y−c−BX)(Y−c−BX)⊤]=ΣYY⋅X+(B−B∗)ΣXX(B−B∗)⊤+dd⊤, where 𝛍𝛍d=μY−c−BμX. So the mean squared error matrix is never smaller, in the nonnegative definite ordering, than ΣYY⋅X. Equality holds iff 𝛍𝛍c=μY−BμX and BΣXX=ΣYX.

Proof. By Theorem 2.5.1(c), write ΣYX=KΣXX. Then B∗ΣXX=KΣXXΣXX−ΣXX=KΣXX=ΣYX.

  • (a) The mean is clear. By Theorem 2.5.1(b), Cov(E,X)=ΣYX−B∗ΣXX=0.

  • (b) Again by Theorem 2.5.1(b), Cov(E)=ΣYY−B∗ΣXY−ΣYXB∗⊤+B∗ΣXXB∗⊤. Using B∗ΣXX=ΣYX, the last term is ΣYXB∗⊤, which cancels the third, so Cov(E)=ΣYY−B∗ΣXY=ΣYY⋅X. Next, ΣYXΣXX−ΣXY=KΣXXΣXX−ΣXXK⊤=KΣXXK⊤, which involves no generalized inverse. A covariance matrix is nonnegative definite. Finally, by Theorem 2.2.3(d), 𝛍X−μX=ΣXXv for some random v, with probability one, and then 𝛍B∗(X−μX)=ΣYXv for every choice of generalized inverse.

  • (c) Decompose 𝛍Y−c−BX=E+(B∗−B)(X−μX)+d. The three pieces are, respectively, mean zero, mean zero and constant, and the first two are uncorrelated by (a). So the cross products have zero expectation, and the expected outer product is Cov(E)+(B∗−B)ΣXX(B∗−B)⊤+dd⊤. Both added matrices are nonnegative definite. They vanish iff d=0 and (B−B∗)ΣXX1/2=0, and the latter is equivalent to (B−B∗)ΣXX=0, that is, to BΣXX=ΣYX.

The affine function 𝛍𝛍μY+B∗(X−μX) is the best linear predictor of Y from X. Because the ordering in (c) is the nonnegative definite ordering, it is simultaneously best for every linear combination a⊤Y and for the total squared error. The matrix ΣYY⋅X is the partial covariance matrix of Y given X. When ΣXX is nonsingular, B∗=ΣYXΣXX−1 holds the population regression coefficients, and ΣYY⋅X is a Schur complement. Two consequences follow from Chapter 1. If Cov(W) is positive definite, the YY block of its inverse is ΣYY⋅X−1 (Theorem 1.6.4). And detCov(W)=det(ΣXX)det(ΣYY⋅X) (Theorem 1.6.3), so the generalized variance factorizes into the part of X and the part of Y that X leaves unexplained.

Nothing here says that E(Y∣X) is linear. The best linear predictor is best among affine functions, whatever the joint distribution. For jointly normal vectors, the conditional mean happens to be this affine function, and ΣYY⋅X is the conditional covariance matrix (Theorem 3.5.2). The partial correlation of Definition 3.6.1 is the correlation computed from ΣYY⋅X. Chapter 6 shows that least squares estimates B∗ in large samples, whether or not the regression function is linear. It also recovers the predictor as an orthogonal projection in the space of square-integrable random variables. For scalar Y, the coefficient vector used there is B∗⊤.

Proposition 2.5.3 (Multiple correlation)

Let Y be scalar with σYY=Var(Y)>0, let ΣXX be positive definite, and write 𝛔σXY=Cov(X,Y). Over all a≠0, 𝛔𝛔maxacorr(Y,a⊤X)2=ρY⋅X2=σXY⊤ΣXX−1σXYσYY, attained at 𝛔a=ΣXX−1σXY, the coefficients of the best linear predictor (if 𝛔σXY=0, every a attains the maximum 0). Moreover 1−ρY⋅X2=σYY⋅X/σYY.

Proof. 𝛔corr(Y,a⊤X)2=(a⊤σXY)2/(σYYa⊤ΣXXa). Write 𝛔𝛔a⊤σXY=(ΣXX1/2a)⊤(ΣXX−1/2σXY). The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives 𝛔𝛔𝛔(a⊤σXY)2≤(a⊤ΣXXa)(σXY⊤ΣXX−1σXY), with equality iff ΣXX1/2a is proportional to 𝛔ΣXX−1/2σXY. Dividing gives the bound and the maximizer. The last identity is the definition of 𝛔𝛔σYY⋅X=σYY−σXY⊤ΣXX−1σXY, divided by σYY.

ρY⋅X2 is the population version of R2: the fraction of the variance of Y that the best linear predictor accounts for. Chapter 14 studies its sample version.

2.5.3. Conditioning: total expectation and total covariance

When the joint distribution is available, conditioning gives another decomposition of the covariance matrix. Conditional expectations and covariances are taken entry by entry, as in Definition 2.1.1, and Cov(Y∣X)=E[(Y−E(Y∣X))(Y−E(Y∣X))⊤|X].

Proposition 2.5.4 (Laws of total expectation and covariance)

If Y has finite second moments, then E(Y)=E[E(Y∣X)] and Cov(Y)=E[Cov(Y∣X)]+Cov(E(Y∣X)).

Proof. The first identity is the tower property of conditional expectation, applied to each component. For the second, let m(X)=E(Y∣X) and write 𝛍𝛍Y−μ=(Y−m(X))+(m(X)−μ). The outer product has two cross terms. The conditional expectation of 𝛍(Y−m(X))(m(X)−μ)⊤ given X is 𝛍[E(Y∣X)−m(X)](m(X)−μ)⊤=0, so its expectation is zero, and likewise for the other. The expectation of (Y−m)(Y−m)⊤ is E[Cov(Y∣X)] by the tower property, and that of 𝛍𝛍(m−μ)(m−μ)⊤ is Cov(m(X)) because 𝛍Em(X)=μ.

Example (A shared random effect)

Measurements Y1,…,Yk on the same subject share a subject effect: 𝛆Y=α1+ε, where α has variance τ2, 𝛆Cov(ε)=σ2Ik, and 𝛆ε is independent of α with mean zero. Given α, the mean is α1 and the covariance is σ2I. By Proposition 2.5.4, Cov(Y)=σ2Ik+τ211⊤, so every pair of measurements has correlation τ2/(τ2+σ2). Independent errors combined with a shared random component produce exactly this equicorrelation structure. It is the starting point of Chapter 32. Ordinary least squares ignores it, with consequences that Chapter 33 examines (Proposition 33.2.1).

2.5.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For scalar Y and X with Var(X)>0, show that the best linear predictor has slope σYX/σXX, and that its mean squared error is σYY(1−ρ2).

Exercise (A2)

Let X be uniform on (−1,1) and Y=X2+ε, with ε independent of X, mean 0 and variance σ2. Find the best linear predictor of Y from X and its mean squared error, and compare with the mean squared error of E(Y∣X).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let (Y1,Y2,Y3) have correlation matrix R. Compute the partial covariance matrix of (Y2,Y3) given Y1 from Theorem 2.5.2, and show that the corresponding correlation is ρ23−ρ12ρ13(1−ρ122)(1−ρ132).

Exercise (B2)

Let X=(X1,X1)⊤ with Var(X1)=1, and let Cov(Y,X1)=c. Find two different generalized inverses of ΣXX that give different B∗, and show that both give the same predictor 𝛍B∗(X−μX).

Exercise (B3)

A random label K takes values 1,…,g with probabilities πk. Given K=k, Y has mean mk and covariance Σk. Show that Cov(Y)=∑kπkΣk+∑kπk(mk−m¯)(mk−m¯)⊤, where m¯=∑kπkmk. Interpret the two terms as within-group and between-group covariance.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let N be the symmetric partitioned matrix N=(ΣYYΣYXΣXYΣXX). Show that N is nonnegative definite iff ΣXX is nonnegative definite, C(ΣXY)⊆C(ΣXX), and ΣYY−ΣYXΣXX−ΣXY is nonnegative definite for some generalized inverse ΣXX− (equivalently, given the column-space condition, for every one). Show by a 2×2 example that the column-space condition cannot be dropped.

Solution. If the matrix is nonnegative definite, it is the covariance matrix of some W=(Y⊤,X⊤)⊤ (Theorem 2.2.3(b)). Then ΣXX is a covariance matrix, the column condition is Theorem 2.5.1(c), and the Schur complement is Cov(E) by Theorem 2.5.2(b). Conversely, the column condition gives ΣYX=KΣXX, and then ΣYXΣXX−ΣXY=KΣXXK⊤. With the nonsingular T=(I−K0I), T(ΣYYΣYXΣXYΣXX)T⊤=(ΣYY−KΣXXK⊤00ΣXX), which is nonnegative definite. So is the original matrix, since a⊤Na=b⊤(TNT⊤)b with b=(T⊤)−1a. For the example, take (1110). Here ΣXX=0 is nonnegative definite, and with the generalized inverse 0 the Schur complement is 1≥0. But the determinant is −1, so the matrix is not nonnegative definite. The column condition fails, because C(1)⊈C(0). Without that condition the Schur complement also depends on the choice: every scalar t is a generalized inverse of 0, and the Schur complement is then 1−1⋅t⋅1=1−t, which is negative for t>1. Under the column condition, ΣYXΣXX−ΣXY=KΣXXK⊤ for every choice, as shown above.

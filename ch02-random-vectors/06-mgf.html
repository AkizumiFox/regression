2.6  Moment generating functions and independence

Means and covariance matrices summarize a distribution. They do not determine it, and they cannot certify independence. For that we need a transform of the whole distribution. This section collects the facts that Chapter 3 and Chapter 4 use.

Definition 2.6.1 (Moment generating function)

The moment generating function of a p×1 random vector Y is MY(t)=Eexp⁡(t⊤Y),t∈Rp, where the expectation may be +∞. We say that MY exists if it is finite for all t in some neighbourhood {‖t‖<h} of the origin, h>0.

Theorem 2.6.1 (Properties of moment generating functions)

Suppose MY exists.

  • (a) Uniqueness. If MV also exists and MY=MV on a neighbourhood of 0, then Y and V have the same distribution.

  • (b) Moments. All moments of Y are finite. The gradient of MY at 0 is E(Y), and its Hessian at 0 is E(YY⊤). The Hessian of log⁡MY at 0 is Cov(Y).

  • (c) Affine maps. For a constant k×p matrix A and k×1 vector b, MAY+b(s)=exp⁡(s⊤b)MY(A⊤s).

  • (d) Independence. Partition Y=(Y1⊤,Y2⊤)⊤ and t=(t1⊤,t2⊤)⊤ conformably. Then Y1 and Y2 are independent iff MY(t)=MY1(t1)MY2(t2) for all t in a neighbourhood of 0.

Proof. For (a), the scalar case is a theorem of analysis: a moment generating function that is finite near the origin extends analytically, which pins down the characteristic function and hence the distribution (Billingsley 1995). For vectors, fix a. For small |s|, Ma⊤Y(s)=MY(sa)=MV(sa)=Ma⊤V(s), so a⊤Y and a⊤V have the same distribution by the scalar case, and Proposition 2.6.2 below finishes the argument. For (b), finiteness near 0 implies Eexp⁡(h′|Yi|)<∞ for some h′>0 and each i, so every polynomial in the components is integrable and differentiation under the expectation is justified. Then ∂M/∂ti=E(Yiet⊤Y) and ∂2M/∂ti∂tj=E(YiYjet⊤Y). At t=0 these are E(Yi) and E(YiYj). For K=log⁡M, the chain rule gives ∇2K=M−1∇2M−M−2(∇M)(∇M)⊤, which at 0, where M=1, is 𝛍𝛍E(YY⊤)−μμ⊤=Cov(Y). For (c), Eexp⁡(s⊤(AY+b))=es⊤bEexp⁡((A⊤s)⊤Y). For (d), independence gives E(et1⊤Y1et2⊤Y2)=E(et1⊤Y1)E(et2⊤Y2). Conversely, let V1 and V2 be independent with the distributions of Y1 and Y2. The vector (V1⊤,V2⊤)⊤ has moment generating function MY1(t1)MY2(t2), which by hypothesis equals MY near 0. By (a) it has the same joint distribution as Y, so Y1 and Y2 are independent.

The next fact explains why Chapter 3 can define the multivariate normal through linear combinations.

Proposition 2.6.2 (Distributions are determined by linear combinations)

If a⊤Y and a⊤V have the same distribution for every a∈Rp, then Y and V have the same distribution.

Proof. The characteristic function ϕY(t)=Eexp⁡(it⊤Y) exists for every random vector and determines its distribution (Billingsley 1995). Since ϕY(t)=ϕt⊤Y(1)=ϕt⊤V(1)=ϕV(t) for every t, the two distributions coincide.

If Y1 and Y2 are independent, so are g(Y1) and h(Y2) for measurable g and h. Independence of statistics is usually proved this way. For normal vectors, Theorem 3.4.1 reduces the independence of two blocks to a zero cross-covariance, and Theorem 4.4.1 and Theorem 4.4.2 build on that.

Independence of several vectors means that the joint distribution factors into all the marginals, not merely that each pair is independent.

Example (Pairwise but not mutually independent)

Let S1 and S2 be independent, each equal to ±1 with probability 12, and put S3=S1S2. Each Sj is ±1 with probability 12. Each pair is independent: for instance, Pr(S1=s,S3=u)=Pr(S1=s,S2=su)=14 for all signs s,u. So Cov(S)=I3. But the three are not mutually independent, since S3 is a function of the other two. A third-order moment shows the failure directly: E(S1S2S3)=1, whereas mutual independence would force it to be 0. No condition on the covariance matrix can detect this, because covariances involve only pairs.

2.6.1. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Find the moment generating function of the multinomial count vector of Example (Multinomial counts), and recover its mean and covariance matrix from Theorem 2.6.1(b).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let S1,…,Sk be independent random signs. For each nonempty A⊆{1,…,k} put TA=∏i∈ASi. Show that the 2k−1 variables TA are pairwise independent with covariance matrix I, and find a triple of them that is not mutually independent.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let φ be the N(0,1) density and, for |a|≤1, let fa(x)=φ(log⁡x)x{1+asin⁡(2πlog⁡x)},x>0.

  • (a) Show that fa is a probability density for every such a, and that f0 is the lognormal density, the density of eW with W∼N(0,1).

  • (b) Show that a random variable Xa with density fa has E(Xak)=ek2/2 for every integer k≥0, the same for all a. Hint: substitute u=log⁡x and use ekuφ(u)=ek2/2φ(u−k), then the symmetry of φ.

  • (c) Show that EetX0=∞ for every t>0. Conclude that the uniqueness statement Theorem 2.6.1(a) cannot be weakened to “all moments are finite and agree”: these distributions differ, yet no moment, and hence no moment-based argument, can separate them.

Solution.

  • (a) Since |sin⁡|≤1 and |a|≤1, the brace is nonnegative, so fa≥0. The substitution u=log⁡x, dx=xdu, turns ∫0∞fa(x)dx into ∫−∞∞φ(u){1+asin⁡(2πu)}du=1, because φ is a density and u↦φ(u)sin⁡(2πu) is odd and integrable. The same substitution identifies f0 as the density of eW.

  • (b) With the same substitution, E(Xak)=∫−∞∞ekuφ(u){1+asin⁡(2πu)}du=ek2/2[1+a∫−∞∞φ(u−k)sin⁡(2πu)du]. The remaining integral is Esin⁡{2π(W+k)} with W∼N(0,1), which expands as sin⁡(2πk)Ecos⁡(2πW)+cos⁡(2πk)Esin⁡(2πW). For integer k the first coefficient sin⁡(2πk) is zero, and Esin⁡(2πW)=0 because W is symmetric. So E(Xak)=ek2/2 whatever a is.

  • (c) For t>0, EetX0=∫−∞∞exp⁡(teu)φ(u)du, and teu−u2/2→∞ as u→∞, so the integrand does not even tend to zero: the integral diverges. Hence Theorem 2.6.1 does not apply to the lognormal law, and indeed it cannot: the perturbed laws have exactly the same moments of every order but different densities, so they are genuinely different distributions.

Exercise (C2)

  • (a) Suppose each component of Y has a moment generating function that is finite on |s|<h. Show that MY(t) is finite whenever maxi|ti|<h/p. Hint: convexity of the exponential gives exp⁡(∑itiYi)≤p−1∑iexp⁡(ptiYi). So the hypothesis of Theorem 2.6.1 is a statement about the components, but its conclusions are not.

  • (b) Let Z∼N(0,1), let S be an independent random sign, and put W=SZ. Show that M(Z,W)(t)=12exp⁡{12(t1+t2)2}+12exp⁡{12(t1−t2)2}. Deduce that Z and W are each N(0,1), that Cov(Z,W)=0, and that they are not independent.

  • (c) Compute E(Z2W2) and compare it with the value it would have under independence.

Solution.

  • (a) Each pti lies in (−h,h), so Eexp⁡(ptiYi)=MYi(pti)<∞. Averaging the hint over i and taking expectations gives MY(t)≤p−1∑iMYi(pti)<∞. The hint itself is the convexity inequality exp⁡(p−1∑isi)≤p−1∑iesi with si=ptiYi.

  • (b) Conditioning on S, Eexp⁡(t1Z+t2SZ)=12Eexp⁡{(t1+t2)Z}+12Eexp⁡{(t1−t2)Z}, and each term is a normal moment generating function, which gives the display. Setting t2=0 leaves et12/2, so Z∼N(0,1); setting t1=0 leaves the same, so W∼N(0,1). Expanding to second order, M(Z,W)(t)=1+12(t12+t22)+O(‖t‖4) with no t1t2 term, so Cov(Z,W)=0 by Theorem 2.6.1(b). Were they independent, the joint moment generating function would be exp⁡{12(t12+t22)} by Theorem 2.6.1(d). Along t=(s,s)⊤ the display equals 12(e2s2+1)=1+s2+s4+O(s6), whereas exp⁡(s2)=1+s2+12s4+O(s6). The two disagree in every neighbourhood of the origin, so Z and W are dependent, although both are normal and uncorrelated.

  • (c) W2=Z2, so E(Z2W2)=E(Z4)=3, whereas independence would give E(Z2)E(W2)=1. This is the failure of Example (A point on a circle) in a normal disguise; joint normality, defined in Chapter 3, is exactly what rules it out.

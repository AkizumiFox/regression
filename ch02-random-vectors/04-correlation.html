2.4  Correlation matrices

Covariances carry units: the covariance of income in dollars with age in years is in dollar-years. To compare the strength of linear association across pairs of variables, each covariance is divided by the two standard deviations.

Definition 2.4.1 (Correlation matrix)

Let Y have covariance matrix Σ=(σij) with every σii>0, and let Dσ=diag(σ111/2,…,σpp1/2) be the diagonal matrix of standard deviations. The correlation matrix of Y is R=Dσ−1ΣDσ−1,with entriesρij=σijσiiσjj=corr(Yi,Yj). Equivalently Σ=DσRDσ. For random vectors U and V with standard-deviation matrices DU and DV, the cross-correlation matrix is DU−1Cov(U,V)DV−1.

Some books write Pρ for the correlation matrix. We use R in this chapter, and R^ for its sample counterpart in Section 2.7.

Proposition 2.4.1 (Properties of correlation matrices)

Let R be the correlation matrix of Y.

  • (a) R is the covariance matrix of the standardized vector 𝛍Z=Dσ−1(Y−μ). Hence R is nonnegative definite with unit diagonal, and tr(R)=p.

  • (b) |ρij|≤1, with |ρij|=1 iff Zj=ρijZi with probability one.

  • (c) rank(R)=rank(Σ). In particular, R is positive definite iff Σ is.

  • (d) If Y∗=DY+c with D=diag(d1,…,dp) and every di≠0, then ρij∗=sign(didj)ρij. Positive rescaling and shifting leave R unchanged.

  • (e) Conversely, every nonnegative definite matrix with unit diagonal is the correlation matrix of some random vector.

Proof.

  • (a) Theorem 2.2.1 gives Cov(Z)=Dσ−1ΣDσ−1=R, and Theorem 2.2.3(a) applies. (b) The 2×2 matrix of rows and columns i,j of R is the covariance matrix of (Zi,Zj), so it is nonnegative definite and its determinant 1−ρij2 is nonnegative. If ρij=±1, the vector (ρij,−1)⊤ is in the null space of that 2×2 matrix, so ρijZi−Zj=0 with probability one by Theorem 2.2.3(c). Conversely, Zj=cZi with Var(Zi)=Var(Zj)=1 forces c=±1 and ρij=c. (c) Dσ is nonsingular. (d) Cov(Y∗)=DΣD has entries didjσij and standard deviations |di|σii1/2. (e) Such a matrix is a covariance matrix by Theorem 2.2.3(b), and its variances are one.

Part (d) is the reason correlation is the natural scale-free summary. Part (e) is less obvious than it looks. A symmetric matrix with unit diagonal and off-diagonal entries in [−1,1] need not be a correlation matrix, because the correlations must be jointly consistent.

Proposition 2.4.2 (Three correlations)

Let |ρ12|<1 and |ρ13|<1. The matrix R=(1ρ12ρ13ρ121ρ23ρ13ρ231) is a correlation matrix iff ρ12ρ13−(1−ρ122)(1−ρ132)≤ρ23≤ρ12ρ13+(1−ρ122)(1−ρ132).

Proof. By Proposition 2.4.1(e) we must decide when R is nonnegative definite. Write r=(ρ12,ρ13)⊤ and let R2 be the lower right 2×2 block. The nonsingular matrix T=(10⊤−rI2) gives TRT⊤=(10⊤0R2−rr⊤), and a⊤Ra=b⊤(TRT⊤)b with b=(T⊤)−1a ranges over all vectors as a does. So R is nonnegative definite iff the Schur complement R2−rr⊤=(1−ρ122ρ23−ρ12ρ13ρ23−ρ12ρ131−ρ132) is. A symmetric 2×2 matrix with positive diagonal is nonnegative definite iff its determinant is nonnegative, because its eigenvalues have positive sum and their product is the determinant. The determinant is (1−ρ122)(1−ρ132)−(ρ23−ρ12ρ13)2, and it is nonnegative exactly on the stated interval.

If Y2 and Y3 both correlate 0.8 with Y1, the interval is [0.28,1]. Two variables that are strongly related to a third cannot be negatively correlated with each other, nor even uncorrelated. The quantity (ρ23−ρ12ρ13)/(1−ρ122)(1−ρ132), which the proof forces into [−1,1], is the correlation of Y2 and Y3 after the linear effect of Y1 has been removed from both. Section 2.5 develops this residualization, and Definition 3.6.1 names the result the partial correlation.

Correlation matrices do not transform simply under linear maps: the correlation matrix of AY must be recomputed from AΣA⊤. Theory is therefore done with covariance matrices, and correlation matrices are used for description.

2.4.1. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that (1−ρ)Ip+ρ11⊤ is a correlation matrix iff −1/(p−1)≤ρ≤1. Explain the lower bound through the variance of ∑iZi.

Solution. The matrix sends 1 to (1+(p−1)ρ)1 and every u⊥1 to (1−ρ)u. Its eigenvalues are 1+(p−1)ρ (once) and 1−ρ (p−1 times). By Proposition 2.4.1(e) it is a correlation matrix iff both are nonnegative. If Z has this correlation matrix, Var(∑iZi)=1⊤R1=p[1+(p−1)ρ], which must be nonnegative. Many variables cannot all be strongly negatively correlated with one another, because their sum would then have negative variance.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that det(Σ)≤∏iσii for every covariance matrix, with equality for positive definite Σ iff Σ is diagonal. Hint: factor out Dσ and use the eigenvalues of R.

Solution. If some σii=0, the ith row of Σ vanishes (apply Theorem 2.2.3(c) with a the ith coordinate vector) and both sides are zero. Otherwise detΣ=det(Dσ)2detR=∏iσii⋅detR. The eigenvalues λi≥0 of R sum to trR=p, so by the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means detR=∏iλi≤(p−1∑iλi)p=1. Equality holds iff every λi=1, that is, iff R=I, that is, iff Σ is diagonal.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)
  • (a) Show that a p×p matrix is a correlation matrix iff it is the Gram matrix (ui⊤uj) of p unit vectors in Rp. (b) For p=3, write ρij=cos⁡ϑij with ϑij∈[0,π]. Show that Proposition 2.4.2 is equivalent to the spherical triangle inequalities |ϑ12−ϑ13|≤ϑ23≤min(ϑ12+ϑ13,2π−ϑ12−ϑ13).

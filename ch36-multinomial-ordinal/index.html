Multinomial and Ordinal Responses

A response with two categories has one logit. A response with c categories has c−1 of them, and the modelling question is which c−1 comparisons to make. If the categories are names — a brand, a party, a mode of travel — the natural comparisons are with a fixed baseline, and the result is the baseline-category logit model, a generalized linear model whose response is a vector of counts. If the categories are ordered, that choice wastes information: a model ignoring the order needs (c−1)p coefficients to say what a single slope may say. Ordinal models get back down to p by cutting the scale and giving every cut the same coefficients. Where the cut is made is the whole story. Cut at a level and you get the cumulative, or proportional odds, model; cut conditionally on having got that far and you get the sequential, or continuation-ratio, model. These answer different questions, and the data can tell them apart.

What you need. Chapter 34 (exponential families, the likelihood equations, Fisher scoring, the deviance and the three test statistics) and Chapter 35 (logistic regression, the alternative links, separation, goodness of fit). From earlier parts: Chapter 11 for the likelihood ratio test Theorem 11.4.1 and the three flavours of test statistic Proposition 11.4.2, Chapter 21 for weighted least squares and the sandwich estimator (Theorem 21.4.2), and Chapter 29 for the information criteria (Definition 29.2.2).

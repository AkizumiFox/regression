12.5  Inverse prediction and calibration

Prediction runs from x to y. Calibration runs the other way: an instrument is calibrated on specimens of known value, a new specimen is measured, and its value is wanted. The estimate reads the fitted line backwards; the interval reads the prediction band backwards, and it is not always an interval.

12.5.1. Ratios of estimable functions

Inverse prediction belongs to a wider problem: a confidence set for a ratio of linear functions of the coefficients, such as the zero crossing −β0/β1 of a line or the turning point −β1/(2β2) of a quadratic. A delta-method interval ignores that the denominator is uncertain and may be near zero. Fieller’s approach tests, for each candidate value, a hypothesis that is linear in the coefficients.

Proposition 12.5.1 (Fieller’s confidence set for a ratio)

Let 𝛌𝛌λ1,λ2∈C(X⊤) be linearly independent, put 𝛌𝛃a=λ1⊤β, 𝛌𝛃b=λ2⊤β, their estimates 𝛌𝛃a^=λ1⊤β^, 𝛌𝛃b^=λ2⊤β^, and 𝛌𝛌vij=λi⊤Gλj, so that V=(vij) is positive definite. Suppose b≠0 and let θ=a/b. With t=tn−r,α/2, let

F(Y)={θ0∈R: (a^−θ0b^)2≤t2s2(v11−2θ0v12+θ02v22)}.
(12.5.1)

  • (a) F(Y) is an exact 1−α confidence set for θ, at every parameter value with b≠0.

  • (b) Let Q=(a^,b^)V−1(a^,b^)⊤. Outside an event of probability zero, F(Y) is

    • a bounded closed interval containing θ^=a^/b^, if b^2>t2s2v22;
    • the union of two disjoint closed rays, if b^2<t2s2v22 and Q>t2s2;
    • the whole real line, if b^2<t2s2v22 and Q<t2s2.

Proof.

  • (a) Fix θ0. The vector 𝛌𝛌λ1−θ0λ2 lies in C(X⊤) and is not zero, so 𝛌𝛌𝛃(λ1−θ0λ2)⊤β=a−θ0b is estimable, with estimate a^−θ0b^ and 𝛌𝛌𝛌𝛌(λ1−θ0λ2)⊤G(λ1−θ0λ2)=v11−2θ0v12+θ02v22. The inequality in Eq. 12.5.1 says that the t interval of Theorem 12.1.1 for a−θ0b contains 0. At θ0=θ, a−θb=0, so the probability of this event is exactly 1−α.

  • (b) The defining inequality is q(θ0)=Aθ02−2Bθ0+K≤0, with A=b^2−t2s2v22,B=a^b^−t2s2v12,K=a^2−t2s2v11. Expanding and using (a^,b^)V−1(a^,b^)⊤=(v22a^2−2v12a^b^+v11b^2)/detV, B2−AK=t2s2(v22a^2−2v12a^b^+v11b^2−t2s2detV)=t2s2det(V)(Q−t2s2). If A>0, then q(θ^)=−t2s2(v11−2θ^v12+θ^2v22)<0, so the upward parabola q has two real roots, and {q≤0} is the closed interval between them, which contains θ^. If A<0, the parabola opens downward. When B2−AK<0 it is negative everywhere and F(Y)=R. When B2−AK>0 it is nonpositive exactly outside the open interval between its roots. The events A=0 and Q=t2s2 have probability zero, because b^ and Q/s2 have continuous distributions.

The first case says that the t test rejects b=0: the denominator is away from zero and the ratio is confined. Otherwise very large |θ| cannot be excluded, and Q/(2s2), the F statistic for a=b=0, decides between two rays and the whole line. Exercise (C1) gives a picture: F(Y) is the set of slopes of lines through the origin that meet a certain confidence ellipse for (a,b).

12.5.2. The calibration problem

A calibration experiment measures n standards with known values x1,…,xn, not all equal, and records readings Yi=β0+β1xi+εi,i=1,…,n. A new specimen with unknown value x0 is then measured m≥1 times, Y0j=β0+β1x0+ε0j, and all n+m errors are independent N(0,σ2). Let Y¯0 be the mean of the new readings, and let β^0, β^1 and s2 (with n−2 degrees of freedom) come from the standards alone. The classical estimator reads the fitted line backwards: x^0=Y¯0−β^0β^1=x¯+Y¯0−Y¯β^1. It is the maximum likelihood estimator (Exercise (A2)) and, being a ratio, inherits a pathology of ratios.

Proposition 12.5.2 (The classical estimator has no mean)

If Pr(Y¯0≠Y¯)>0, which always holds here, then E|x^0|=∞.

Proof. Y¯ and β^1 are jointly normal and uncorrelated (Corollary 5.5.4), hence independent (Theorem 3.4.1), and Y¯0 is independent of both. So g=Y¯0−Y¯ is independent of β^1 and E|x^0−x¯|=E|g|E|1/β^1|, with E|g|>0. The density f of β^1 is continuous and positive at 0, so f≥c>0 on some [−ϵ,ϵ] and E|1/β^1|≥c∫−ϵϵ|u|−1du=∞. Finally |x^0|≥|x^0−x¯|−|x¯|.

So a delta-method “standard error” of x^0 approximates a quantity that does not exist. That is harmless when β^1 is many standard errors from zero, and harmful exactly when the slope is poorly determined.

Theorem 12.5.3 (Calibration set)

In the calibration model, let t=tn−2,α/2 and

C={x∈R: (Y¯0−β^0−β^1x)2≤t2s2(1m+1n+(x−x¯)2Sxx)}.
(12.5.2)

  • (a) C is an exact 1−α confidence set for x0, at every (β0,β1,σ2,x0), including β1=0.

  • (b) C is the set of x at which Y¯0 lies inside the 1−α prediction interval of Theorem 12.3.1(c) for the mean of m new readings at x.

  • (c) Let T1=β^1Sxx/s be the t statistic for the slope and g=Y¯0−Y¯. Outside an event of probability zero, C is

    • a bounded interval containing x^0, if T12>t2;
    • the union of two disjoint closed rays, if T12<t2 and T12+g2/{s2(1/m+1/n)}>t2;
    • the whole real line, if T12+g2/{s2(1/m+1/n)}<t2.

Proof.

  • (a) and (b). For fixed x, Y¯0−β^0−β^1x is the difference between the mean of the new readings and the estimated mean response at x. At x=x0, Theorem 12.3.1(c), with x0=(1,x0)⊤ and h0=1/n+(x0−x¯)2/Sxx (Eq. 5.5.2), shows that Y¯0−β^0−β^1x0s1/m+1/n+(x0−x¯)2/Sxx∼t(n−2), whatever the parameters are. This is a pivot, and x0∈C is the event that its absolute value is at most t. Nothing in the argument requires β1≠0. Part (b) restates the definition.

  • (b) Put d=x−x¯ and c=1/m+1/n. Since Y¯0−β^0−β^1x=g−β^1d, the defining inequality is Ad2−2β^1gd+(g2−t2s2c)≤0 with A=β^12−t2s2/Sxx. Its discriminant is β^12g2−A(g2−t2s2c)=t2s2(g2Sxx+cA)=t2s4cSxx(T12+g2s2c−t2). The sign of A is that of T12−t2. If A>0, the quadratic is negative at d=g/β^1, that is at x=x^0, where it equals −t2s2(c+d2/Sxx); so it has two real roots and C is the interval between them. If A<0, the argument of Proposition 12.5.1(b) applies: two rays if the discriminant is positive and the whole line if it is negative. Equalities have probability zero.

Part (b) is the practical recipe (Figure 12.5.1): draw the prediction band for the mean of m readings and the horizontal line at Y¯0, and read off where the line is inside the band. A steep calibration line crosses the band in a short interval. A flat one lets both hyperbolic edges pass the horizontal line, giving two rays or everything. The set is bounded iff the slope test rejects, as it does for any useful instrument.

Proposition 12.5.4 (Unbounded calibration sets cannot be avoided)

Let D be any confidence set for x0 with coverage at least 1−α>0 at every parameter value, including those with β1=0. Then at every parameter value with β1=0, Pr{v∈D}≥1−α for every real v, and if D is measurable in the sense of Proposition 12.1.3, its expected length is infinite.

Proof. When β1=0, the joint distribution of the standards and the new readings does not involve x0. So for every v, the probability that v∈D is the same as it would be if x0 were v, and that probability is at least 1−α. The rest is the Fubini argument in the proof of Proposition 12.1.3.

By continuity the same happens approximately for small β1. A procedure that always reports a bounded interval, like the delta method, pays with coverage below 1−α somewhere. The Fieller set’s unbounded answers are the honest answer to a question the data cannot settle.

Example (Calibrating an assay)

In a simulated assay, nine standards of concentration 0,1,…,8 are each measured three times (n=27). The fitted line is 0.0338+0.1161x, with s=0.0131 and slope t statistic 118.7. A new specimen is read m=3 times, with mean reading 0.6521. The classical estimate is x^0=5.327, and the 95% calibration interval is (5.184, 5.471) (t25,0.025=2.060). For an assay this precise the delta-method interval, x^0±t(s/|β^1|)1/m+1/n+(x^0−x¯)2/Sxx, agrees to three decimals: (5.184, 5.471).

Now consider a weak assay with the same design, in which the fitted slope is 0.0021 and its t statistic is only 1.67. The mean reading of the standards is 0.0475. A specimen with mean reading 0.0525, close to that mean, gives the whole real line: nothing about its concentration can be concluded. A specimen with mean reading 0.0775 gives two rays, (−∞, −55.3]∪[7.6, ∞): the middle of the scale is excluded, but not extreme concentrations of either sign. Figure 12.5.1 shows both assays.

In 20000 simulated experiments at x0=5.3, both intervals cover in 0.9517 for the precise assay. With the slope three standard errors from zero (β1=0.00335) the Fieller set again covers in 0.9517, since its event involves only the errors, and is bounded in 0.817 of the experiments, close to the power 0.822 of the slope t test; the delta-method interval covers in 0.9827, wastefully. With the slope two standard errors from zero and x0=12, beyond the standards, the Fieller set covers in 0.9528 and the delta-method interval in only 0.9337.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(1206)
x = np.repeat(np.arange(9.0), 3)                   # standards, in triplicate
n = len(x)
y = 0.04 + 0.115 * x + 0.015 * rng.normal(size=n)  # absorbance readings
m = 3
y0 = 0.04 + 0.115 * 5.3 + 0.015 * rng.normal(size=m)   # the unknown sample, true x0 = 5.3


def calibrate(x, y, y0bar, m, level=0.95):
    """Fieller set {x0 : |y0bar - b0 - b1 x0| <= t s sqrt(1/m + 1/n + (x0 - xbar)^2 / Sxx)}."""
    n = len(x)
    xbar, Sxx = x.mean(), np.sum((x - x.mean()) ** 2)
    b1 = np.sum((x - xbar) * (y - y.mean())) / Sxx
    s2 = np.sum((y - y.mean() - b1 * (x - xbar)) ** 2) / (n - 2)
    t2 = stats.t.ppf(0.5 + level / 2, n - 2) ** 2
    g = y0bar - y.mean()
    # quadratic in d = x0 - xbar:  A d^2 - 2 B d + K <= 0
    A = b1**2 - t2 * s2 / Sxx
    B = b1 * g
    K = g**2 - t2 * s2 * (1 / m + 1 / n)
    disc = B**2 - A * K
    if disc < 0:
        return "whole line", (-np.inf, np.inf), xbar + g / b1
    r1, r2 = sorted([(B - np.sqrt(disc)) / A, (B + np.sqrt(disc)) / A])
    if A > 0:
        return "interval", (xbar + r1, xbar + r2), xbar + g / b1
    return "two rays", (xbar + r1, xbar + r2), xbar + g / b1   # (-inf, first] and [second, inf)


shape, (lo, hi), x0_hat = calibrate(x, y, y0.mean(), m)
print(f"estimate {x0_hat:.3f}; 95% calibration set: {shape} ({lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f})")

Remark (Classical or inverse estimation?). The inverse estimator regresses the xi on the readings and predicts from Y¯0: x~0=x¯+(Sxy/Syy)(Y¯0−Y¯), with Sxy=∑(xi−x¯)(Yi−Y¯) and Syy=∑(Yi−Y¯)2. Since Y¯0−Y¯=β^1(x^0−x¯) and β^1=Sxy/Sxx, x~0−x¯=Sxy2SxxSyy(x^0−x¯)=R2(x^0−x¯), where R is the sample correlation of the calibration pairs. So x~0 shrinks x^0 toward x¯ by the factor R2∈[0,1]. It has finite moments and can beat x^0 in mean squared error near the centre of the standards (Krutchkoff 1967); away from the centre it does worse (Exercise (B3)). Hoadley (1970) showed that it is a Bayes estimator under a particular prior for x0.

12.5.3. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For a straight line, write down Fieller’s 1−α confidence set for the point ξ=−β0/β1 where the line crosses zero, and state the condition under which it is a bounded interval. Show that it is the calibration set of Theorem 12.5.3 with Y¯0 replaced by 0 and 1/m replaced by 0.

Solution. With 𝛌λ1=(1,0)⊤ and 𝛌λ2=(0,1)⊤, ξ=−a/b. Apply Proposition 12.5.1 to θ=a/b and change sign, or directly: ξ0 is in the set iff the t interval for β0+β1ξ0 contains 0, that is iff (β^0+β^1ξ0)2≤t2s2{1/n+(ξ0−x¯)2/Sxx}. This is Eq. 12.5.2 with Y¯0=0 and the 1/m term absent, because 0 is a known number rather than an average of new readings. It is bounded iff T12>t2.

Exercise (A2)

Show that x^0, together with the least squares estimates from the standards, maximizes the joint likelihood of the n+m readings over (β0,β1,σ2,x0) when β^1≠0. What is the maximum likelihood estimate of σ2?

Solution. For fixed σ2 the likelihood is maximized by minimizing ∑i(Yi−β0−β1xi)2+∑j(Y0j−β0−β1x0)2. For any β1≠0 the second sum is minimized over x0 by choosing β0+β1x0=Y¯0, leaving ∑j(Y0j−Y¯0)2, which is free of the parameters. The first sum is then minimized by least squares on the standards, and x0=(Y¯0−β^0)/β^1=x^0. The maximum likelihood estimate of σ2 is {SSE+∑j(Y0j−Y¯0)2}/(n+m).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

When m≥2, the new readings carry their own information about σ2. Let sp2={SSE+∑j(Y0j−Y¯0)2}/(n+m−3). Show that replacing s by sp and tn−2,α/2 by tn+m−3,α/2 in Eq. 12.5.2 gives another exact 1−α set. Why is it shorter on average, and why is it more often bounded?

Solution. The within-specimen sum of squares is σ2χ2(m−1), independent of Y¯0 (the sample mean and sample variance of a normal sample are independent) and of the standards. So {SSE+∑j(Y0j−Y¯0)2}/σ2∼χ2(n+m−3), independent of the numerator of the pivot, and the pivot with sp is t(n+m−3). More degrees of freedom give a smaller multiplier and a less variable estimate of σ, so the set is shorter on average. The bounded case requires β^12Sxx>t2sp2, and a smaller t makes this easier.

Exercise (B2)

In the quadratic regression EYi=β0+β1xi+β2xi2, use Proposition 12.5.1 to give a confidence set for the location x∗=−β1/(2β2) of the turning point. When is it a bounded interval, and what does that condition mean for the curvature?

Solution. Take 𝛌λ1=(0,1,0)⊤, 𝛌λ2=(0,0,2)⊤ and θ=β1/(2β2), so x∗=−θ. The set is {x0:(β^1+2β^2x0)2≤t2s2(c11+4c12x0+4c22x02)}, where cij are the entries of (X⊤X)−1 belonging to β1,β2. It is the set of x0 at which the estimated derivative β1+2β2x0 is not significantly different from zero. It is bounded iff β^22>t2s2c22, that is iff the curvature is significant: without significant curvature, the fitted curve may be nearly straight and its turning point anywhere.

Exercise (B3)

Study the two estimators of the Remark “Classical or inverse estimation?” in an idealized large calibration experiment: the standards are so numerous that β^0, β^1 and R2 may be replaced by constants β0, β1≠0 and ρ2∈(0,1), while Y¯0 is still the mean of m new readings. Let v=σ2/(mβ12).

  1. Show that x^0 is unbiased with mean squared error v, and that x~0=x¯+ρ2(x^0−x¯) has mean squared error ρ4v+(1−ρ2)2(x0−x¯)2.
  2. Show that x~0 has the smaller mean squared error iff (x0−x¯)2<v(1+ρ2)/(1−ρ2).
  3. Let τ2=Sxx/n, so that in this limit ρ2=β12τ2/(β12τ2+σ2). Show that the condition of part 2 reads (x0−x¯)2<v+2τ2/m. With m=1 and a precise instrument, roughly how far from x¯, in units of the spread τ of the standards, does the inverse estimator stay ahead?

Solution.

  1. Here x^0=x0+ε¯0/β1 with ε¯0∼N(0,σ2/m), so it is unbiased with variance v. Then x~0−x0=ρ2(x^0−x0)−(1−ρ2)(x0−x¯): variance ρ4v plus squared bias (1−ρ2)2(x0−x¯)2.
  2. The inequality ρ4v+(1−ρ2)2(x0−x¯)2<v is (1−ρ2)2(x0−x¯)2<(1−ρ2)(1+ρ2)v; divide by (1−ρ2)2>0.
  3. (1+ρ2)/(1−ρ2)=(2β12τ2+σ2)/σ2, so v(1+ρ2)/(1−ρ2)=v+2τ2/m. When σ is small, v≈0 and the inverse estimator wins only for |x0−x¯|<2τ, a little more than one spread from the centre; beyond that its bias dominates.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In the setting of Proposition 12.5.1, let 𝛟𝛟𝛟Et={ϕ∈R2:((a^,b^)⊤−ϕ)⊤V−1((a^,b^)⊤−ϕ)≤t2s2}, an ellipse with the t constant in place of 2Fα(2,n−r). Show that θ0∈F(Y) iff the line {(ϕ1,ϕ2):ϕ1=θ0ϕ2} meets Et. Use the picture to explain the three cases: the set is the whole line iff Et contains the origin, and it is bounded iff Et does not meet the axis ϕ2=0.

Solution. The line is 𝛟𝛟{ϕ:c⊤ϕ=0} with c=(1,−θ0)⊤. By Corollary 12.2.4 (with the constant t2s2), the values of 𝛟c⊤ϕ over Et form the interval a^−θ0b^±tsc⊤Vc, and c⊤Vc=v11−2θ0v12+θ02v22. So the line meets Et iff this interval contains 0, which is Eq. 12.5.1. Every line through the origin meets Et iff the origin is in Et, which is Q≤t2s2. Lines of large slope approach the axis ϕ2=0 (the ϕ1-axis in these coordinates); the set is unbounded iff lines of arbitrarily large slope meet Et, iff Et meets that axis, which by Corollary 12.2.4 with c=(0,1)⊤ is b^2≤t2s2v22.

Exercise (C2)

Two straight lines EY=αk+βkx, k=1,2, are fitted to two independent samples in one model with a common σ2, as in a two-phase regression. Use Proposition 12.5.1 to construct a confidence set for the x-coordinate (α1−α2)/(β2−β1) of their intersection. When is it bounded? Interpret the unbounded cases in terms of parallel lines.

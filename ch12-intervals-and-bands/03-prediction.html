12.3  Prediction intervals

Often the quantity of interest is an observation not yet made: tomorrow’s stack loss, or the reading of the next specimen. An interval for it must allow for the error in the estimated mean, which shrinks as data accumulate, and the error of the new observation itself, which does not. The first depends on where the new point lies relative to the data; the second makes prediction intervals depend on normality in a way that confidence intervals do not.

12.3.1. Predicting a new observation

Let the data follow the normal linear model of Section 12.1, and let

𝛃Y0=x0⊤β+ε0,ε0∼N(0,σ2),  ε0 independent of Y,
(12.3.1)

be a future observation at a regressor vector x0∈C(X⊤). The requirement x0∈C(X⊤) makes the mean 𝛃x0⊤β estimable. Without it, Proposition 12.1.3 shows that nothing useful can be said. We call h0=x0⊤Gx0 the leverage of the new point. When x0 is the ith row of X, h0 is the diagonal entry hii of M (Proposition 6.8.1), and σ2h0 is always the variance of 𝛃Y^0=x0⊤β^ (Theorem 7.5.1(a)).

Theorem 12.3.1 (Prediction interval)

Under Eq. 12.3.1, put 𝛃Y^0=x0⊤β^.

  • (a) Y0−Y^0∼N(0,σ2(1+h0)), it is independent of s2, and Y0−Y^0s1+h0∼t(n−r).

  • (b) The interval Y^0±tn−r,α/2s1+h0 contains Y0 with probability exactly 1−α, the probability being computed over Y and Y0 jointly, at every 𝛃(β,σ2).

  • (c) More generally, if Y¯0 is the mean of m independent new observations at x0, each distributed as in Eq. 12.3.1, then Y^0±tn−r,α/2s1/m+h0 contains Y¯0 with probability exactly 1−α. As m→∞ this interval becomes the confidence interval Y^0±tn−r,α/2sh0 for 𝛃x0⊤β.

Proof. We prove (c), of which (a) and (b) are the case m=1. The new observations are independent of Y, and 𝛃Y¯0∼N(x0⊤β,σ2/m). The vector (Y¯0,Y⊤)⊤ is normal with independent blocks, so Y¯0−Y^0, a linear function of it, is normal (Theorem 3.3.1) with mean 𝛃𝛃x0⊤β−x0⊤β=0 and variance σ2/m+σ2h0, the two terms adding because Y¯0 and Y^0 are independent. For independence from s2: Y^0 is a function of MY, and MY and SSE are independent (Theorem 7.5.1(c)). Since Y¯0 is independent of Y, for any events, Pr(Y¯0∈A, MY∈B, SSE∈D)=Pr(Y¯0∈A)Pr(MY∈B)Pr(SSE∈D), so (Y¯0,MY) is independent of SSE, and so is Y¯0−Y^0. Dividing the standardized normal variable by (SSE/σ2)/(n−r) gives t(n−r) (Definition 4.1.3), and the interval statement is Pr{|T|≤tn−r,α/2}=1−α. The limit as m→∞ is immediate, and the limiting interval is Eq. 12.1.1 with 𝛌λ=x0.

Part (a) was sketched in Exercise (B2). A prediction interval is for a random variable, and its probability refers to the joint experiment: collect the data, compute the interval, observe Y0. Given the data, the probability that the interval contains Y0 is random, 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃Pr(Y0∈interval∣Y)=Φ(x0⊤(β^−β)+ts1+h0σ)−Φ(x0⊤(β^−β)−ts1+h0σ), with t=tn−r,α/2 and Φ the standard normal distribution function, and it is only its expectation that equals 1−α.

In the variance σ2(1+h0), the leverage h0 is the price of not knowing 𝛃β, of order r/n at a typical point (Proposition 6.8.1). The 1 is the new error, which no amount of data removes: the prediction interval never becomes shorter than about 2zα/2σ, while the interval for the mean shrinks to a point.

Example (Predicting a state’s murder rate)

In the murder-rate regression, consider a state at the centroid of the regressors. Its leverage is h0=1/n=0.0200 (see Proposition 12.3.2), the fitted rate is 4.514, and the 95% intervals are mean response: (4.090, 4.938),new state: (1.487, 7.541). The prediction interval is 7.14 times as long as the interval for the mean, and nearly all of its length, 6.053 per 100,000, comes from s.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats

data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data
data = data.drop(index="District of Columbia")
y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), data[["poverty", "single", "urban"]]])
n, p = X.shape
C = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
beta_hat = C @ X.T @ y
s = np.sqrt(np.sum((y - X @ beta_hat) ** 2) / (n - p))
q = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - p)
h = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", X, C, X)            # leverages of the 50 states


def intervals(x0):
    """Leverage h0, 95% interval for the mean response, 95% prediction interval."""
    h0 = x0 @ C @ x0
    fit = x0 @ beta_hat
    ci = (fit - q * s * np.sqrt(h0), fit + q * s * np.sqrt(h0))
    pi = (fit - q * s * np.sqrt(1 + h0), fit + q * s * np.sqrt(1 + h0))
    return h0, fit, ci, pi


x_centre = X.mean(axis=0)                        # the average state
x_hidden = np.array([1.0, 20.0, 19.0, 60.0])     # each coordinate inside its observed range
for name, x0 in [("centre", x_centre), ("hidden", x_hidden)]:
    h0, fit, ci, pi = intervals(x0)
    print(f"{name}: h0 = {h0:.4f}, fit {fit:.3f}, mean ({ci[0]:.3f}, {ci[1]:.3f}), "
          f"new state ({pi[0]:.3f}, {pi[1]:.3f})")
print(f"largest leverage in the data: {h.max():.4f}")

12.3.2. Leverage and extrapolation

Proposition 12.3.2 (Leverage of a new point)

Let 𝛒x0=X⊤ρ∈C(X⊤) and h0=x0⊤Gx0.

  • (a) h0=min{‖a‖2: X⊤a=x0}, and the minimum is attained only at 𝛒a=Mρ.

  • (b) If X=[1,X1] has full column rank, x0=(1,x01⊤)⊤, x¯ is the vector of column means of X1 and X~1=X1−1x¯⊤, then h0=1n+(x01−x¯)⊤(X~1⊤X~1)−1(x01−x¯).

  • (c) If further columns are added, X+=[X,Z], and x0+=(x0⊤,z0⊤)⊤∈C(X+⊤), then the leverage of x0+ in the enlarged model is at least h0.

Proof.

  • (a) Let X⊤a=x0. Then 𝛒X⊤Ma=X⊤a=x0=X⊤Mρ, so 𝛒Ma−Mρ lies in C(X) and is orthogonal to C(X); hence 𝛒Ma=Mρ. By Pythagoras, 𝛒𝛒‖a‖2=‖Mρ‖2+‖(I−M)a‖2≥‖Mρ‖2, with equality iff 𝛒a=Mρ, and 𝛒𝛒𝛒‖Mρ‖2=ρ⊤XGX⊤ρ=h0 by Theorem 6.3.6.

  • (b) X=[1,X~1]A with A=(1x¯⊤0I), which is invertible. Since 1⊤X~1=0⊤, X⊤X=A⊤diag(n,X~1⊤X~1)A, and so h0=w⊤diag(1/n,(X~1⊤X~1)−1)w with w=A−⊤x0. Solving A⊤w=x0 gives w=(1,(x01−x¯)⊤)⊤.

  • (c) Every a with X+⊤a=x0+ also satisfies X⊤a=x0. A minimum over a smaller set is at least as large, so (a), applied to both models, gives the claim.

Part (a) is the Gauss–Markov theorem in disguise. Part (b) makes leverage a distance: the quadratic form is (n−1)−1 times the squared Mahalanobis distance of x01 from the centroid of the regressors, as for the observed points (Proposition 6.8.2). So the interval for the mean is shortest at the centroid, where h0=1/n, and widens along ellipsoidal contours. Part (c) says a larger model never predicts more precisely at a given point, though it may predict with less bias; Chapter 29 weighs the two.

With several correlated regressors, a point can lie inside the range of every regressor and still be far from the data, because it breaks their correlation. This hidden extrapolation is detected by the leverage, not by the separate ranges.

Example (Hidden extrapolation)

Poverty ranges from 8.5% to 21.9% over the 50 states, and single-parent households from 17.9% to 32.8%. A state with poverty 20%, single-parent households 19% and 60% urban is inside every range, but poor with few single parents, a combination absent from the data. Its leverage is h0=0.435, far above the largest leverage among the actual states, 0.260 (Mississippi). Figure 12.3.1 shows the contours of h0 over the two regressors, with urbanization held at its mean.

The fitted rate is 3.817. The 95% interval for the mean is (1.840, 5.794), longer than at the centroid by the factor 0.435/0.0200=4.66. The prediction interval, (0.227, 7.407), is only 1.186 times as long as at the centroid, because it is dominated by s. The prediction interval barely registers the extrapolation, and the real danger, that the model may be wrong where there are no data to check it, is in neither formula. If urbanization is dropped from the model the leverage of this point falls to 0.287, as Proposition 12.3.2(c) requires.

12.3.3. When the errors are not normal

The estimate Y^0 is a weighted sum of n errors, approximately normal by a central limit theorem when no weight dominates (Theorem 19.3.2). The new error ε0 is a single draw, and its distribution enters the prediction interval unchanged, however large n is.

Proposition 12.3.3 (Prediction intervals without normality)

Consider a sequence of full-rank models 𝛃𝛆Yn=Xnβ+εn with p fixed and n→∞, and a fixed x0 whose leverage h0,n=x0⊤(Xn⊤Xn)−1x0 tends to zero. Let ε1,…,εn and ε0 be independent and identically distributed with mean 0, variance σ2, finite fourth moment and a continuous distribution function, and let 𝛃Y0=x0⊤β+ε0. Then the coverage of the nominal 1−α prediction interval Y^0±tn−p,α/2s1+h0,n satisfies Pr{|Y0−Y^0|≤tn−p,α/2s1+h0,n}⟶ Pr{|ε0|≤zα/2σ}, where zα/2 is the upper α/2 point of N(0,1). Likewise the probabilities of missing above and below tend to Pr{ε0>zα/2σ} and Pr{ε0<−zα/2σ}.

Proof. Write Tn=(Y0−Y^0)/(s1+h0,n), tn=tn−p,α/2 and E=ε0/σ. First, 𝛃𝛃Y0−Y^0=ε0−x0⊤(β^−β), and 𝛃𝛃x0⊤(β^−β) has mean zero and variance σ2h0,n→0 (Theorem 5.5.1), so it tends to zero in probability by Chebyshev’s inequality. Second, s→σ in probability (Corollary 5.6.4), and tn→zα/2. Hence Dn=Tn−E→0 in probability. Fix η>0. On the event {|Dn|≤η/2, |tn−zα/2|≤η/2}, whose probability tends to one, |E|≤zα/2−η ⟹ |Tn|≤tn ⟹ |E|≤zα/2+η. So Pr{|E|≤zα/2−η}≤lim infPr{|Tn|≤tn}≤lim supPr{|Tn|≤tn}≤Pr{|E|≤zα/2+η}. The distribution function of E is continuous, so letting η→0 gives the limit. The one-sided statements are proved the same way.

The limit is the nominal 1−α only if Pr{|ε0|≤zα/2σ}=1−α, a property of the normal distribution, not of large samples. A simulation shows both effects: a straight line with n equally spaced design points on [0,1], prediction at x0=0.8, and errors with mean zero and variance one that are normal, centred exponential (skewed) or uniform (short-tailed). With 20000 data sets for each case, the coverages of the nominal 80% and 95% intervals are:

Errors n mean, 80% mean, 95% new obs., 80% new obs., 95%
normal 20 0.799 0.949 0.797 0.949
normal 400 0.805 0.953 0.802 0.953
exponential 20 0.774 0.931 0.863 0.939
exponential 400 0.799 0.949 0.896 0.949
uniform 20 0.797 0.948 0.787 0.983
uniform 400 0.800 0.950 0.741 1.000

At n=400 the intervals for the mean are near nominal for every law, while the prediction intervals approach the limits of Proposition 12.3.3: 0.898 and 0.948 (exponential), 0.740 and 1.000 (uniform). The exponential 95% interval looks fine only by accident: at n=400 it misses above in a proportion 0.051 of the samples and below in 0.0000, since a centred exponential error is never below −1.

rng = np.random.default_rng(1203)
laws = {
    "normal": lambda size: rng.normal(size=size),
    "exponential": lambda size: rng.exponential(size=size) - 1.0,                  # skewed
    "uniform": lambda size: rng.uniform(-np.sqrt(3), np.sqrt(3), size=size),       # short tails
}                                                                                  # all: mean 0, variance 1
x0_sim = 0.8


def simulate(law, n_sim, reps=20_000):
    x = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, n_sim)
    Xs = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_sim), x])
    Hs = np.linalg.solve(Xs.T @ Xs, Xs.T)                        # beta_hat = Hs y
    z0 = np.array([1.0, x0_sim])
    h0 = z0 @ np.linalg.solve(Xs.T @ Xs, z0)
    E = laws[law]((reps, n_sim))                                  # true beta = 0, sigma = 1
    fit = E @ (Hs.T @ z0)
    s_sim = np.sqrt(np.sum((E - (E @ Hs.T) @ Xs.T) ** 2, axis=1) / (n_sim - 2))
    new = laws[law](reps)                                         # the new observation's error
    out = {}
    for level in (0.80, 0.95):
        t = stats.t.ppf(0.5 + level / 2, n_sim - 2)
        out[("mean", level)] = np.mean(np.abs(fit) <= t * s_sim * np.sqrt(h0))
        out[("new", level)] = np.mean(np.abs(new - fit) <= t * s_sim * np.sqrt(1 + h0))
    return out


table = {(law, m): simulate(law, m) for law in laws for m in (20, 400)}
for (law, m), out in table.items():
    print(f"{law:12s} n = {m:3d}: " + ", ".join(f"{k[0]} {k[1]:.2f}: {v:.3f}" for k, v in out.items()))

Remedies, transformation (Chapter 22) and resampling (Chapter 23), come later. Checking normality matters much more for prediction than for estimation.

Remark (Prediction is not intervention). A prediction interval describes the next observation from the same process. If the regressors are set by intervention, say a policy that changes a state’s poverty rate, the observed association need not describe what happens (Chapter 25).

12.3.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that the ratio of the length of the prediction interval to that of the confidence interval for the mean at x0 is (1+h0)/h0. What is it in Example (Predicting a state’s murder rate), and what happens to it as n→∞ with the design growing regularly?

Solution. Both intervals use the multiplier tn−r,α/2s, with 1+h0 and h0 respectively. At the centroid, h0=0.0200 and the ratio is 51=7.14. As the design grows, h0→0 and the ratio tends to infinity: the confidence interval shrinks to a point while the prediction interval does not.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Two new observations will be made, at x01 and x02, with independent errors. Find an exact 1−α prediction interval for their difference Y01−Y02. Show that when x01=x02 it does not depend on the data except through s.

Solution. 𝛃Y01−Y02−(x01−x02)⊤β^ is normal with mean zero and variance σ2{2+(x01−x02)⊤G(x01−x02)}, independent of s2 by the argument of Theorem 12.3.1. The interval is 𝛃(x01−x02)⊤β^±tn−r,α/2s2+(x01−x02)⊤G(x01−x02). When the two points coincide it is ±tn−r,α/2s2: the difference of two new errors, for which the fitted model contributes only its estimate of σ.

Exercise (B2)

In Proposition 12.3.2(c), let X have full column rank and add a single column z∉C(X). Show that the new leverage is h0+=h0+(z0−k⊤x0)2‖(I−M)z‖2,k=(X⊤X)−1X⊤z. So the leverage is unchanged iff z0 equals the value k⊤x0 predicted for it by the other regressors.

Solution. Use Proposition 12.3.2(a) for the enlarged model. Write 𝛒x0=X⊤ρ and split any feasible a as a=Ma+b with b⊥C(X). The constraint X⊤a=x0 forces 𝛒Ma=Mρ, as in the proof of the proposition. The constraint z⊤a=z0 then reads 𝛒z⊤Mρ+z⊤b=z0, and 𝛒𝛒z⊤Mρ=z⊤X(X⊤X)−1X⊤ρ=k⊤x0. Since b⊥C(X), z⊤b=((I−M)z)⊤b, and by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality the shortest b with ((I−M)z)⊤b=z0−k⊤x0 is a multiple of (I−M)z, with ‖b‖2=(z0−k⊤x0)2/‖(I−M)z‖2. Adding 𝛒‖Mρ‖2=h0 gives the formula.

Exercise (B3)

For the line through the origin, EYi=βxi with ∑xi2>0, write the 1−α confidence interval for the mean at x0 and the prediction interval for Y0. Show that the confidence interval for the mean has length proportional to |x0|, and is a point at x0=0. Why is that reasonable, and what does it assume?

Solution. Here r=1, β^=∑xiyi/∑xi2, s2=∑(yi−β^xi)2/(n−1) and h0=x02/∑xi2. The intervals are β^x0±tn−1,α/2s|x0|/∑xi2 and β^x0±tn−1,α/2s1+x02/∑xi2. At x0=0 the model asserts that the mean is exactly zero, so there is nothing to estimate. The interval is only as good as that assertion: if the true line has a small intercept, the interval near x0=0 is too short.

Exercise (B4)

Suppose the new regressor vector X0 is random, independent of the pair (Y,ε0), and always lies in C(X⊤). Show that the prediction interval of Theorem 12.3.1, computed at the realized X0, still covers Y0 with probability exactly 1−α.

Solution. Condition on X0=x0. By independence, given this event (Y,ε0) has its unconditional distribution, so Theorem 12.3.1 applies and the conditional coverage is 1−α for every x0. Averaging over the distribution of X0 gives 1−α.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let k new observations be made at the rows of a k×p matrix X0 whose rows lie in C(X⊤), with errors independent of each other and of Y. Show that 𝛃D=Y0−X0β^∼Nk(0,σ2(Ik+X0GX0⊤)), independent of s2, and deduce that the intervals 𝛃x0j⊤β^ ± kFα(k,n−r) s1+h0j,j=1,…,k, contain all k new observations simultaneously with probability at least 1−α.

Solution. Normality, the mean and the covariance follow as in Theorem 12.3.1, and S=Ik+X0GX0⊤ is positive definite. So D⊤S−1D/(ks2)∼F(k,n−r) (Corollary 4.3.3 and Definition 4.1.2). By Lemma 12.2.3 with a=ej, the event D⊤S−1D≤ks2Fα implies |Dj|≤kFαsSjj for every j, and Sjj=1+h0j. Chapter 13 compares this with Bonferroni’s method, which is shorter when k is small.

Exercise (C2)

A (P,1−α) tolerance interval at x0 is a random interval that, with probability at least 1−α over Y, contains at least a proportion P of the distribution of Y0. Show that the prediction interval of Theorem 12.3.1 is not a (1−α,1−α) tolerance interval, by showing that its conditional coverage given Y is below 1−α with probability bounded away from zero. Construct a conservative tolerance interval from a lower confidence bound for the conditional coverage, using a 1−α/2 upper confidence bound for σ and a 1−α/2 confidence interval for 𝛃x0⊤β.

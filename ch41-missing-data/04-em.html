41.4  The EM algorithm

Maximizing the observed-data likelihood Eq. 41.3.2 directly means differentiating an integral. EM avoids that: it alternates between filling in the missing pieces of the complete-data log-likelihood and maximizing the filled-in version, and it never decreases the observed-data likelihood. The iteration of Theorem 18.6.1(c) — fill each lost plot with its fitted value, refit, repeat — is the oldest instance in this book.

41.4.1. The two steps

Let 𝛉𝛉ℓ(θ)=log⁡f(Dobs∣θ) and 𝛉𝛉ℓc(θ)=log⁡f(D∣θ) be the observed-data and complete-data log-likelihoods, the second a function of data we do not have. The mechanism is ignorable throughout, so Theorem 41.3.1 licenses working with ℓ.

Definition 41.4.1 (EM)

Given a current value 𝛉θ(s), define

𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉Q(θ∣θ(s))=E{ℓc(θ) | Dobs,θ(s)},
(41.4.1)

the expectation being over Dmis under the conditional density 𝛉f(Dmis∣Dobs,θ(s)). The E step computes 𝛉Q(⋅∣θ(s)); the M step sets 𝛉θ(s+1) to a maximizer of it. If the M step only increases Q, the scheme is called generalized EM.

The E step is not “fill in the missing values”; it fills in whatever functions of them the complete-data log-likelihood contains. When ℓc is linear in a set of sufficient statistics, as for every exponential family (Definition 34.1.1), the E step reduces to filling in those statistics (Exercise (B2)) — which is the same as filling in the missing values only when the statistics are linear in them. A normal variance, whose statistic contains ∑iyi2, is the standing counterexample, and only the second reading is EM.

41.4.2. Monotonicity

Theorem 41.4.1 (The EM sequence never decreases the likelihood)

Let 𝛉θ(s+1) satisfy 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉Q(θ(s+1)∣θ(s))≥Q(θ(s)∣θ(s)). Then

  • (a) 𝛉𝛉ℓ(θ(s+1))≥ℓ(θ(s)), with equality only if 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉Q(θ(s+1)∣θ(s))=Q(θ(s)∣θ(s)) and the two conditional densities of Dmis agree almost everywhere;

  • (b) if ℓ is bounded above, 𝛉ℓ(θ(s)) converges;

  • (c) if Q(⋅∣⋅) is differentiable in its first argument and 𝛉θ(s+1) is an interior maximizer, then at any fixed point 𝛉θ∗ of the iteration the observed-data score vanishes: 𝛉𝛉𝛉∂ℓ(θ)/∂θ|θ∗=0.

Proof. Write 𝛉𝛉g(Dmis∣θ)=f(Dmis∣Dobs,θ) for the conditional density of the missing part. Since 𝛉𝛉𝛉f(D∣θ)=f(Dobs∣θ)g(Dmis∣θ), taking logarithms and rearranging gives, for every Dmis, 𝛉𝛉𝛉ℓ(θ)=ℓc(θ)−log⁡g(Dmis∣θ). The left side does not depend on Dmis, so taking the expectation of both sides under 𝛉g(⋅∣θ(s)) leaves it unchanged and yields the decomposition

𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉ℓ(θ)=Q(θ∣θ(s))−H(θ∣θ(s)),whereH(θ∣θ(s))=E{log⁡g(Dmis∣θ) | Dobs,θ(s)}.
(41.4.2)

  • (a) Subtract Eq. 41.4.2 at 𝛉θ(s) from the same at 𝛉θ(s+1): 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉ℓ(θ(s+1))−ℓ(θ(s))={Q(θ(s+1)∣θ(s))−Q(θ(s)∣θ(s))}−{H(θ(s+1)∣θ(s))−H(θ(s)∣θ(s))}. The first brace is nonnegative by hypothesis. For the second, write 𝛉gs=g(⋅∣θ(s)) and 𝛉gs+1=g(⋅∣θ(s+1)); then 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉H(θ(s+1)∣θ(s))−H(θ(s)∣θ(s))=∫gslog⁡gs+1gs≤log⁡∫gsgs+1gs=log⁡1=0, by Jensen’s inequality applied to the concave logarithm. So the difference of log-likelihoods is a nonnegative number minus a nonpositive one. Equality throughout forces equality in Jensen’s inequality, which for the strictly concave logarithm requires gs+1/gs to be constant almost everywhere under gs, hence gs+1=gs, and forces the first brace to vanish.

  • (b) A nondecreasing sequence bounded above converges.

  • (c) At a fixed point, 𝛉θ∗ maximizes 𝛉Q(⋅∣θ∗), so 𝛉𝛉𝛉∂Q(θ∣θ∗)/∂θ vanishes at 𝛉𝛉θ=θ∗. The function 𝛉𝛉𝛉θ↦H(θ∣θ∗) is maximized at 𝛉𝛉θ=θ∗ by the Jensen argument just given, so its derivative vanishes there too. Differentiating Eq. 41.4.2 at 𝛉θ∗ gives 𝛉∂ℓ/∂θ=0−0.

Warning. Part (c) says fixed points are stationary points, not maxima: EM can converge to a saddle point or a local maximum, and with several modes the limit depends on the start. Wu (1983) gives conditions under which the limit points are stationary and under which the sequence converges; none rules out a local maximum. Start from several values.

41.4.3. Missing responses in a linear model

The oldest case is the one where every step is explicit, and it settles a question left open in Section 18.6.

Proposition 41.4.2 (EM for a normal linear model with missing responses)

Let 𝛃𝛆y=Xβ+ε with 𝛆ε∼Nn(0,σ2I) and rank(X)=p; let the responses of a set of m rows be unrecorded, the covariates of those rows being known, with an ignorable mechanism, and suppose the observed rows Xo have rank p. Write SSEo for the residual sum of squares of the least squares fit on the observed rows. Then:

  • (a) the E step replaces each missing yi by 𝛃x(i)⊤β(s) in the cross-product terms of ℓc, and each yi2 by 𝛃(x(i)⊤β(s))2+σ2(s);

  • (b) the M step is 𝛃β(s+1)=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y(s) with y(s) the completed vector, and 𝛃σ2(s+1)=n−1{‖y(s)−Xβ(s+1)‖2+mσ2(s)};

  • (c) the unique fixed point is 𝛃𝛃β∗=β^o, the least squares estimate from the observed rows, and

    σ2∗=SSEon−m,
    (41.4.3)

    which is the observed-data maximum likelihood estimate, based on n−m observations.

Proof.

  • (a) The complete-data log-likelihood 𝛃−12nlog⁡(2πσ2)−12σ2∑i(yi−x(i)⊤β)2 is quadratic in the yi, so its conditional expectation needs their first two conditional moments. Given the observed data an unrecorded yi is a draw from its own model, so those are 𝛃x(i)⊤β(s) and 𝛃(x(i)⊤β(s))2+σ2(s).

  • (b) With those substitutions, 𝛃Q=−12nlog⁡(2πσ2)−12σ2{‖y(s)−Xβ‖2+mσ2(s)}, because each missing row contributes 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃E(yi2)−2x(i)⊤βE(yi)+(x(i)⊤β)2=(x(i)⊤β(s)−x(i)⊤β)2+σ2(s). Maximizing in 𝛃β is ordinary least squares on y(s); maximizing in σ2 gives the stated average.

  • (c) At a fixed point the completed vector has 𝛃yi=x(i)⊤β∗ at the missing rows, so those rows have zero residual and contribute nothing to the least squares criterion; hence 𝛃β∗ minimizes ‖yo−Xob‖2 and equals 𝛃β^o, which is Theorem 18.6.1(a). Its residual sum of squares is SSEo, so the variance equation reads nσ2∗=SSEo+mσ2∗, giving Eq. 41.4.3. Uniqueness of 𝛃β∗ follows from Theorem 18.6.1(b), and Eq. 41.4.3 then determines σ2∗.

The term mσ2(s) is the whole point. Without it — fill the gaps with fitted values and hand the completed data to a regression routine — the variance comes out as SSEo/n. That is the error Theorem 18.6.1(e) warns against, now derived rather than asserted: the filled-in values are not data, and the likelihood knows it.

Example (The Mauna Loa gaps)

Fit the trend-plus-harmonics model of Section 5.3 to all 2284 weeks, with the 59 missing ones handled by EM. The coefficients agree with the complete-case fit to machine precision, as Proposition 41.4.2(c) requires, and the error standard deviation is 0.7992 ppm; filling in the gaps and treating them as data gives 0.7889 ppm, too small by the factor n/(n−m)=1.0132. The discrepancy is small here only because the gaps are few.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

co2 = sm.datasets.co2.load_pandas().data["co2"]
t = (co2.index.year + (co2.index.dayofyear - 0.5) / 365.25).to_numpy()
tc = t - 1980.0
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(t)), tc, tc**2,
                     np.cos(2 * np.pi * t), np.sin(2 * np.pi * t),
                     np.cos(4 * np.pi * t), np.sin(4 * np.pi * t)])
y = co2.to_numpy()
obs = ~np.isnan(y)
n, m, p = len(y), int((~obs).sum()), X.shape[1]

beta, sigma2 = np.zeros(p), 1.0                       # any starting value will do
for sweep in range(60):
    filled = np.where(obs, y, X @ beta)               # E step: gaps get their fitted values
    beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, filled, rcond=None)  # M step for the coefficients
    sse = float(np.sum((filled - X @ beta) ** 2))
    sigma2 = (sse + m * sigma2) / n                   # M step for the variance: add m*sigma^2
print(f"EM after {sweep + 1} sweeps: sigma-hat = {np.sqrt(sigma2):.4f} ppm")

beta_cc, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X[obs], y[obs], rcond=None)
sse_o = float(np.sum((y[obs] - X[obs] @ beta_cc) ** 2))
print(f"observed-data MLE:          {np.sqrt(sse_o / (n - m)):.4f} ppm")
print(f"treating the fills as data: {np.sqrt(sse_o / n):.4f} ppm")

41.4.4. A missing covariate

When a covariate is missing, the conditional distribution the E step needs runs backwards: what does a recorded response say about an unrecorded covariate? A model for the covariate is now unavoidable.

Proposition 41.4.3 (EM with a missing covariate)

Let xi∼N(μ,τ2) independently, and yi∣xi∼N(β0+β1xi,σ2), with 𝛉θ=(β0,β1,σ2,μ,τ2). Suppose yi is always recorded and xi is missing for a subset, with an ignorable mechanism. Then for a record with xi missing the E step uses

E(xi∣yi)=μ+β1τ2β12τ2+σ2(yi−β0−β1μ),Var(xi∣yi)=τ2−β12τ4β12τ2+σ2,
(41.4.4)

both evaluated at 𝛉θ(s), and the M step is the complete-data maximum likelihood fit with the sufficient statistics ∑ixi, ∑ixi2 and ∑ixiyi replaced by ∑iE(xi∣yi), ∑iE(xi2∣yi) and ∑iyiE(xi∣yi).

Proof. The pair (xi,yi) is bivariate normal, with Eyi=β0+β1μ, Varyi=β12τ2+σ2 and Cov(xi,yi)=β1τ2. The conditional moments Eq. 41.4.4 are the standard normal conditional mean and variance (Theorem 3.5.2). The complete-data log-likelihood is −n2log⁡σ2−∑i(yi−β0−β1xi)22σ2−n2log⁡τ2−∑i(xi−μ)22τ2+const, which is linear in the three listed statistics together with ∑iyi and ∑iyi2; taking conditional expectations therefore replaces exactly those statistics, and the maximizer is the complete-data one evaluated at them.

Note what the E step does not do: it does not impute xi by μ, nor from other covariates alone. It uses the response. Imputing a covariate without the response is among the commonest errors in practice, and it attenuates β^1 towards zero, for the reason Chapter 24 gives.

Example (An EM fit with a missing covariate)

Simulate N=400 records with β1=0.8, and let the covariate be missing more often when the response is large; this loses 169 covariate values. The mechanism is MAR — it uses only the recorded y — so likelihood is valid and the complete-case fit is not. EM gives β^1=0.825; the complete cases give 0.620, attenuated by a quarter.

Figure 41.4.1 shows the two faces of the algorithm. The observed-data log-likelihood climbs monotonically, as Theorem 41.4.1(a) requires, and the distance to the fixed point falls by the constant factor 0.760 per sweep, matching to three decimals the largest eigenvalue of Ic−1Im below. Reaching eight decimal places takes 68 sweeps — slow by the standards of Newton’s method, which is the price of never differentiating an integral.

rng = np.random.default_rng(4104)
N = 400
theta_true = dict(b0=1.0, b1=0.8, s2=1.0, mu=0.5, t2=1.0)
x = theta_true["mu"] + np.sqrt(theta_true["t2"]) * rng.normal(size=N)
y2 = theta_true["b0"] + theta_true["b1"] * x + np.sqrt(theta_true["s2"]) * rng.normal(size=N)
seen = rng.uniform(size=N) > 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(-0.4 + 2.0 * (y2 - y2.mean()))))
print(f"{N - seen.sum()} of {N} covariates missing, more often when y is large")


def estep(th, y, seen):
    """Conditional mean and second moment of the covariate given the response."""
    b0, b1, s2, mu, t2 = th
    vy = b1**2 * t2 + s2                              # marginal variance of y
    cond_mean = mu + (b1 * t2 / vy) * (y - b0 - b1 * mu)
    cond_var = t2 - (b1 * t2) ** 2 / vy
    ex = np.where(seen, x, cond_mean)
    ex2 = np.where(seen, x**2, cond_mean**2 + cond_var)
    return ex, ex2


def mstep(ex, ex2, y):
    mu, n_ = ex.mean(), len(y)
    t2 = ex2.mean() - mu**2
    sxx = ex2.sum() - n_ * mu**2
    sxy = (ex * y).sum() - n_ * mu * y.mean()
    b1 = sxy / sxx
    b0 = y.mean() - b1 * mu
    s2 = ((y**2).sum() - 2 * b0 * y.sum() - 2 * b1 * (ex * y).sum()
          + n_ * b0**2 + 2 * b0 * b1 * ex.sum() + b1**2 * ex2.sum()) / n_
    return np.array([b0, b1, s2, mu, t2])


def loglik(th, y, seen):
    """Observed-data log-likelihood: joint for complete rows, marginal for the rest."""
    b0, b1, s2, mu, t2 = th
    ll = -0.5 * np.sum(np.log(2 * np.pi * s2) + (y[seen] - b0 - b1 * x[seen]) ** 2 / s2)
    ll += -0.5 * np.sum(np.log(2 * np.pi * t2) + (x[seen] - mu) ** 2 / t2)
    vy = b1**2 * t2 + s2
    ll += -0.5 * np.sum(np.log(2 * np.pi * vy) + (y[~seen] - b0 - b1 * mu) ** 2 / vy)
    return ll


th = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0, 1.0])
path, iterates = [loglik(th, y2, seen)], [th]
for sweep in range(200):
    ex, ex2 = estep(th, y2, seen)
    th = mstep(ex, ex2, y2)
    path.append(loglik(th, y2, seen))
    iterates.append(th)
print("EM   b0, b1 =", np.round(th[:2], 4))

cc = np.polyfit(x[seen], y2[seen], 1)[::-1]           # complete cases only
print("complete cases:", np.round(cc, 4))

41.4.5. Rate of convergence, and the standard errors EM does not give

Both the speed of EM and its principal defect come from the same matrix. Define the complete-data information 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉Ic=−∂2Q(θ∣θ∗)/∂θ∂θ⊤ at 𝛉𝛉θ=θ∗, the observed information 𝛉𝛉Io=−∂2ℓ/∂θ∂θ⊤ there, and their difference Im=Ic−Io, the missing information.

Proposition 41.4.4 (The missing-information principle)

At any 𝛉θ, with Sc the complete-data score,

𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉∂ℓ∂θ=E(Sc∣Dobs,θ),Io=E(−∂Sc/∂θ⊤∣Dobs,θ)−Var(Sc∣Dobs,θ).
(41.4.5)

Consequently 𝛉Im=Var(Sc∣Dobs,θ) is nonnegative definite: the observed information is never larger than the expected complete-data information.

Proof. Differentiate the identity 𝛉𝛉𝛉ℓ(θ)=ℓc(θ)−log⁡g(Dmis∣θ) from the proof of Theorem 41.4.1 and take the conditional expectation under 𝛉g(⋅∣θ). The conditional score of g has mean zero, being the score of a density in its own parameter, so the first identity follows. Differentiating again, 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉−∂2ℓ∂θ∂θ⊤=−∂2ℓc∂θ∂θ⊤+∂2log⁡g∂θ∂θ⊤, and taking the conditional expectation turns the last term into minus the information of g, which by the usual information identity equals minus the variance of its score. Since the score of g is 𝛉Sc−∂ℓ/∂θ and the second term is a constant given the observed data, that variance is Var(Sc∣Dobs).

Identity Eq. 41.4.5 is Louis’s formula (Louis, 1982); “observed = complete minus missing” is the missing-information principle of Orchard and Woodbury (1972). Two consequences:

The rate. Near a fixed point the EM map is approximately linear with Jacobian I−Ic−1Io=Ic−1Im, so the iterates converge linearly at its largest eigenvalue — the largest fraction of missing information (Dempster, Laird and Rubin, 1977). In Example (An EM fit with a missing covariate) that eigenvalue is 0.760 and the measured rate is 0.760; the log-likelihood gap, quadratic near the maximum, falls at its square, 0.577 per sweep. In Theorem 18.6.1(c) the same matrix is Mmm, the submatrix of the hat matrix belonging to the lost plots.

The standard errors. The matrix EM manipulates is Ic, which is too large, so inverting it understates the variance by the fraction of missing information. Three honest routes: Eq. 41.4.5; numerical differentiation of ℓ, as this section’s script does; or the bootstrap, resampling records and rerunning EM (Definition 23.5.1). In Example (An EM fit with a missing covariate) these give 0.0611 and 0.0605 (bootstrap, 300 resamples), against 0.0512 from the complete-data information — an understatement of 16%.

Warning. The variance of an EM estimate is never the inverse of the matrix the M step inverts. If a routine reports standard errors after EM without saying how it got them, assume it has made this mistake.

41.4.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

The EM algorithm for the zero-inflated model of Section 37.5 treats the component label as the missing datum. Identify Dmis, Q and the M step there, and check that Exercise (B4) is the special case of Theorem 41.4.1(a).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In Proposition 41.4.2, suppose one carries out the iteration but omits the term mσ2(s) from the M step for the variance. Show that the coefficients are unaffected and that the variance converges to SSEo/n. Relate the discrepancy to Theorem 18.6.1(e).

Solution. The coefficient update does not involve σ2 at all, so 𝛃β(s) follows the same sequence and converges to 𝛃β^o; at that point the completed residual sum of squares is SSEo. The truncated variance update is σ2(s+1)=SSEo/n once the coefficients have settled. That is the complete-data formula applied to n “observations”, m of which have been manufactured to fit exactly; reducing the divisor by m is the degrees-of-freedom correction of Theorem 18.6.1(e).

Exercise (B2)

Let the complete-data model be an exponential dispersion family (Definition 34.1.1) with sufficient statistic t(D). Show that the E step reduces to computing 𝛉E{t(D)∣Dobs,θ(s)} and the M step to solving the complete-data likelihood equations with t replaced by that expectation. Why does the normal variance in Proposition 41.4.2 still require the extra term mσ2(s)?

Solution. The complete-data log-likelihood is 𝛉𝛉θ⊤t(D)−A(θ) plus a term free of 𝛉θ, so it is affine in t and its conditional expectation replaces t by E(t∣⋅). Setting the derivative to zero gives 𝛉Eθt=E(t∣Dobs). The normal case is no exception: the sufficient statistics include ∑iyi2, and its conditional expectation over a missing row is 𝛃(x(i)⊤β(s))2+σ2(s), which is where the extra term comes from. The trap is to substitute the conditional mean of the data rather than the conditional mean of the statistic.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Construct a two-component normal mixture with missing labels — the classical EM problem — for which the likelihood is unbounded, and show that EM started near a degenerate configuration drives one variance to zero while the likelihood increases at every step. Reconcile this with Theorem 41.4.1(b).

41.3  Likelihood under missingness at random

A complete-case analysis throws away recorded values: a unit whose income is missing still told us its age. Likelihood throws nothing away. It asks what the observed data were likely to be, and the observed data include the partial records. This section writes that likelihood down, proves the theorem that makes it usable, and says what the theorem costs.

41.3.1. The observed-data likelihood

The full model is Eq. 41.1.1: a density for the complete array and a mechanism. Since Dmis was never seen, the likelihood of what was seen integrates it out,

𝛉𝛙𝛉𝛙Lfull(θ,ψ)=∫f(Dobs,Dmis∣θ)f(R∣Dobs,Dmis,ψ)dDmis,
(41.3.1)

a function of both parameters. Beside it stands the much simpler

𝛉𝛉𝛉L(θ)=f(Dobs∣θ)=∫f(Dobs,Dmis∣θ)dDmis,
(41.3.2)

which ignores the mechanism entirely. The observed-data likelihood Eq. 41.3.2 is what software computes when told that some entries are absent. The question is when using it is the same as using Eq. 41.3.1.

Theorem 41.3.1 (Ignorability)

Suppose that

  • (i) the mechanism is MAR at the realized (R,Dobs) in the sense of Definition 41.1.1(b);

  • (ii) the parameters are distinct: the parameter space of 𝛉𝛙(θ,ψ) is a product set Θ×Ψ, so that knowing one places no restriction on the other.

Then the full likelihood factors,

𝛉𝛙𝛉𝛙Lfull(θ,ψ)=L(θ)⋅f(R∣Dobs,ψ),
(41.3.3)

and consequently:

  • (a) the maximizer of Lfull in 𝛉θ is the maximizer of L, and likelihood ratio statistics for hypotheses about 𝛉θ alone are the same under both;

  • (b) if the prior is 𝛉𝛙𝛉𝛙p(θ,ψ)=p(θ)p(ψ), the posterior of 𝛉θ given (Dobs,R) equals its posterior given Dobs alone, computed from Eq. 41.3.2;

  • (c) if in addition the MAR condition holds at every (R,Dobs), the observed-data score has mean zero and the usual maximum likelihood asymptotics (Theorem 7.3.1) apply to L under the ordinary regularity conditions.

A mechanism satisfying (i) and (ii) is called ignorable.

Proof. Under (i) the factor 𝛙f(R∣D,ψ) in Eq. 41.3.1 takes the same value for every Dmis, namely 𝛙f(R∣Dobs,ψ), so it comes out of the integral: 𝛉𝛙𝛙𝛉𝛙𝛉Lfull(θ,ψ)=f(R∣Dobs,ψ)∫f(Dobs,Dmis∣θ)dDmis=f(R∣Dobs,ψ)L(θ), which is Eq. 41.3.3.

  • (a) By (ii), 𝛉θ ranges over Θ whatever 𝛙ψ is, so 𝛙𝛉𝛙𝛙maxψLfull=L(θ)maxψf(R∣Dobs,ψ), and the second factor does not involve 𝛉θ. Maximizing over 𝛉θ therefore maximizes L. For a hypothesis 𝛉θ∈Θ0, both the numerator and the denominator of the likelihood ratio carry the same second factor, which cancels.

  • (b) The posterior is proportional to 𝛉𝛙p(θ)p(ψ)Lfull. By Eq. 41.3.3 this is 𝛉𝛉𝛙𝛙{p(θ)L(θ)}⋅{p(ψ)f(R∣Dobs,ψ)}, a product of a function of 𝛉θ and a function of 𝛙ψ. Integrating out 𝛙ψ multiplies the first bracket by a constant, so the marginal posterior of 𝛉θ is proportional to 𝛉𝛉p(θ)L(θ).

  • (c) If (i) holds at every realization, then at every realization the joint density of the observed data (Dobs,R) is exactly 𝛉𝛙L(θ)f(R∣Dobs,ψ), whose logarithm is 𝛉log⁡L(θ) plus a term free of 𝛉θ. So 𝛉∂log⁡L/∂θ is the 𝛉θ component of the score of a correctly specified density for the data actually seen, and has mean zero under the ordinary regularity conditions of Theorem 7.3.1.

Remark (Where each hypothesis is used). Condition (i) alone gives Eq. 41.3.3 and part (b): a Bayesian who conditions on the data in hand needs nothing more. Condition (ii) is what makes the factorization useful to a frequentist, who maximizes; without it the two factors share parameters and neither can be maximized alone. Part (c) needs the everywhere version of MAR, because a sampling distribution averages over data sets that did not occur. Textbooks usually state “MAR plus distinctness” without separating these roles; the separation is due to Seaman, Galati, Jackson and Carlin (2013).

Idea. Under MAR with distinct parameters, the analyst may forget the mechanism and maximize the likelihood of what was recorded. That is the entire licence for the methods of the next two sections — and it is a licence to use a likelihood that is usually an integral, not a product.

41.3.2. What the licence costs

Ignorability licenses ignoring the mechanism, not ignoring the missing values. Three consequences follow.

A model is now needed for variables previously conditioned on. In a complete data set, least squares never models the distribution of X: the likelihood conditions on it. When a covariate is missing, Eq. 41.3.2 integrates over it, and that integral needs a density. Missing responses cost nothing extra; missing covariates cost a model for the covariates.

When only the response is missing, the partial records add nothing. If every covariate is recorded, the integral in Eq. 41.3.2 over a missing yi is one, and the observed-data likelihood is exactly the complete-case likelihood. This is Proposition 41.2.1(b) again, as a statement about likelihoods, and it is the situation of Theorem 18.6.1.

The likelihood is no longer a product of simple factors. Each incomplete record contributes a marginal density, obtained by integrating the complete-data density over the entries it lacks. For the multivariate normal those integrals are marginals of a normal; for almost anything else they have no closed form. Section 41.4 is the standard response.

For a monotone pattern the integrals are free: integrating out a record’s unrecorded tail deletes exactly its conditional factors, so the likelihood becomes a chain of conditional models on nested subsamples, each maximized on its own; that is Exercise (B1) of Section 41.1. The bivariate case is the subject in miniature. With u always recorded and v recorded on the first k units, the estimate of μv is v¯(k)+β^{u¯(n)−u¯(k)} by Exercise (B1) below: the complete-case mean of v, corrected by the shift in the mean of u, using the n−k records that carry a u and no v. Those records were useless to the complete-case analysis and are decisive here — and they repair the damage only to the extent that u predicts v, a remark that returns, with teeth, in Section 41.5. Anderson (1957) solved the general monotone multivariate normal case this way.

41.3.3. What changes under MNAR

Drop the MAR assumption and Eq. 41.3.3 fails: the integral in Eq. 41.3.1 cannot release the mechanism, and the two parameters are entangled. The mechanism must then be modelled, and the model is not checkable, since Proposition 41.1.1 says that varying the assumed conditional distribution of the missing values traces out a family with identical observed-data likelihoods. Whatever identification such a model achieves comes from functional-form assumptions rather than from the data, and the likelihood surface is often nearly flat in the directions that matter. Section 41.6 takes this up as a problem of reporting rather than of estimation.

41.3.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Give a mechanism under which MAR holds but the parameters are not distinct, and show that Eq. 41.3.3 still holds while part (a) of Theorem 41.3.1 can fail. (Hint: let the probability of recording yi be a known function of 𝛃β.)

Solution. Take 𝛃Pr(Ri=1∣x(i))=Φ(x(i)⊤β), with no free 𝛙ψ at all. The mechanism depends only on recorded covariates, so MAR holds and the factorization Eq. 41.3.3 is valid, but the second factor now depends on 𝛃β. Maximizing the product is not the same as maximizing 𝛃L(β), and the indicators carry information about 𝛃β: the parameter spaces are not a product set.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let (ui,vi) be a bivariate normal sample with u recorded for all n units and v for the first k, the mechanism depending on u but not on v. Reparameterizing by (μu,σu2) and the regression parameters of v on u, derive the maximum likelihood estimates and the formula for μ^v quoted above. Extend to three variables with a monotone pattern: u recorded n times, v the first k2, z the first k3≤k2.

Solution. Factor as f(u)f(v∣u); by Exercise (B1) an unrecorded vi loses its conditional factor, so L=∏i≤nf(ui)∏i≤kf(vi∣ui). The two parameter groups are variation independent, so each product is maximized alone: a normal sample of size n, and a normal linear model on k observations (Theorem 7.3.1). Since μv=α+βμu and α^=v¯(k)−β^u¯(k), and since a maximum likelihood estimate transforms with the parameterization, μ^v is as stated. For three variables the same argument on f(u)f(v∣u)f(z∣u,v) gives a univariate fit on all n, the regression of v on u over the first k2, and the regression of z on (u,v) over the first k3.

Exercise (B2)

A linear model has a single covariate x that is sometimes missing, with x∼N(μ,τ2) and y∣x∼N(β0+β1x,σ2). Write the observed-data likelihood contribution of a record with y recorded and x missing, and show that it is the density of a normal with mean β0+β1μ and variance β12τ2+σ2. Why does this contribution carry information about β1 even though it contains no x?

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that Theorem 18.6.1 is the special case of Theorem 41.3.1 in which the missing entries are responses, the mechanism depends only on the design, and the complete-data model is the normal linear model. Which part of Theorem 18.6.1 corresponds to Eq. 41.3.2, and which to the warning that the degrees of freedom must be reduced?

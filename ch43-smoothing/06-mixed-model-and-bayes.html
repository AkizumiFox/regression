43.6  Mixed-model and Bayesian representations

The criteria of Section 43.5 treat λ as a tuning constant to be optimized. There is another way to think about it. A quadratic penalty is, up to a constant, the logarithm of a normal density, so penalizing 𝛄𝛄γ⊤Pγ is the same as believing in advance that 𝛄γ is a draw from a normal distribution with precision proportional to P. Then λ is a ratio of two variances, and a variance is not a tuning constant but a parameter the data can estimate. The machinery of Chapter 32 becomes available, with a second dividend: an interval whose width knows about the bias.

43.6.1. Splitting the penalty

The obstacle is that P is singular. A normal prior with precision matrix P/τ2 constrains nothing on the k-dimensional null space N(P) — the straight lines, for a second-order penalty — so it is not proper. The fix is to split 𝛄γ into the part the penalty does not see, a fixed effect, and the rest, a random effect with a proper distribution.

Theorem 43.6.1 (A penalized spline is a linear mixed model)

Let P be nonnegative definite of order m and rank r=m−k, write P=LL⊤ with L of size m×r and full column rank, let Xγ be m×k with columns spanning N(P), and set Zγ=L(L⊤L)−1.

  • (a) (The transformation.) [Xγ,Zγ] is nonsingular, so every 𝛄γ is uniquely 𝛄𝛃γ=Xγβ+Zγu; moreover 𝛄u=L⊤γ and 𝛄𝛄γ⊤Pγ=u⊤u.

  • (b) (The mixed model.) With X=BXγ and Z=BZγ, the P-spline criterion Eq. 43.3.1 is

    𝛃‖y−Xβ−Zu‖2+λu⊤u,
    (43.6.1)

    which is the penalized criterion Eq. 32.3.6 of the linear mixed model 𝛃𝛆Y=Xβ+Zu+ε, u∼Nr(0,σu2I), 𝛆ε∼Nn(0,σ2I) independent, with

    λ=σ2/σu2.
    (43.6.2)

    Its solution is Henderson’s (Theorem 32.3.2): 𝛃β^ is the generalized least squares estimate and u^ the BLUP (Theorem 32.3.1).

  • (c) (The Bayesian reading.) Suppose σ2 is known and 𝛄γ has the prior with density proportional to 𝛄𝛄exp⁡{−γ⊤Pγ/(2τ2)}, τ2=σ2/λ — equivalently 𝛃β flat and u∼Nr(0,τ2I). Then

    𝛄𝛄γ∣Y=y ∼ Nm{γ^, σ2(B⊤B+λP)−1},
    (43.6.3)

    with 𝛄γ^ the penalized estimate of Proposition 43.3.2(a).

  • (d) (REML.) The marginal distribution is 𝛃Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2Vλ) with Vλ=I+λ−1ZZ⊤. Profiling σ2 out of the restricted likelihood (Definition 32.4.1, Theorem 32.4.2) leaves

    −2ℓR(λ)=(n−k)log⁡σ^λ2+log⁡|Vλ|+log⁡|X⊤Vλ−1X|+c,
    (43.6.4)

    where σ^λ2=y⊤Aλy/(n−k) with Aλ=Vλ−1−Vλ−1X(X⊤Vλ−1X)−1X⊤Vλ−1, and c does not depend on λ. Minimizing Eq. 43.6.4 over λ>0 estimates the smoothing parameter.

Proof.

  • (a) From P=LL⊤ we get 𝛄Pγ=0 iff 𝛄𝛄𝛄‖L⊤γ‖2=γ⊤Pγ=0 iff 𝛄L⊤γ=0, so N(P)=N(L⊤)=C(L)⊥ and C(Zγ)=C(L)=N(P)⊥. Hence C(Xγ) and C(Zγ) are orthogonal subspaces of dimensions k and r that together span Rm, so [Xγ,Zγ] has rank m and the decomposition is unique. Multiplying 𝛄𝛃γ=Xγβ+Zγu by L⊤ gives 𝛄L⊤γ=0+L⊤L(L⊤L)−1u=u, and then 𝛄𝛄𝛄γ⊤Pγ=‖L⊤γ‖2=u⊤u.

  • (b) Substituting 𝛄𝛃Bγ=Xβ+Zu and (a) into Eq. 43.3.1 gives Eq. 43.6.1. In the mixed model with G=σu2I and R=σ2I, Henderson’s criterion is 𝛃σ−2‖y−Xβ−Zu‖2+σu−2u⊤u, which is σ−2 times Eq. 43.6.1 when λ=σ2/σu2; the two have the same minimizers.

  • (c) The prior is flat in 𝛃β and normal in u, so the joint density of 𝛄(y,γ) is proportional to 𝛄𝛄𝛄exp⁡[−{‖y−Bγ‖2+λγ⊤Pγ}/(2σ2)]. By the algebra in the proof of Proposition 43.3.2(a), the exponent is 𝛄𝛄𝛄𝛄−{(γ−γ^)⊤(B⊤B+λP)(γ−γ^)+C}/(2σ2) with C free of 𝛄γ. As a function of 𝛄γ this is the normal density Eq. 43.6.3 (Definition 39.4.1).

  • (d) Cov(Y)=σu2ZZ⊤+σ2I=σ2Vλ by Eq. 32.2.2, and Theorem 32.4.2 gives the restricted log likelihood −12{(n−k)log⁡σ2+log⁡|Vλ|+log⁡|X⊤Vλ−1X|+y⊤Aλy/σ2} up to a constant. Differentiating in σ2 gives σ^λ2=y⊤Aλy/(n−k), and substituting it back gives Eq. 43.6.4.

Idea. The same algebra has now been read three ways. A quadratic penalty is a shrinkage rule (Theorem 27.2.2), a normal prior (Proposition 39.5.1) and a variance component. Only the third makes the tuning constant estimable, because only the third supplies a probability model for the coefficients and with it a likelihood in λ. Nothing is obtained for nothing: that model says the true f is a draw from a particular distribution of curves, a stronger assumption than “f is smooth”.

Remark (What the transformation looks like). For a second-order difference penalty, N(D2) is spanned by 1 and (1,…,m)⊤, so the two fixed-effect columns of X=BXγ span the constant and the linear function (Proposition 43.3.3(c)): the mixed model has a straight line as its fixed part and the deviations from it as its random part — the decomposition a data analyst would choose by hand, forced by the penalty rather than assumed. Other choices of L and Xγ give the same fitted values, since they differ by a nonsingular reparameterization (Theorem 6.7.1).

43.6.2. Bands that know about the bias

Proposition 43.5.1(e) left a problem: the obvious interval is centred at E{f^(xi)}, which is not f(xi). The posterior Eq. 43.6.3 offers a different interval, and it is wider.

Proposition 43.6.2 (Wahba–Nychka bands)

Let Sλ=B(B⊤B+λP)−1B⊤ with diagonal entries sii.

  • (a) Under Theorem 43.6.1(c), 𝛄f(xi)=Bi⋅γ has posterior distribution N(f^(xi),σ2sii), giving the Bayesian band

    f^(xi) ± z1−α/2σ^sii.
    (43.6.5)

  • (b) sii≥(SλSλ⊤)ii, so Eq. 43.6.5 is never narrower than the interval of Proposition 43.5.1(e), and is strictly wider unless Sλ is a projection.

  • (c) (Stated, not proved.) Treating f as fixed, the extra width in (b) accounts approximately for the squared bias, in the sense that n−1∑iσ2sii approximates n−1∑i[Var{f^(xi)}+bias{f^(xi)}2] when λ is near its risk-optimal value. Consequently the average over the design points of the coverage of Eq. 43.6.5 is close to 1−α, while the coverage at any single xi is not: it is below nominal where f curves sharply and above it where f is nearly straight. This is across-the-function coverage (Wahba, 1983; Nychka, 1988).

Proof.

  • (a) By Eq. 43.6.3, 𝛄Bi⋅γ is normal with mean 𝛄Bi⋅γ^=f^(xi) and variance σ2Bi⋅(B⊤B+λP)−1Bi⋅⊤=σ2sii.

  • (b) Sλ is symmetric with eigenvalues sj∈(0,1] by Proposition 43.3.2(b), so Sλ−Sλ2 is symmetric with eigenvalues sj(1−sj)≥0 and hence nonnegative definite; its ith diagonal entry sii−(SλSλ⊤)ii is therefore nonnegative, and zero for all i only if the matrix is zero, that is only if every sj∈{0,1}.

Part (c) is not proved here; see the notes.

Warning. “Across-the-function” is a weaker guarantee than it sounds. Count how often the band contains the true curve across the design points and the fraction is about 1−α. It does not say that the band contains the curve everywhere with probability 1−α — that is a simultaneous band, which is wider (Sun and Loader, 1994) — nor that the interval at any one chosen point has nominal coverage. If a particular x is the question, Eq. 43.6.5 is not the answer.

Example (Measuring the coverage)

Take n=120 equally spaced points, the mean function of Example (Bias, variance and the optimal bandwidth) and σ=0.25, with a cubic P-spline on K=20 knots, so m=24 coefficients split into k=2 fixed and r=22 random ones. On one data set the transformation of Theorem 43.6.1 reproduces the penalized fit to machine precision, REML chooses λ=5.01×100, which is 8.12 effective degrees of freedom, against 7.10 for GCV; the REML estimate of σ is 0.254.

Over 400 replicates, with λ re-estimated by REML each time, the nominal 95 per cent band Eq. 43.6.5 covers f(xi) at a fraction 0.954 of the design points on average, the pointwise coverages running from 0.900 at the narrow dip near x=0.8, where the fit is most biased, to 0.985 on the flat stretches (Figure 43.6.1). The Bayesian standard errors are on average 1.12 times the naive ones of Proposition 43.5.1(e), whose bands cover only 0.927 of the design points: a twelve per cent widening buys back nearly three percentage points of average coverage, and what is left over is the bias that no single width can equalize.

import numpy as np


def f_true(x):
    """The mean function of Section 43.1."""
    return 2 * x + np.exp(-25 * (x - 0.35) ** 2) - 0.75 * np.exp(-50 * (x - 0.80) ** 2)


def uniform_knots(a, b, K, d):
    """K interior knots equally spaced in (a, b), continued d further steps at each end."""
    delta = (b - a) / (K + 1)
    return a + delta * np.arange(-d, K + 2 + d)


def bspline_basis(x, kn, d):
    """The B-splines of degree d on the knot vector kn, by the de Boor recurrence."""
    x = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(x, float))
    B = np.array([(x >= kn[j]) & (x < kn[j + 1]) for j in range(len(kn) - 1)], float).T
    last = np.max(np.nonzero(kn[:-1] < kn[1:])[0])
    B[x == kn[-1], last] = 1.0
    for deg in range(1, d + 1):
        C = np.zeros((len(x), B.shape[1] - 1))
        for j in range(C.shape[1]):
            if kn[j + deg] > kn[j]:
                C[:, j] += (x - kn[j]) / (kn[j + deg] - kn[j]) * B[:, j]
            if kn[j + deg + 1] > kn[j + 1]:
                C[:, j] += (kn[j + deg + 1] - x) / (kn[j + deg + 1] - kn[j + 1]) * B[:, j + 1]
        B = C
    return B


def split_penalty(P, tol=1e-9):
    """Bases X_g of N(P) and Z_g of its complement with Z_g' P Z_g = I and X_g' P = 0."""
    e, U = np.linalg.eigh(P)
    pos = e > tol * e.max()
    L = U[:, pos] * np.sqrt(e[pos])                    # P = L L' with L of full column rank
    Xg = U[:, ~pos]                                    # the null space of the penalty
    Zg = L @ np.linalg.inv(L.T @ L)
    return Xg, Zg


n, sigma, K, d, k = 120, 0.25, 20, 3, 2
x = (np.arange(1, n + 1) - 0.5) / n
kn = uniform_knots(0.0, 1.0, K, d)
B = bspline_basis(x, kn, d)
m = B.shape[1]
Dk = np.diff(np.eye(m), n=k, axis=0)
P = Dk.T @ Dk
Xg, Zg = split_penalty(P)
X, Z = B @ Xg, B @ Zg
r = Z.shape[1]

rng = np.random.default_rng(431)
g = rng.normal(size=m)                                           # any coefficient vector
beta_u = np.linalg.solve(np.column_stack([Xg, Zg]), g)
print("the penalty, in the old and the new coordinates:", g @ P @ g, beta_u[k:] @ beta_u[k:])

y = f_true(x) + sigma * rng.normal(size=n)
lam = 1.0
fit_pen = B @ np.linalg.solve(B.T @ B + lam * P, B.T @ y)
C = np.block([[X.T @ X, X.T @ Z], [Z.T @ X, Z.T @ Z + lam * np.eye(r)]])   # Henderson's equations
sol = np.linalg.solve(C, np.concatenate([X.T @ y, Z.T @ y]))
fit_mix = X @ sol[:k] + Z @ sol[k:]
print("penalized fit versus mixed-model fit:", np.max(np.abs(fit_pen - fit_mix)))
Uz, sz, _ = np.linalg.svd(Z, full_matrices=False)     # the design is fixed, so do this once


def reml(y, lam):
    """Profiled REML criterion (to be minimized) for V = I + Z Z' / lam."""
    w = sz**2 / (lam + sz**2)
    def Vi(M):                                         # multiplication by V^{-1}
        M2 = M if M.ndim == 2 else M[:, None]
        out = M2 - Uz @ (w[:, None] * (Uz.T @ M2))
        return out if M.ndim == 2 else out[:, 0]

    logdetV = np.sum(np.log1p(sz**2 / lam))
    ViX = Vi(X)
    A = X.T @ ViX
    resid = Vi(y) - ViX @ np.linalg.solve(A, ViX.T @ y)
    s2 = y @ resid / (n - k)
    return 0.5 * ((n - k) * np.log(s2) + logdetV + np.linalg.slogdet(A)[1]), s2


def reml_lambda(y, grid):
    return grid[int(np.argmin([reml(y, l)[0] for l in grid]))]


grid = np.geomspace(1e-6, 1e6, 121)
lam_reml = reml_lambda(y, grid)
S_reml = B @ np.linalg.solve(B.T @ B + lam_reml * P, B.T)
df_reml = np.trace(S_reml)
gcvs = []
for l in grid:
    S = B @ np.linalg.solve(B.T @ B + l * P, B.T)
    gcvs.append(np.mean((y - S @ y) ** 2) / (1 - np.trace(S) / n) ** 2)
lam_gcv = grid[int(np.argmin(gcvs))]
df_gcv = np.trace(B @ np.linalg.solve(B.T @ B + lam_gcv * P, B.T))
print(f"REML: lambda = {lam_reml:.3g}, df = {df_reml:.2f}; GCV: df = {df_gcv:.2f}")

43.6.3. REML against the criteria, and full Bayes

REML and GCV estimate different things. GCV aims at prediction error; REML maximizes a likelihood under the assumption that f is a draw from the prior — which nobody believes, since nobody believes the deviations from a straight line are exchangeable normal increments. In practice the two rarely differ by much: in Example (Measuring the coverage) REML chose 8.12 effective degrees of freedom against GCV’s 7.10. But REML is the more stable, its criterion better determined near the optimum, and it almost never produces the interpolating fits that GCV occasionally does (Reiss and Ogden, 2009). It has no answer when the error model is wrong: with the AR(1) errors of Example (What correlated errors do) it overfits just as GCV does, unless the correlation is put into 𝛆Cov(ε), which the mixed-model form at least makes possible (Definition 33.4.1).

Remark (Full Bayes). Theorem 43.6.1(c) fixes λ. A fully Bayesian treatment puts a prior on τ2 and σ2 too — conventionally inverse-gamma, which is conditionally conjugate — and samples 𝛄(γ,τ2,σ2) by the Gibbs sampler of Definition 39.4.2: each sweep draws 𝛄γ from Eq. 43.6.3, then τ2 from an inverse-gamma with shape a+r/2 and scale b+u⊤u/2, then σ2 likewise. The posterior for f then averages over the smoothing parameter instead of conditioning on an estimate of it, the cleanest answer to the selection problem of Section 43.5. It is also where the prior shows its weakness: the posterior for τ2 is sensitive to the hyperparameters, as Proposition 39.5.1 warns. Chapter 44 uses this machinery for several smooth terms at once.

43.6.4. What this book does not do with smoothing

Three threads end here rather than continue, and it is only fair to name them.

Adaptive smoothing. A single λ is a compromise when f is rough in one place and flat in another; letting it vary with x — a second smooth for log⁡λ, free-knot splines (Denison, Mallick and Smith, 1998), wavelet thresholding (Donoho and Johnstone, 1994) — is a subject this book does not enter.

Simultaneous bands. Eq. 43.6.5 is pointwise, or at best across-the-function; a band containing the whole curve with a stated probability needs the tube formula of Sun and Loader (1994) or a bootstrap over the whole fit.

Shape constraints. Monotone, convex or unimodal fits add linear inequality constraints to Eq. 43.3.1, a quadratic program; Ramsay (1988) treats the monotone case.

What does continue is the penalty. Everything after Section 43.2 rests on one idea — a rich basis, a quadratic penalty, a trace that counts what was spent, and a variance ratio that can be estimated — and that idea is indifferent to how many covariates there are, to whether the response is normal, and to which parameter of the response distribution is modelled. Chapter 44 adds covariates and Chapter 45 parameters, both by writing down more terms of the form Eq. 43.3.1.

43.6.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Using Eq. 43.6.2, say what σu2→0 and σu2→∞ correspond to in Proposition 43.3.2, and what a REML estimate σ^u2=0 would mean about the fitted curve. Is that estimate on the boundary of the parameter space, and does Theorem 32.5.2 apply?

Solution. σu2→0 gives λ→∞, leaving only the fixed part, the straight line; σu2→∞ gives λ→0, the unpenalized spline fit. A REML estimate of zero means the data give no evidence of departure from a straight line, and the reported fit is the least squares line. It is on the boundary, so a likelihood ratio test of σu2=0 has not the usual χ2 null distribution but the mixture of Theorem 32.5.2 — the same caution as for testing whether a smooth term is needed at all.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Run the simulation of Example (The three criteria on one data set, and over 200 of them) again with λ chosen by REML, and compare the median selected degrees of freedom, the average squared error and the upper tail of the df distribution with the values for CV, GCV and AICc. Which criterion is safest, and at what cost?

Exercise (B2)

Example (Measuring the coverage) uses σ^ from Eq. 43.5.2 in Eq. 43.6.5. Repeat the coverage simulation with σ known, and with z1−α/2 replaced by a t quantile on n−2tr(S)+tr(SS⊤) degrees of freedom. How much of the departure from nominal coverage is due to estimating σ, and how much to the bias?

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that in the mixed model of Theorem 43.6.1(b), the BLUP satisfies 𝛃u^=(Z⊤Z+λI)−1Z⊤(y−Xβ^), and hence that u^ is a ridge estimate (Theorem 27.2.2) on the residuals from the fixed part. Explain, using Theorem 32.3.1, why the shrinkage factor is σu2/(σu2+σ2/dj2) in the jth singular direction of Z, and what happens to it as n grows with r fixed.

Solution. The second block of Henderson’s equations is 𝛃Z⊤Xβ^+(Z⊤Z+λI)u^=Z⊤y, which rearranges to the displayed formula; it is Eq. 27.2.1 with response 𝛃y−Xβ^ and ridge parameter λ=σ2/σu2. With Z=∑jdjujvj⊤, Theorem 27.2.2(a) gives the factor dj2/(dj2+λ)=σu2/{σu2+σ2/dj2}, which is Eq. 32.3.3: the random effect is shrunk according to how much information the data carry about it. As n grows with the basis fixed, every dj2 grows like n and the factors tend to one, which is why a low-rank smoother with a fixed basis is asymptotically a parametric fit unless m grows with n.

Exercise (C2)

The prior of Theorem 43.6.1(c) is improper on N(P). Show that the posterior Eq. 43.6.3 is nevertheless proper, provided BXγ has full column rank. By Exercise (C2) that holds for k≤2 whenever the design has at least k distinct points, however the knots fall; show that it fails when it has fewer — for k=2, a design concentrated at a single x — and say what the posterior then does in the unidentified direction. Compare with the discussion of improper priors in Section 39.5.

43.5  Choosing the smoothing parameter

Four smoothers have appeared: the local constant and local linear estimators of Section 43.1, the penalized spline of Section 43.3 and the smoothing spline of Section 43.4. Each is linear in y, each has one dial, and none has said how to set it. The rules of this section approximate a risk that cannot be observed: they agree when the sample is large and the errors independent, and fail in recognisably different ways when it is not.

43.5.1. Every smoother here is a linear operator

Proposition 43.5.1 (Linear smoothers)

Call f^=Sy a linear smoother with smoother matrix S when S does not depend on y. The estimators of Definition 43.1.1, Definition 43.3.1 and Theorem 43.4.2 are linear smoothers, with S given by Eq. 43.1.4, Eq. 43.3.2 and Eq. 43.4.2. Suppose E(Y)=f and Cov(Y)=σ2I. Then:

  • (a) (Moments.) E(f^)=Sf, Cov(f^)=σ2SS⊤, and

    E‖f^−f‖2=‖(I−S)f‖2+σ2tr(SS⊤).
    (43.5.1)

  • (b) (Residuals.) E‖y−f^‖2=‖(I−S)f‖2+σ2{n−2tr(S)+tr(SS⊤)}, so

    σ^2=‖y−f^‖2n−2tr(S)+tr(SS⊤)
    (43.5.2)

    is unbiased for σ2 when the bias term is negligible.

  • (c) (Degrees of freedom.) If S is symmetric with eigenvalues in [0,1] — as it is for a penalized or smoothing spline, by Proposition 43.3.2(b) — then tr(SS⊤)≤tr(S)≤2tr(S)−tr(SS⊤), with equality throughout iff S is an orthogonal projection, in which case all three equal its rank.

  • (d) (Shrinkage.) For a penalized spline, Eq. 43.3.2 writes Sλ in the Demmler–Reinsch basis as a diagonal shrinkage uj⊤y↦uj⊤y/(1+λej): smooth directions (ej small) pass through, rough ones are damped. The smoothing spline has the same form with ej the eigenvalues of the roughness matrix relative to the identity.

  • (e) (Pointwise bands.) f^(xi)±z1−α/2σ^(SS⊤)ii is an interval for E{f^(xi)}=(Sf)i, not for f(xi). Its coverage of f(xi) is 1−α only to the extent that the bias {(S−I)f}i is small next to σ(SS⊤)ii.

Proof. Linearity is Proposition 43.1.1 for the kernel estimators, Proposition 43.3.2(a) for the penalized spline and Eq. 43.4.2 for the smoothing spline; in each case the matrix is built from the design, the knots and the tuning constant alone.

  • (a) E(SY)=Sf and Cov(SY)=SCov(Y)S⊤. For the risk, 𝛆f^−f=(S−I)f+Sε, the two terms are uncorrelated, and 𝛆E‖Sε‖2=σ2tr(SS⊤).

  • (b) 𝛆y−f^=(I−S)f+(I−S)ε, and 𝛆E‖(I−S)ε‖2=σ2tr{(I−S)(I−S)⊤}=σ2{n−2tr(S)+tr(SS⊤)}.

  • (c) With eigenvalues sj∈[0,1] we have tr(S)=∑jsj and tr(SS⊤)=∑jsj2, while sj2≤sj≤2sj−sj2, the last inequality because (1−sj)2≥0. Equality in either needs every sj∈{0,1}, which for a symmetric matrix means S is idempotent (Theorem 6.3.3); its trace is then its rank (Proposition 6.3.4).

  • (d) is Eq. 43.3.2; for the smoothing spline apply Lemma 43.3.1 with B=I and the roughness matrix of Proposition 43.4.3(b) in place of P.

  • (e) By (a), f^(xi) has mean (Sf)i and standard deviation σ{(SS⊤)ii}1/2, so under normal errors the interval covers that mean with probability 1−α if σ^ is replaced by σ. Writing f(xi)=(Sf)i−{(S−I)f}i shows that its coverage of f(xi) is the normal probability of an interval displaced by the bias.

Warning. Part (e) is the most important caveat in this chapter, and the one most often ignored. A smoother is deliberately biased; that is what the penalty buys. The interval above treats the bias as zero, so where f curves most — a peak, a trough, the boundary — it is centred in the wrong place and undercovers, while on flat stretches it overcovers. Section 43.6 gives a band that repairs the average of this, and says what “average” means.

43.5.2. Three rules

The quantity one would like to minimize is the risk Eq. 43.5.1, which involves the unknown f. Each rule estimates something close to it.

Theorem 43.5.2 (Cross-validation, generalized cross-validation and AIC for smoothers)

Let Sλ be the smoother matrix, f^=Sλy, df=tr(Sλ) and sii the diagonal entries of Sλ — the leverage of Proposition 6.8.1 and Section 20.2, read now for a smoother instead of a projection.

  • (a) (The leave-one-out shortcut is exact.) For a penalized spline and for a smoothing spline,

    CV(λ)=1n∑i=1n{yi−f^(−i)(xi)}2=1n∑i=1n(yi−f^(xi)1−sii)2,
    (43.5.3)

    where f^(−i) is the fit computed without case i at the same λ. The same identity holds for a local polynomial estimator, with sii=ℓi(xi).

  • (b) (Generalized cross-validation.) Replacing each sii by the average df/n gives Definition 29.3.2,

    GCV(λ)=n−1‖y−f^‖2(1−df/n)2,
    (43.5.4)

    which needs only the trace, and satisfies GCV=n−1‖y−f^‖2(1+2df/n)+O(df2/n2).

  • (c) (Information criteria.) Under normal errors with σ2 profiled out, Proposition 29.2.4 gives AIC(λ)=nlog⁡(‖y−f^‖2/n)+2df. Its small-sample correction (Hurvich, Simonoff and Tsai, 1998) is

    AICc(λ)=log⁡(‖y−f^‖2n)+1+2(df+1)n−df−2,
    (43.5.5)

    which penalizes large df much more heavily and has a pole at df=n−2.

  • (d) (What they estimate.) If Cov(Y)=σ2I, then E{CV(λ)} is, up to O(n−1) terms, the average prediction error at a fresh response, which exceeds n−1 times the risk Eq. 43.5.1 by σ2. A minimizer of any of the three is therefore aiming at Eq. 43.5.1, not at λ itself, and nothing here estimates a “true” smoothing parameter, since no such thing is assumed to exist.

Proof.

  • (a) A penalized spline minimizes 𝛄𝛄𝛄‖y−Bγ‖2+γ⊤Ωγ with Ω=λP, and a smoothing spline minimizes ‖y−z‖2+z⊤(λP)z, which is the same form with B=I. Both have Ω nonnegative definite and the deleted cross-product matrices nonsingular, so Theorem 29.3.2 applies verbatim and gives Eq. 43.5.3. For a local polynomial estimator, fix i and let y~ be y with yi replaced by f^(−i)(xi). The weighted least squares problem Eq. 43.1.2 at x=xi with data y~ has the same minimizer as the problem with case i deleted. Indeed the row of Ux belonging to case i is (1,0,…,0), so the objective with y~ is the deleted objective plus Kh(0)(y~i−b0)2; the deleted minimizer makes the first term as small as possible and the second term zero, so it minimizes the sum. Hence f^(−i)(xi)=∑jℓj(xi)y~j=f^(xi)+ℓi(xi){f^(−i)(xi)−yi}; solving for yi−f^(−i)(xi) gives {yi−f^(xi)}/{1−ℓi(xi)}.

  • (b) The substitution is the definition of GCV; the expansion is (1−t)−2=1+2t+O(t2) with t=df/n.

  • (c) With a normal likelihood and σ2 replaced by its maximum likelihood estimate ‖y−f^‖2/n, the maximized log likelihood is −n2log⁡(‖y−f^‖2/n)+const, and Proposition 29.2.4 counts df+1 parameters, the +1 being σ2; dropping constants gives AIC. Eq. 43.5.5 is the stated correction, which reduces to AIC/n plus constants when df/n→0.

  • (d) is Proposition 29.3.1 and Definition 29.1.1, which do not use the form of the estimator.

Idea. All three rules do the same arithmetic: residual sum of squares, inflated by a factor that grows with the degrees of freedom — (1−sii)−2 case by case, (1−df/n)−2 on average, exp⁡{2df/n} approximately for AIC. That is why they usually agree, and why they part company at large df. Mallows’ Cp (Definition 29.2.1) inflates additively, ‖y−f^‖2+2σ^2tr(Sλ), but needs a σ^2 from outside — usually Eq. 43.5.2 at a deliberately undersmoothed fit — and inherits whatever that choice got wrong.

43.5.3. What they do in practice

Example (The three criteria on one data set, and over 200 of them)

Take n=120 equally spaced design points, the mean function of Example (Bias, variance and the optimal bandwidth), σ=0.25, and a cubic P-spline with K=25 knots and a second-order penalty. On one data set the shortcut Eq. 43.5.3 agrees with 120 actual refits to 4.6×10−15, and the three criteria choose 9.83, 10.29 and 9.40 degrees of freedom.

Over 200 replicates the three are hard to tell apart. Each has median selected df 7.82, and the average n−1‖f^−f‖2 is 0.00448 for CV, 0.00449 for GCV and 0.00429 for AICc. The differences show in the upper tail: CV and GCV choose more than 15 degrees of freedom in 2.5 per cent of the samples, AICc in 0.5 per cent. The criteria are flat to the right of the minimum (Figure 43.5.1(a)), so an occasional sample sends the minimum far out, and steep to the left, so gross oversmoothing is rare.

Warning (Three small-sample failures). A flat criterion. Near the minimum, GCV can change by a fraction of a per cent over a factor of two in λ; the choice is then arbitrary, and reporting the fit at a single λ hides that. Plot the criterion.

Interpolation. Since (1−df/n)−2 becomes large only as df→n, a small sample can give GCV a local minimum at the interpolating end. AICc cannot, because of the pole in Eq. 43.5.5; this is its main practical advantage.

Correlated errors. All three estimate prediction error at a new, independent response. If the errors are serially correlated, a wiggly fit predicts its neighbours well without describing f at all, and every criterion rewards it (Example (What correlated errors do)).

Example (What correlated errors do)

Repeat the simulation with errors from a stationary AR(1) process of the same marginal standard deviation and autocorrelation ρ=0.7 (Definition 21.6.1). The median selected degrees of freedom rise from 7.82 to 25.40 for CV, 24.53 for GCV and 23.50 for AICc, and the average squared error rises from the values just quoted to 0.04209, 0.04145 and 0.03982 — roughly a ninefold loss. The fitted curve in Figure 43.5.1(b) uses 27 degrees of freedom and traces every excursion of the error process.

There is no repair inside this section: a smooth mean and smooth-looking noise are not separately identified without an assumption about one of them. The honest responses are to model the correlation (Part VII), to choose λ by a criterion that accounts for it — leaving out whole blocks rather than single points, or REML in the mixed-model form of Section 43.6 — or to say that the wiggles are not interpretable. Opsomer, Wang and Yang (2001) survey what is known.

import numpy as np


def f_true(x):
    """The mean function of Section 43.1."""
    return 2 * x + np.exp(-25 * (x - 0.35) ** 2) - 0.75 * np.exp(-50 * (x - 0.80) ** 2)


def uniform_knots(a, b, K, d):
    """K interior knots equally spaced in (a, b), continued d further steps at each end."""
    delta = (b - a) / (K + 1)
    return a + delta * np.arange(-d, K + 2 + d)


def bspline_basis(x, kn, d):
    """The B-splines of degree d on the knot vector kn, by the de Boor recurrence."""
    x = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(x, float))
    B = np.array([(x >= kn[j]) & (x < kn[j + 1]) for j in range(len(kn) - 1)], float).T
    last = np.max(np.nonzero(kn[:-1] < kn[1:])[0])
    B[x == kn[-1], last] = 1.0
    for deg in range(1, d + 1):
        C = np.zeros((len(x), B.shape[1] - 1))
        for j in range(C.shape[1]):
            if kn[j + deg] > kn[j]:
                C[:, j] += (x - kn[j]) / (kn[j + deg] - kn[j]) * B[:, j]
            if kn[j + deg + 1] > kn[j + 1]:
                C[:, j] += (kn[j + deg + 1] - x) / (kn[j + deg + 1] - kn[j + 1]) * B[:, j + 1]
        B = C
    return B


def smoother(B, P, lam):
    """The smoother matrix of the penalized fit with basis B and penalty matrix P."""
    return B @ np.linalg.solve(B.T @ B + lam * P, B.T)


def criteria(B, P, y, lam):
    """Leave-one-out CV by the leverage shortcut, GCV, and the corrected AIC."""
    n = len(y)
    S = smoother(B, P, lam)
    resid = y - S @ y
    df = np.trace(S)
    cv = np.mean((resid / (1 - np.diag(S))) ** 2)
    gcv = np.mean(resid**2) / (1 - df / n) ** 2
    aicc = np.log(np.mean(resid**2)) + 1 + 2 * (df + 1) / (n - df - 2)
    return df, cv, gcv, aicc


n, sigma, K, d, k = 120, 0.25, 25, 3, 2
x = (np.arange(1, n + 1) - 0.5) / n
kn = uniform_knots(0.0, 1.0, K, d)
B = bspline_basis(x, kn, d)
Dk = np.diff(np.eye(B.shape[1]), n=k, axis=0)
P = Dk.T @ Dk
rng = np.random.default_rng(4343)
y = f_true(x) + sigma * rng.normal(size=n)

lams = np.geomspace(1e-9, 1e3, 121)
table = np.array([criteria(B, P, y, lam) for lam in lams])
dfs, cvs, gcvs, aiccs = table.T
pick = {"CV": lams[np.argmin(cvs)], "GCV": lams[np.argmin(gcvs)], "AICc": lams[np.argmin(aiccs)]}
df_pick = {name: criteria(B, P, y, lam)[0] for name, lam in pick.items()}
print("degrees of freedom chosen:", {k_: round(v, 2) for k_, v in df_pick.items()})

43.5.4. After the choice

Remark (Inference after selecting the smoothing parameter). Every interval, test and standard error in this chapter is computed as if λ were fixed in advance. It was not: it was chosen by minimizing a criterion computed from the same y. This is Proposition 29.5.1 in a continuous guise. The residual sum of squares at the selected λ is the smallest of many, so σ^2 from Eq. 43.5.2 is biased downwards and a pointwise band does not attain its nominal coverage.

The effect is modest when the criterion has a well-determined minimum, which is why the practice survives, and large when it does not. The defences are those of Chapter 29: hold out data, bootstrap the whole procedure including the selection of λ (Chapter 23), or report the fit at several λ and let the reader see the dependence. What is not a defence is quoting a p-value from a fit whose flexibility was chosen to make that p-value small.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Exercise (B2)

Show that log⁡GCV=log⁡(n−1‖y−f^‖2)−2log⁡(1−df/n) and that −2log⁡(1−t)=2t+t2+O(t3). Compare with Eq. 43.5.5 expanded for small df/n, and explain why GCV and AIC almost always choose the same λ when n is large and differ when df is an appreciable fraction of n.

Solution. The first identity is immediate from Eq. 43.5.4. Expanding, log⁡GCV=log⁡(RSS/n)+2df/n+(df/n)2+…, while AICc=log⁡(RSS/n)+1+2(df+1)/(n−df−2)=log⁡(RSS/n)+1+2df/n+2df2/n2+…. To first order in df/n the two differ by a constant, so their minimizers agree; the second-order terms differ by a factor of two, and AICc has a pole at df=n−2 where GCV has one only at df=n. Hence they separate exactly when df/n is not small.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

GCV was introduced by Craven and Wahba (1979) as a version of cross-validation invariant to rotation. Show that if Γ is orthogonal and one replaces y by Γy and Sλ by ΓSλΓ⊤, then GCV is unchanged while CV is not. Why is this a virtue for ridge regression (where such rotations are natural) and less obviously one for a smoother (where they are not)?

Solution. Under the substitution, the residual vector becomes Γ(I−Sλ)y, whose squared length is unchanged, and tr(ΓSλΓ⊤)=tr(Sλ); so Eq. 43.5.4 is invariant, while CV depends on the individual sii, which are not. In ridge regression the canonical coordinates of Theorem 27.2.2 are as natural as the original ones, so invariance is attractive; for a smoother the coordinates are cases in a fixed order along the x-axis and the sii carry real information — small in dense regions, large in sparse ones — which replacing them by their average throws away. Hence CV and GCV differ most in designs with isolated points.

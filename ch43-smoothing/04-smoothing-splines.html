43.4  Smoothing splines

A P-spline starts from a basis and adds a penalty to it. The smoothing spline does the opposite: it asks a question about functions, with no basis in sight — among all twice-differentiable functions, which balances fit against roughness best? — and the answer turns out to be a spline, by the shortest and most satisfying derivation in the subject.

43.4.1. The variational problem

Measure the roughness of a function by ∫ab{f″(t)}2dt: zero exactly for straight lines, large for a function that turns sharply, and, unlike the number of knots, defined for every function smooth enough to have it. Let W={f:[a,b]→R | f′ is absolutely continuous and∫ab{f″(t)}2dt<∞} be the set of such functions, and consider

Sλ(f)=∑i=1n{yi−f(xi)}2+λ∫ab{f″(t)}2dt,λ>0.
(43.4.1)

Take the design points distinct and ordered, a≤x1<⋯<xn≤b. (If some coincide, replace each group by its mean with a weight equal to its size; Exercise (B1) checks that this changes nothing.)

The two extremes are clear. As λ→0 the criterion is minimized by any function through the data, so the problem is ill-posed without the penalty; as λ→∞ the penalty forces f″≡0 and the minimizer is the least squares line. In between the minimizer is some compromise between an interpolating curve and a line, and it is not obvious in advance that it is finite-dimensional.

Definition 43.4.1 (Natural cubic spline)

A natural cubic spline with knots x1<⋯<xn is a function g:[a,b]→R that is a cubic polynomial on each [xi,xi+1], has two continuous derivatives on [a,b], and is linear on [a,x1] and on [xn,b]. Equivalently: on [x1,xn], g is a cubic spline with interior knots x2,…,xn−1 satisfying the natural boundary conditions g″(x1)=g″(xn)=0, extended to [a,b] by its tangent lines at x1 and xn. (The two conditions are exactly what makes the extension C2.)

43.4.2. Interpolation with the least roughness

Everything follows from one integration by parts.

Lemma 43.4.1 (The natural interpolant, and why it is the smoothest)

Let n≥2 and x1<⋯<xn.

  • (a) If g is a natural cubic spline with these knots and h∈W satisfies h(xi)=0 for every i, then ∫abg″h″=0.

  • (b) For every z∈Rn there is exactly one natural cubic spline g with g(xi)=zi; the space of natural cubic splines with these knots has dimension n.

  • (c) That g is the unique minimizer of ∫abf″2 among all f∈W with f(xi)=zi.

Proof.

  • (a) Integrating by parts, ∫abg″h″=[g″h′]ab−∫abg‴h′. The boundary term vanishes because g is linear near a and b, so g″(a)=g″(b)=0. On [a,x1] and [xn,b] we have g‴=0, and on (xi,xi+1) the third derivative of a cubic is a constant ci. Hence ∫abg‴h′=∑i=1n−1ci∫xixi+1h′=∑i=1n−1ci{h(xi+1)−h(xi)}=0.

  • (b) A cubic spline on [x1,xn] with the n−2 interior knots x2,…,xn−1 lives in a space of dimension 4+(n−2)=n+2 (Theorem 42.3.1 with d=3), and extending it linearly outside is determined once g″(x1)=g″(xn)=0. Those are two linear constraints, so the natural cubic splines form a space of dimension at least n. The evaluation map g↦{g(x1),…,g(xn)} is linear into Rn; if g is in its kernel, apply (a) with h=g to get ∫abg″2=0, so g″≡0 and g is linear, and a line vanishing at n≥2 distinct points is zero. The map is therefore injective, so the dimension is at most n and hence exactly n, and the map is a bijection.

  • (c) Let f∈W interpolate z and put h=f−g, so h(xi)=0. By (a) the cross term vanishes, leaving ∫abf″2=∫abg″2+2∫abg″h″+∫abh″2=∫abg″2+∫abh″2, which is at least ∫abg″2, with equality iff h″≡0, that is iff h is linear; and a line vanishing at n≥2 points is zero.

Theorem 43.4.2 (The smoothing spline)

Let λ>0 and n≥2 distinct design points. Then Sλ of Eq. 43.4.1 has a unique minimizer over W, and it is the natural cubic spline with knots at x1,…,xn whose vector of values is

f^=(I+λP)−1y,
(43.4.2)

where P is the nonnegative definite matrix representing the roughness, ∫abg″2=z⊤Pz, of the natural cubic spline with values z.

Proof. Let f∈W be arbitrary and let g be the natural cubic spline with g(xi)=f(xi), which exists and is unique by Lemma 43.4.1(b). The two functions have the same residual sum of squares, and by Lemma 43.4.1(c) the penalty of g is no larger, strictly smaller unless f=g. Hence Sλ(g)≤Sλ(f) with equality only if f=g, and it suffices to minimize over the n-dimensional space of natural cubic splines.

Parameterize that space by z∈Rn through the bijection of Lemma 43.4.1(b). Since g depends linearly on z, so does g″, and the penalty ∫abg″2 is a quadratic form z⊤Pz with P symmetric and nonnegative definite. Eq. 43.4.1 becomes ‖y−z‖2+λz⊤Pz, a strictly convex quadratic in z because I+λP is positive definite; by Proposition 1.11.2 its unique minimizer is Eq. 43.4.2.

Idea. The theorem is a representer result: an optimization over an infinite-dimensional space of functions has a finite-dimensional solution, with one basis function per observation. The reason is the orthogonality in Lemma 43.4.1(a): any direction in which f differs from a natural spline without changing the fit costs roughness and buys nothing. Kimeldorf and Wahba (1971) proved the general version; see the notes.

43.4.3. The Reinsch algorithm

The proof used P without exhibiting it. Doing so turns Eq. 43.4.2, an n×n dense solve, into a banded one.

Proposition 43.4.3 (Band matrices for the roughness)

Let hi=xi+1−xi for i=1,…,n−1, and let n≥3. Define Q, of size n×(n−2), and R, symmetric tridiagonal of order n−2, by qj−1,j=1hj−1,qj,j=−1hj−1−1hj,qj+1,j=1hj, rjj=hj−1+hj3,rj,j+1=rj+1,j=hj6, for j=2,…,n−1 (columns and rows indexed by the interior knots), all other entries zero. Then:

  • (a) R is positive definite.

  • (b) If g is the natural cubic spline with values z and 𝛄γ={g″(x2),…,g″(xn−1)}⊤, then 𝛄Rγ=Q⊤z and 𝛄𝛄∫abg″2=γ⊤Rγ, so that P=QR−1Q⊤.

  • (c) The fit of Theorem 43.4.2 satisfies

    𝛄𝛄(R+λQ⊤Q)γ^=Q⊤y,f^=y−λQγ^,
    (43.4.3)

    and R+λQ⊤Q has bandwidth two, so the fit costs O(n) operations.

Proof.

  • (a) R is symmetric with positive diagonal, and rj,j−1+rj,j+1=(hj−1+hj)/6<rjj, so it is strictly diagonally dominant and therefore positive definite.

  • (b) On [xj,xj+1], g″ is linear with endpoint values γj and γj+1 (setting γ1=γn=0). Writing u=t−xj and integrating twice, g(xj+u)=zj+g′(xj)u+12γju2+γj+1−γj6hju3. Setting u=hj and solving for g′(xj) gives g′(xj)=(zj+1−zj)/hj−hj(2γj+γj+1)/6, and differentiating at u=hj, g′(xj+1−)=zj+1−zjhj+hj(γj+2γj+1)6. Equating this with g′(xj+1+), read off the interval [xj+1,xj+2], and writing i=j+1, zi+1−zihi−zi−zi−1hi−1=hi−1γi−1+2(hi−1+hi)γi+hiγi+16, which is 𝛄(Q⊤z)i=(Rγ)i. For the penalty, ∫xjxj+1g″2=hj(γj2+γjγj+1+γj+12)/3 by direct integration of a linear function squared, and summing over j reproduces 𝛄𝛄γ⊤Rγ term by term. Since 𝛄γ=R−1Q⊤z, the penalty is z⊤QR−1Q⊤z.

  • (c) Put 𝛄γ=R−1Q⊤f^. From Eq. 43.4.2, 𝛄f^+λQγ=y, and multiplying by Q⊤ and using 𝛄Rγ=Q⊤f^ gives 𝛄𝛄Rγ=Q⊤y−λQ⊤Qγ, which is Eq. 43.4.3. R is tridiagonal and Q has three nonzero entries per column in consecutive rows, so R+λQ⊤Q has bandwidth two. A Cholesky factor inherits the bandwidth: in Eq. 10.2.1, if rki=0 whenever i−k>2 for every k<j, then for i−j>2 every product rkjrki in the sum vanishes (a nonzero rkj needs j−k≤2, whence i−k>2) and so does aji, so rji=0. The factorization of Theorem 10.2.1 therefore visits O(n) entries instead of O(n3), and so do the two multiplications by Q.

Reinsch (1967) gave this algorithm before the variational theorem was widely known; it is why smoothing splines remain the cheapest smoother for a single covariate. Nothing in it forms Sλ=(I+λP)−1, which is dense; the diagonal of Sλ, needed for cross-validation in Section 43.5, can also be got in O(n) from a banded inverse (Hutchinson and de Hoog, 1985).

Example (A smoothing spline, checked three ways)

Take n=120 design points drawn uniformly on [0,1] and the mean function of Example (Bias, variance and the optimal bandwidth) with σ=0.25. At λ=1×10−4 the fit uses 12.32 effective degrees of freedom. Three independent computations agree with it. The roughness 𝛄𝛄γ⊤Rγ of the natural spline interpolating the true curve, 1069.5, matches ∫abg″2 from a separately constructed natural cubic interpolant to a relative error of 1.6×10−12; the banded solve Eq. 43.4.3 agrees with the dense form Eq. 43.4.2 to 1.3×10−10; and minimizing 𝛄𝛄𝛄‖y−Bγ‖2+λγ⊤Ωγ over the whole space of cubic splines with a knot at every design point — dimension 122, with Ωjk=∫Bj″Bk″ computed exactly — gives fitted values differing from the smoothing spline by 4.4×10−10. That is Theorem 43.4.2 in numerical form: the minimizer over the larger space is the natural spline.

import numpy as np


def f_true(x):
    """The mean function of Section 43.1."""
    return 2 * x + np.exp(-25 * (x - 0.35) ** 2) - 0.75 * np.exp(-50 * (x - 0.80) ** 2)


def reinsch(t):
    """The band matrices Q (N x (N-2)) and R ((N-2) x (N-2)) of the Reinsch form."""
    N = len(t)
    h = np.diff(t)
    Q = np.zeros((N, N - 2))
    R = np.zeros((N - 2, N - 2))
    for j in range(N - 2):                       # column j belongs to interior knot t[j+1]
        Q[j, j], Q[j + 1, j], Q[j + 2, j] = 1 / h[j], -1 / h[j] - 1 / h[j + 1], 1 / h[j + 1]
        R[j, j] = (h[j] + h[j + 1]) / 3
        if j + 1 < N - 2:
            R[j, j + 1] = R[j + 1, j] = h[j + 1] / 6
    return Q, R


def smoothing_spline(t, y, lam):
    """Fitted values of the smoothing spline, by solving a pentadiagonal system."""
    Q, R = reinsch(t)
    gamma = np.linalg.solve(R + lam * Q.T @ Q, Q.T @ y)   # the second derivatives
    return y - lam * Q @ gamma, gamma


def smoother_matrix(t, lam):
    """The matrix S_lambda with fitted values S_lambda y (formed only to look at it)."""
    Q, R = reinsch(t)
    return np.eye(len(t)) - lam * Q @ np.linalg.solve(R + lam * Q.T @ Q, Q.T)


n, sigma = 120, 0.25
rng = np.random.default_rng(430430)
t = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 1, n))
y = f_true(t) + sigma * rng.normal(size=n)

lam = 1e-4
fit, _ = smoothing_spline(t, y, lam)
S = smoother_matrix(t, lam)
df = np.trace(S)
print("effective degrees of freedom at lambda = 1e-4:", round(df, 2))
print("rows of S sum to one, largest departure:", np.max(np.abs(S.sum(axis=1) - 1)))

43.4.4. Variations, and the relation to penalized splines

Other orders. Replacing f″ by f(m) in Eq. 43.4.1 gives a natural spline of degree 2m−1: the proof of Lemma 43.4.1 goes through with m integrations by parts and the boundary conditions f(j)(x1)=f(j)(xn)=0 for j=m,…,2m−2. The case m=1 gives a piecewise linear fit, m=2 the cubic one above, m=3 a quintic. In practice m=2 is used almost always; the extra smoothness of m=3 rarely shows.

Reproducing kernels. The penalty ∫f″2 is the squared norm of the part of f orthogonal to the straight lines, in a Hilbert space of functions in which evaluation f↦f(x) is a bounded linear functional and so representable as an inner product with a function Rx, the reproducing kernel. In that language Theorem 43.4.2 is a special case of the representer theorem of Kimeldorf and Wahba (1971): the minimizer of ∑i{yi−f(xi)}2+λ‖Pf‖2 lies in the span of Rx1,…,Rxn plus the null space of P. That is the route of Wahba (1990), and it covers thin-plate splines, splines on a sphere and the kernel methods of machine learning. This book does not develop it; the proof above is self-contained for a single covariate.

Smoothing splines and P-splines. A smoothing spline is a penalized spline with a knot at every distinct design point and the exact derivative penalty, as Example (A smoothing spline, checked three ways) confirms numerically, and a P-spline with K=20 knots is a low-rank approximation to it: both fits are determined, through Eq. 43.3.2, by how the penalty ranks a handful of smooth directions, so the two agree once m comfortably exceeds the effective degrees of freedom. The smoothing spline has no knot choice and costs O(n); the P-spline carries an explicit coefficient vector and generalizes to several covariates and to nonnormal responses, which is why Chapter 44 uses it.

Remark (The equivalent kernel of a smoothing spline). Panel (b) of Figure 43.4.1 shows the rows of Sλ, the smoothing spline’s equivalent kernel in the sense of Section 43.1. Silverman (1984) proved that at an interior point, for large n and small λ, the weight given to a design point at distance u is approximately {nb(x)g(x)}−112exp⁡(−|v|/2)sin⁡(|v|/2+π/4) with v=u/b(x) and local bandwidth b(x)={λ/(ng(x))}1/4. Two features of that formula show in the picture: the weights decay exponentially, and the sine makes them change sign, so a smoothing spline is a kernel smoother with a small negative side lobe — here the most negative weight is −0.0034 against a peak of 0.0778. The bandwidth adapts to the design density by itself, as g(x)−1/4. We state this without proof; the argument is a Green’s-function calculation.

43.4.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show directly from Eq. 43.4.3 that f^→y as λ→0 and that f^ tends to the least squares line as λ→∞. (For the second, use Proposition 43.4.3(b): what does Q⊤z=0 say about z?)

Solution. As λ→0, Eq. 43.4.3 gives 𝛄γ^→R−1Q⊤y, bounded, so 𝛄f^=y−λQγ^→y. As λ→∞, the penalty z⊤Pz must tend to zero, and P=QR−1Q⊤ with R positive definite, so Q⊤z→0. Now (Q⊤z)i is a second divided difference of z at xi−1,xi,xi+1, and it vanishes for all i exactly when the points (xi,zi) are collinear. So the limit lies in the two-dimensional space C([1,x]), and since the residual sum of squares is still being minimized there, it is the least squares line.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Suppose the design has ties: the distinct values are t1<⋯<tN and nk observations share tk, with mean y¯k. Show that ∑i{yi−f(xi)}2=∑knk{y¯k−f(tk)}2+C with C free of f, so that Theorem 43.4.2 holds with knots at the distinct values and I replaced by diag(n1,…,nN) in Eq. 43.4.2.

Exercise (B2)

Using the cell above, build Q and R for n=8 equally spaced points on [0,1] and verify by hand that R is tridiagonal with rjj=2h/3 and rj,j+1=h/6, and that Q⊤1=0 and Q⊤x=0. What does the second pair of identities say about Sλ1 and Sλx?

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

State and prove the analogue of Lemma 43.4.1(a) for the penalty ∫ab{f(m)}2, with g a natural spline of degree 2m−1. Which boundary conditions are needed to kill the boundary terms after m integrations by parts, and what replaces “a line vanishing at n≥2 points is zero”?

Exercise (C2)

Show that if λ>0 then f^ never interpolates the data unless the data already lie on a straight line. (Use Eq. 43.4.2 and the fact that Pz=0 iff z∈C([1,x]), which Exercise (A1) establishes.) Contrast this with a regression spline with K=n−4 knots, and say what the difference means for the leave-one-out shortcut of Theorem 29.3.2.

Solution. f^=y requires (I+λP)y=y, that is Py=0, that is y in C([1,x]). So for λ>0 the fit leaves a nonzero residual unless the data are exactly linear, and every sii is strictly less than one, which is what makes the shortcut of Theorem 29.3.2 well defined. An unpenalized regression spline with n basis functions interpolates, every sii=1, the shortcut divides by zero, and cross-validation is useless — one more reason the dial should be a penalty and not a knot count.

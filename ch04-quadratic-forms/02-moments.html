4.2  Mean and variance of quadratic forms

4.2.1. The mean needs no normality

Theorem 2.3.1 computed E(Y⊤AY) for any random vector with mean 𝛍μ and covariance Σ:

𝛍𝛍E(Y⊤AY)=tr(AΣ)+μ⊤Aμ.
(4.2.1)

The formula has two parts with distinct roles. The trace term is what the noise contributes. The term 𝛍𝛍μ⊤Aμ is what the signal contributes. For nonnegative definite A it vanishes exactly when 𝛍Aμ=0, which describes the mean vectors a well-designed estimator is meant to ignore.

Example (Two estimators of σ2 under a drifting mean)

Let Y1,…,Yn be uncorrelated with common variance σ2, and consider S2=1n−1∑i=1n(Yi−Y¯)2,Q=12(n−1)∑i=1n−1(Yi+1−Yi)2, the sample variance and the mean square successive difference. In matrix form S2=Y⊤CY/(n−1) with C=I−n−111⊤, and Q=Y⊤D⊤DY/{2(n−1)}, where D is the (n−1)×n first-difference matrix with rows (…,−1,1,…). Both matrices annihilate 1, so both estimators ignore a constant mean. Since tr(C)=n−1 and tr(D⊤D)=tr(DD⊤)=2(n−1), Eq. 4.2.1 with Σ=σ2I shows that both are unbiased when the mean is constant.

Now let the mean drift linearly, μi=μ0+θi. Then 𝛍Cμ is the centred trend and 𝛍Dμ=θ1n−1, so E(S2)=σ2+θ2n(n+1)12,E(Q)=σ2+θ22. The bias of S2 grows quadratically with the length of the series, while that of Q does not grow at all. With n=20, σ=1 and θ=0.1, the means are 1.350 and 1.005. Differencing removes smooth trends, and this is why Q was proposed for series whose level may wander (von Neumann 1941). The price of robustness to drift is paid in variance, which we compute below.

4.2.2. A canonical representation

Higher moments and distributions need normality, and one representation answers all such questions at once.

Theorem 4.2.1 (Canonical form of a normal quadratic form)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) and let A be symmetric. Write Σ=LL⊤ with L of size n×k, and take a spectral decomposition L⊤AL=QΛQ⊤ with Λ=diag(λ1,…,λk). Put 𝛍b=Q⊤L⊤Aμ. Then

𝛍𝛍Y⊤AY=dμ⊤Aμ+∑j=1k(λjWj2+2bjWj),W∼Nk(0,Ik).
(4.2.2)

For λj≠0 the jth term equals λj(Wj+bj/λj)2−bj2/λj. The nonzero λj are the nonzero eigenvalues of AΣ.

Proof. Such an L exists. By Theorem 1.7.2, Σ=UDU⊤ with U orthogonal and D diagonal with entries di≥0, and L=UD1/2 works with k=n. Let Z∼Nk(0,I). The vector 𝛍μ+LZ is normal with mean 𝛍μ and covariance LL⊤=Σ (Theorem 3.3.1). A normal distribution is determined by its mean and covariance (Theorem 3.2.1), so Y and 𝛍μ+LZ have the same law, and so do Y⊤AY and 𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍(μ+LZ)⊤A(μ+LZ)=μ⊤Aμ+2μ⊤ALZ+Z⊤L⊤ALZ. Put W=Q⊤Z, again Nk(0,I). Then Z⊤L⊤ALZ=W⊤ΛW=∑λjWj2 and 𝛍μ⊤ALZ=b⊤W, which is Eq. 4.2.2. The completed square is algebra. Finally, L⊤AL=HF with F=AL and H=L⊤, and FH=AΣ. By Proposition 1.7.1(d) these two products have the same nonzero eigenvalues, with the same multiplicities.

So a normal quadratic form is a constant plus a sum of independent pieces. Each piece is either a scaled noncentral χ2(1) variable, with scale λj, or a normal variable with mean zero, when λj=0 but bj≠0. Because the λj may be negative, Y⊤AY need not be positive. The linear pieces are special to singular covariances. If Σ is positive definite, L can be taken square and nonsingular, and then λj=0 gives ALqj=0 for the jth column qj of Q, so 𝛍bj=(ALqj)⊤μ=0. A linear piece needs a direction in which A responds to the mean while the noise is absent.

Corollary 4.2.2 (Moment generating function)

In the setting of Theorem 4.2.1, for |t|<1/(2maxj|λj|) (any t if all λj=0), 𝛍𝛍EetY⊤AY=etμ⊤Aμ∏j=1k(1−2λjt)−1/2exp⁡(2bj2t21−2λjt).

Proof. The terms of Eq. 4.2.2 are independent. If λj=0, the term 2bjWj has mgf e2bj2t2. If λj≠0, apply Lemma 4.1.1 to Wj+νj with νj=bj/λj at the argument λjt, and multiply by e−tbj2/λj. The exponent is bj2t/{λj(1−2λjt)}−bj2t/λj=2bj2t2/(1−2λjt).

4.2.3. Variance and covariances

Theorem 4.2.3 (Moments of normal quadratic forms)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,Σ), let A and B be symmetric n×n matrices, and let K be an m×n matrix. Then

  • (a) 𝛍𝛍E(Y⊤AY)=tr(AΣ)+μ⊤Aμ;

  • (b) 𝛍𝛍Var(Y⊤AY)=2tr{(AΣ)2}+4μ⊤AΣAμ;

  • (c) 𝛍Cov(KY,Y⊤AY)=2KΣAμ;

  • (d) 𝛍𝛍Cov(Y⊤AY,Y⊤BY)=2tr(AΣBΣ)+4μ⊤AΣBμ.

Proof.

  • (a) is Eq. 4.2.1. For (b), use Eq. 4.2.2. Independent terms have additive variances, and for W∼N(0,1), Var(λW2+2bW)=2λ2+4b2, because Cov(W2,W)=EW3=0. Hence 𝛍𝛍Var(Y⊤AY)=2∑jλj2+4‖b‖2=2tr{(L⊤AL)2}+4μ⊤ALQQ⊤L⊤Aμ. Now tr{(L⊤AL)2}=tr(L⊤AΣAL)=tr(AΣAΣ) by Theorem 1.6.1, and QQ⊤=I, so the second term is 𝛍𝛍4μ⊤AΣAμ. For (c), write 𝛍Y=μ+LZ as in the proof of Theorem 4.2.1. Then 𝛍Y⊤AY−E(Y⊤AY)=Z⊤L⊤ALZ−tr(AΣ)+2μ⊤ALZ and KY−E(KY)=KLZ. Every entry of E[Z(Z⊤GZ)] is a sum of third moments of a Nk(0,I) vector, which vanish. So only the linear term contributes, and 𝛍𝛍E[KLZ⋅2Z⊤L⊤Aμ]=2KLL⊤Aμ. For (d), apply (b) to A, B and A+B, and use Var(X+Y)=VarX+VarY+2Cov(X,Y).

For 𝛍μ=0, part (b) can also be read off the fourth moments of a normal vector (Exercise (B1)). The variance has the same two-part structure as the mean: a pure-noise term, plus a term that is present only when A responds to the mean. Part (c) shows that a quadratic form and a linear form are uncorrelated when KΣA=0. Under normality this will turn out to give independence (Theorem 4.4.1).

Example

Example (Two estimators of σ2 under a drifting mean)

With Σ=σ2I and a constant mean, Theorem 4.2.3(b) gives Var(S2)=2σ4tr(C2)/(n−1)2=2σ4/(n−1). For Q, the matrix DD⊤ is tridiagonal with 2 on the diagonal and −1 beside it, so tr{(D⊤D)2}=tr{(DD⊤)2}=4(n−1)+2(n−2)=6n−8 and Var(Q)=2σ4(6n−8)4(n−1)2=σ4(3n−4)(n−1)2. The ratio Var(Q)/Var(S2)=(3n−4)/{2(n−1)} tends to 3/2. With n=20 the two variances are 0.1551 and 0.1053, a ratio of 1.474. So with no drift, Q needs about half as many observations again to match S2. Under the drift θ=0.1, the signal term 𝛍𝛍4μ⊤AΣAμ raises the variances to 0.1552 and 0.1789. Combining variance and bias, the root mean squared errors are 0.394 for Q and 0.549 for S2. The listing evaluates the formulas, and the script confirms all eight moments with 400,000 simulated series.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(4402)
n, sigma, theta = 20, 1.0, 0.1
t = np.arange(1, n + 1)
mu = 5.0 + theta * t                                   # mean with a linear trend
Sigma = sigma**2 * np.eye(n)

def qf_moments(A, mu, Sigma):
    """Mean and variance of Y^T A Y for Y ~ N(mu, Sigma), A symmetric."""
    AS = A @ Sigma
    mean = np.trace(AS) + mu @ A @ mu
    var = 2 * np.trace(AS @ AS) + 4 * mu @ AS @ A @ mu
    return mean, var

C = np.eye(n) - np.ones((n, n)) / n                    # centring matrix
D = np.diff(np.eye(n), axis=0)                         # (n-1) x n first differences
A_s2 = C / (n - 1)
A_q = D.T @ D / (2 * (n - 1))
for name, A in [("S2", A_s2), ("Q", A_q)]:
    print(name, qf_moments(A, mu, Sigma), qf_moments(A, 0 * mu, Sigma))

Normality enters (b)–(d) through the third and fourth moments of Y. For independent coordinates with excess kurtosis the variance acquires an extra term that involves only the diagonal of A (Theorem 2.3.3). Variance formulas for sums of squares derived under normality can be badly wrong for heavy-tailed errors, a point taken up in Chapter 19 (Proposition 19.3.4, Proposition 19.3.5).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let (Ui,Wi), i=1,…,n, be independent copies of a bivariate normal pair with variances σU2, σW2 and covariance σUW, and let sUW=(n−1)−1∑i(Ui−U¯)(Wi−W¯). Write sUW=Y⊤AY for the stacked vector Y=(U⊤,W⊤)⊤ and a symmetric 2n×2n matrix A, and use Theorem 4.2.3 to show that Var(sUW)=(σU2σW2+σUW2)/(n−1). Check the answer against Var(S2) when U=W.

Exercise (B2)

Let Y1,…,Yn be independent with mean 0, variance σ2 and EYi4=μ4. By Exercise (B4), for symmetric A and B, Cov(Y⊤AY,Y⊤BY)=(μ4−3σ4)∑iaiibii+2σ4tr(AB). Show that in a one-way layout with g≥2 groups, not all of size one, the between-group and within-group sums of squares are correlated unless μ4=3σ4. Which sign does the correlation have for heavy-tailed errors?

Solution. In the one-way layout the between-group matrix is A=M−P1 and the within-group matrix is B=I−M. They satisfy AB=0, so tr(AB)=0 and the covariance reduces to (μ4−3σ4)∑iaiibii. An observation in group ℓ has aii=1/nℓ−1/n and bii=1−1/nℓ, so ∑iaiibii=∑ℓnℓ(1nℓ−1n)(1−1nℓ)=∑ℓ(1−nℓn)(1−1nℓ). With g≥2 every first factor is positive, and the second is positive for any group with two or more observations. So the sum is positive, and the covariance vanishes iff μ4=3σ4. For heavy-tailed errors, μ4>3σ4, the two sums of squares are positively correlated: a large error inflates both.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let Y∼Nn(μ1,σ2I) with μ and σ2 unknown. Among symmetric A with E(Y⊤AY)=σ2 for all μ and σ2, show that A=C/(n−1), which gives S2, uniquely minimizes Var(Y⊤AY) at every parameter value. (Compare Exercise (B5), where the mean is known to be zero.)

Solution. Since E(Y⊤AY)=σ2trA+μ21⊤A1, unbiasedness means trA=1 and 1⊤A1=0. By Theorem 4.2.3, Var(Y⊤AY)=2σ4tr(A2)+4μ2σ2‖A1‖2. The map A↦CAC is an orthogonal projection for the inner product tr(AB) on symmetric matrices, so tr(A2)≥tr{(CAC)2}, with equality iff A=CAC. Also tr(CAC)=trA−1⊤A1/n=1. The symmetric matrix CAC has rank at most n−1. If λ1,…,λn−1 are its eigenvalues on 1⊥, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives 1=(∑λi)2≤(n−1)∑λi2. Hence tr(A2)≥1/(n−1), with equality iff A=CAC and all λi=1/(n−1), that is, iff A=C/(n−1). That matrix also has A1=0, so it minimizes both variance terms at once.

4.6  Summary and Notes

4.6.1. Summary

Idea.

  1. If Z∼Nr(μ,I) then ‖Z‖2∼χ2(r,γ) with γ=‖μ‖2, mean r+γ and variance 2r+4γ. The law is a Poisson(γ/2) mixture of χ2(r+2k) laws, and its tails increase strictly with γ (Theorem 4.1.2 and Proposition 4.1.3).

  2. F(r,s,γ) and t(s,δ) put a noncentral numerator over an independent central denominator. The power of an F test increases strictly with γ, and T2∼F(1,s,δ2) (Theorem 4.1.4).

  3. E(Y⊤AY)=tr(AΣ)+μ⊤Aμ for any distribution. Under normality Var(Y⊤AY)=2tr{(AΣ)2}+4μ⊤AΣAμ,Cov(KY,Y⊤AY)=2KΣAμ (Theorem 4.2.3).

  4. A normal quadratic form is a constant plus independent terms λj(Wj+νj)2, and for singular Σ possibly normal terms. The weights λj are the nonzero eigenvalues of AΣ (Theorem 4.2.1).

  5. For Y∼Nn(μ,I), Y⊤AY is chi-squared iff A is idempotent. Then the degrees of freedom are rankA=trA and the noncentrality is μ⊤Aμ (Theorem 4.3.1). For positive definite Σ the condition is that AΣ be idempotent. For projections, ‖PY‖2/σ2∼χ2(rankP,‖Pμ‖2/σ2) (Theorem 4.3.2).

  6. BY and Y⊤AY are independent if BΣA=0, and Y⊤AY and Y⊤BY are independent if AΣB=0, because a quadratic form is a function of AY (Theorem 4.4.1 and Theorem 4.4.2).

  7. Projections of a spherical normal vector onto mutually orthogonal subspaces are independent. This gives the independence of Y¯ and S2 and the exact F(r−r0,n−r,γ) law of the nested-model F statistic (Theorem 4.4.4, Corollary 4.4.5 and Theorem 4.4.6).

  8. Cochran: if symmetric matrices add to an idempotent matrix and their ranks add up, the corresponding sums of squares are independent chi-squared variables (Theorem 4.5.1 and Theorem 4.5.2).

4.6.2. Notes and sources

Source texts. Rencher and Schaalje (2008, chapter 5) cover the material of Section 4.2, Section 4.3, Section 4.4 and Section 4.5 for positive definite covariance matrices. They derive the mgf of a quadratic form by integrating against the normal density and obtain its variance by differentiating the logarithm of the mgf; the converse of the chi-squared theorem is referred to the literature. They use the noncentrality μ⊤μ/2. Seber and Lee (2003, section 2.4) reduce the general case to a spherical normal vector by an orthogonal diagonalization. They prove necessity of idempotence for central forms by factorizing the moment generating function, and give the eigenvalue criterion for singular covariance matrices. Christensen (2020, section 1.3) states the three conditions of Theorem 4.3.2(c) for arbitrary nonnegative definite Σ, and obtains independence by viewing quadratic forms as functions of linear ones, the approach followed in Section 4.4. The canonical representation (Theorem 4.2.1), the proof of necessity through the coefficients Eq. 4.3.1, the monotonicity proof through the Poisson mixture, and the treatment of the Cochran algebra are this book’s own arrangements of standard material.

Noncentral distributions. The noncentral chi-squared distribution first appeared in Fisher (1928). Patnaik (1949) studied its approximation by a scaled central chi-squared variable, and Satterthwaite (1946) used the same two-moment idea for linear combinations of mean squares, which reappears in Chapter 32. Johnson et al. (1995, chapters 29–31) collect the properties of the noncentral chi-squared, F and t families. The monotonicity of power in the noncentrality and in the degrees of freedom is treated systematically by Ghosh (1973).

General quadratic forms. The exact distribution of a quadratic form that is not chi-squared can be computed by numerical inversion of its characteristic function (Gil-Pelaez 1951; Imhof 1961). Mathai and Provost (1992) give a book-length treatment of quadratic forms in normal and non-normal variables. The mean square successive difference of Example (Two estimators of σ2 under a drifting mean) goes back to von Neumann (1941), who derived the exact distribution of its ratio to S2.

Independence and Cochran’s theorem. Cochran (1934) proved the decomposition theorem that bears his name, in the context of the analysis of covariance. The necessity of AΣB=0 for independence of two forms is attributed to Craig (1943). Its full proof for arbitrary symmetric matrices and arbitrary means turned out to be subtle, and Driscoll and Gundberg (1986) trace the flawed and correct arguments. Searle (1971) gives general proofs of the independence and Cochran theorems in the style most linear-models texts follow. For nonnegative definite A and B, the condition ΣAΣBΣ=0 of Christensen (2020, Theorem 1.3.8) is in fact equivalent to AΣB=0 (Exercise (B2)), so that theorem adds nothing to Theorem 4.4.2 even for singular Σ, whereas his Theorem 1.3.9, for general symmetric A and B, does. That the independence of Y¯ and S2 characterizes the normal distribution was shown by Lukacs (1942).

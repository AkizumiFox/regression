4.3  When a quadratic form is chi-squared

A quadratic form is a weighted sum of independent noncentral χ2(1) pieces, and the weights are eigenvalues. It is itself chi-squared when every nonzero weight equals one. This section turns that observation into checkable conditions on A and Σ, and shows that for the spherical case the condition is necessary as well as sufficient.

4.3.1. Spherical normal vectors

Theorem 4.3.1 (Chi-squared forms in a spherical normal vector)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,In), let A be symmetric, and let r≥1 be an integer and γ≥0. Then Y⊤AY∼χ2(r,γ) if and only if A is idempotent with rank(A)=r and 𝛍𝛍γ=μ⊤Aμ.

Proof. Sufficiency. By Theorem 1.9.2, A=Q1Q1⊤, where the n×r matrix Q1 has orthonormal columns and r=rank(A)=tr(A). Then Y⊤AY=‖Q1⊤Y‖2 and 𝛍𝛍Q1⊤Y∼Nr(Q1⊤μ,Q1⊤Q1)=Nr(Q1⊤μ,Ir), so by definition 𝛍Y⊤AY∼χ2(r,‖Q1⊤μ‖2), and 𝛍𝛍𝛍‖Q1⊤μ‖2=μ⊤Aμ.

Necessity. Apply Theorem 4.2.1 with L=I, so that A=QΛQ⊤. Let 𝛎𝛍ν=Q⊤μ. Then 𝛍𝛎b=Q⊤Aμ=Λν, so bj=λjνj. The constant left after completing squares is 𝛍𝛍𝛎𝛎μ⊤Aμ−∑λj≠0λjνj2=ν⊤Λν−∑jλjνj2=0. Hence Y⊤AY has the law of ∑j∈Jλj(Wj+νj)2, where J indexes the nonzero eigenvalues. J is not empty, because a χ2(r,γ) variable with r≥1 is not identically zero.

By Lemma 4.1.1, near t=0 the logarithm of the mgf of this sum is ∑j∈J{−12log⁡(1−2λjt)+λjνj2t/(1−2λjt)}. Expanding −12log⁡(1−x)=∑k≥1xk/(2k) and x/(1−x)=∑k≥1xk, the coefficient of tk is (2k−1/k)∑j∈Jλjk(1+kνj2). The same expansion of Eq. 4.1.2 gives (2k−1/k)(r+kγ). Equal distributions have equal mgfs, and two power series that agree on an interval have equal coefficients, so

∑j∈Jλjk(1+kνj2)=r+kγfor every k≥1.
(4.3.1)

Let ρ=maxj∈J|λj|. For even k every term on the left is nonnegative and one of them is at least ρk. The right side grows only linearly in k, so ρ≤1. Split J into J+={λj=1}, J−={λj=−1} and J0={0<|λj|<1}, and write N±=|J±| and S±=∑J±νj2. The terms over J0 tend to zero as k→∞, so N++(−1)kN−+k(S++(−1)kS−)−r−kγ⟶0. Dividing by k and letting k→∞ through even and through odd values gives S++S−=γ=S+−S−. Then the same limit without the factor k gives N++N−=r=N+−N−. So N−=S−=0, N+=r and S+=γ. The terms over J+ in Eq. 4.3.1 now account for all of r+kγ, and with k=2 the remaining sum ∑J0λj2(1+2νj2) is zero. Every term of that sum is positive, so J0 is empty. Thus every eigenvalue of A is 0 or 1, A is idempotent, rank(A)=N+=r, and 𝛍𝛍μ⊤Aμ=∑J+νj2=γ.

The necessity half is the one that makes the theorem a tool for recognizing distributions. If a quadratic form in a spherical normal vector is chi-squared at all, then it is the squared length of a projection. There is no other way.

4.3.2. General covariance matrices

Theorem 4.3.2 (When a quadratic form is chi-squared)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) and let A be symmetric.

  • (a) (Projections.) If Σ=σ2I with σ>0 and P is symmetric and idempotent, then 𝛍‖PY‖2σ2=Y⊤PYσ2∼χ2(rank(P),‖Pμ‖2σ2).

  • (b) (Positive definite Σ.) If Σ is positive definite, then Y⊤AY has a χ2(r,γ) distribution for some r≥1 and γ≥0 iff AΣ is idempotent and A≠0. In that case r=rank(A)=tr(AΣ) and 𝛍𝛍γ=μ⊤Aμ.

  • (c) (Any Σ.) If 𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍(i) ΣAΣAΣ=ΣAΣ,(ii) ΣAΣAμ=ΣAμ,(iii) μ⊤AΣAμ=μ⊤Aμ, then 𝛍𝛍Y⊤AY∼χ2(tr(AΣ),μ⊤Aμ). The three conditions hold in particular when AΣA=A, and when AΣ is idempotent and 𝛍μ∈C(Σ).

Proof.

  • (a) 𝛍σ−1Y∼Nn(σ−1μ,I). Apply Theorem 4.3.1 with A=P, using P⊤P=P to write Y⊤PY=‖PY‖2 and 𝛍𝛍𝛍μ⊤Pμ=‖Pμ‖2.

  • (b) Let Σ1/2 be the positive definite square root (Theorem 1.7.6). Then 𝛍Z=Σ−1/2Y∼Nn(Σ−1/2μ,I) and Y⊤AY=Z⊤BZ with B=Σ1/2AΣ1/2 symmetric. By Theorem 4.3.1, Y⊤AY is noncentral chi-squared iff B is idempotent and nonzero. Now B2=Σ1/2AΣAΣ1/2, so B2=B iff AΣA=A (multiply by Σ−1/2 on both sides) iff AΣAΣ=AΣ (multiply by Σ or Σ−1 on the right). Also B≠0 iff A≠0. The degrees of freedom are rank(B)=rank(A), which equals tr(B)=tr(AΣ), and the noncentrality is 𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍μ⊤Σ−1/2BΣ−1/2μ=μ⊤Aμ.

  • (c) Take L of size n×k with full column rank k=rank(Σ) and LL⊤=Σ, for instance L=U1D11/2 from the eigenvectors U1 for the positive eigenvalues D1 of Σ. (If Σ=0 the claim is trivial.) As in Theorem 4.2.1, 𝛍Y=dμ+LZ with Z∼Nk(0,I). Since L has full column rank, L⊤ has full row rank, so LGL⊤=LHL⊤ implies G=H, and Lg=Lh implies g=h. Put B=L⊤AL and 𝛍c=L⊤Aμ. Condition (i) reads LB2L⊤=LBL⊤, so B2=B. Condition (ii) reads LBc=Lc, so Bc=c. Condition (iii) reads 𝛍𝛍c⊤c=μ⊤Aμ. Hence 𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍(μ+LZ)⊤A(μ+LZ)=μ⊤Aμ+2c⊤Z+Z⊤BZ=(Z+c)⊤B(Z+c), because (Z+c)⊤B(Z+c)=Z⊤BZ+2c⊤BZ+c⊤Bc and Bc=c. Now Z+c∼Nk(c,I), and Theorem 4.3.1 gives the law χ2(rankB,c⊤Bc), where rankB=trB=tr(AΣ) and 𝛍𝛍c⊤Bc=c⊤c=μ⊤Aμ. If B=0, then c=Bc=0 and the form is identically 0, which is χ2(0). If AΣA=A, all three conditions follow by substitution. If AΣ is idempotent and 𝛍μ=Σv, then (i) follows from multiplying AΣAΣ=AΣ by Σ on the left, and (ii) and (iii) from the same identity applied to v.

Part (a) is the case used most often in this book. For the linear model 𝛃Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I) and the projection M onto C(X), Chapter 6 shows that the residual sum of squares is SSE=‖(I−M)Y‖2. Since 𝛃(I−M)Xβ=0, part (a) gives SSE/σ2∼χ2(n−rank(X)), a central distribution whatever the value of 𝛃β. The projections that define test statistics, by contrast, do not annihilate the mean under the alternative, and their noncentrality 𝛃‖PXβ‖2/σ2 measures how far the truth is from the hypothesis in the metric that the test can see.

In part (c) idempotence of AΣ alone is not enough when Σ is singular. A mean with a component outside C(Σ) is a deterministic shift that the form may pick up as a constant or as a linear term (Exercise (B2)). Part (c) also covers the natural generalization of the Mahalanobis distance.

Corollary 4.3.3 (Mahalanobis forms)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,Σ).

  • (a) If Σ is positive definite, 𝛍𝛍(Y−μ)⊤Σ−1(Y−μ)∼χ2(n) and 𝛍𝛍Y⊤Σ−1Y∼χ2(n,μ⊤Σ−1μ).

  • (b) If G is a symmetric generalized inverse of Σ and 𝛍μ∈C(Σ), then 𝛍𝛍Y⊤GY∼χ2(rank(Σ),μ⊤Gμ).

Proof.

  • (a) A=Σ−1 satisfies AΣA=A, and 𝛍Y−μ∼Nn(0,Σ). The central statement was already obtained by whitening in Proposition 3.2.3.
  • (b) GΣ is idempotent because ΣGΣ=Σ, so part (c) of the theorem applies and the degrees of freedom are tr(GΣ)=tr(L⊤GL). With L as in that proof, LL⊤GLL⊤=LL⊤ gives L⊤GL=Ik, whose trace is k=rank(Σ).

A symmetric generalized inverse always exists (Proposition 1.3.3(d)). The value of Y⊤GY does not depend on which one is chosen, with probability one (Exercise (B3)).

Example (Correlated observations about a common level)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) with Σ positive definite, and suppose we want to measure how far Y is from a common level m1. The generalized least squares level is m^=1⊤Σ−1Y/1⊤Σ−11, and the natural discrepancy is (Y−m^1)⊤Σ−1(Y−m^1)=Y⊤AY with A=Σ−1−Σ−111⊤Σ−11⊤Σ−11. A direct multiplication gives AΣA=A, and tr(AΣ)=n−1. By Theorem 4.3.2(b) the form is noncentral chi-squared with n−1 degrees of freedom and noncentrality 𝛍𝛍μ⊤Aμ, which is zero exactly when 𝛍μ is constant. The listing takes n=8, an autoregressive correlation Σ=(ρ|i−j|) with ρ=0.6, and a linear trend in the mean, for which γ=4.200. Figure 4.3.1(a) compares 200,000 simulated values with the χ2(7,4.200) density.

import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate, stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(31415)
n, rho = 8, 0.6
idx = np.arange(n)
Sigma = rho ** np.abs(idx[:, None] - idx[None, :])     # AR(1) correlation matrix
mu = 0.4 * (idx - idx.mean())                          # linear trend, mean zero
one = np.ones(n)

W = np.linalg.inv(Sigma)
A = W - np.outer(W @ one, W @ one) / (one @ W @ one)   # GLS-centred form
AS = A @ Sigma
print("A Sigma idempotent:", np.allclose(AS @ AS, AS))
df, gamma = np.trace(AS), mu @ A @ mu                  # chi^2(df, gamma)

L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)                          # Y = mu + L Z
Z = rng.standard_normal((200_000, n))
Y = mu + Z @ L.T
q_gls = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Y, A, Y)
print(df, gamma, stats.kstest(q_gls, stats.ncx2(df, gamma).cdf).pvalue)

4.3.3. Forms that are not chi-squared

Most quadratic forms fail the conditions of Theorem 4.3.2. The ordinary sum of squares about the mean, Y⊤CY with C=I−n−111⊤, is an example as soon as the observations are correlated. Then CΣ is not idempotent, and by Theorem 4.2.1 the law is that of ∑jλj(Wj+νj)2, with weights λj equal to the nonzero eigenvalues of CΣ. In Example (Correlated observations about a common level) these range from 0.259 to 1.832. Two practical ways to handle such a law are the following.

Exact computation. The characteristic function of the weighted sum is known in closed form (put t=iu in Corollary 4.2.2), and a distribution function can be recovered from a characteristic function φ by the inversion formula of Gil-Pelaez (1951), Pr(X>x)=12+1π∫0∞Im{e−iuxφ(u)}udu. For a weighted sum of k chi-squared terms the integrand decays like u−1−k/2, so ordinary quadrature works well. Imhof (1961) gave the real-variable form of this integral that most software uses. The listing implements it directly and checks it against simulation.

Two-moment approximation. Match aχ2(ν) to the mean m and variance v from Theorem 4.2.3. Since aχ2(ν) has mean aν and variance 2a2ν, this gives a=v/(2m) and ν=2m2/v, with ν usually not an integer (Satterthwaite 1946; Patnaik 1949).

In Example (Correlated observations about a common level), the simulated 95th percentile of Y⊤CY is 26.51. The exact tail probability beyond it is 0.0490, and the approximation with a=2.488 and ν=4.68 gives 0.0484. Treating the observations as independent, and so using χ2(7,6.720), gives 0.040. Figure 4.3.1(b) shows the three laws. Here the error from ignoring correlation is modest at the 5% point but visible in the shape. It is not a monotone function of ρ, but strong correlation makes it severe: with ρ=0.9 and the same mean, the naive tail probability at the true 95th percentile is 0.197.

def canonical(A, mu, L):
    """Y^T A Y = sum_j lam_j (W_j + nu_j)^2 + c (all lam_j nonzero here), W ~ N(0, I)."""
    lam, Qm = np.linalg.eigh(L.T @ A @ L)
    b = Qm.T @ L.T @ A @ mu
    keep = np.abs(lam) > 1e-10
    assert np.allclose(b[~keep], 0)                    # no linear terms in this example
    c = mu @ A @ mu - np.sum(b[keep] ** 2 / lam[keep])
    return lam[keep], b[keep] / lam[keep], c

def tail_prob(x, lam, nu, c=0.0):
    """P(sum lam_j (W_j + nu_j)^2 + c > x) by Gil-Pelaez inversion."""
    def integrand(u):
        z = 1 - 2j * lam * u
        phi = np.prod(z ** -0.5 * np.exp(1j * u * lam * nu**2 / z))
        return (np.exp(-1j * u * (x - c)) * phi).imag / u
    edges = np.linspace(0, 400, 401)                   # integrand decays like u^(-1-k/2)
    val = sum(integrate.quad(integrand, a, b)[0] for a, b in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:]))
    return 0.5 + val / np.pi

4.3.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let Y∼Nn(0,I) with n≥2, and A=αI+β11⊤. Find all (α,β) for which Y⊤AY has a chi-squared distribution, and identify each form.

Solution. A has eigenvalue α+nβ on 1 and α on 1⊥. By Theorem 4.3.1, the form is chi-squared iff A≠0 and both eigenvalues lie in {0,1}. The three solutions are (1,0), giving ‖Y‖2∼χ2(n); (0,1/n), giving nY¯2∼χ2(1); and (1,−1/n), giving ∑(Yi−Y¯)2∼χ2(n−1).

Exercise (A2)

Two independent samples of sizes m and k come from N(μ1,σ2) and N(μ2,σ2). Write the pooled within-sample sum of squares as ‖(I−M)Y‖2 for a suitable projection, and show that the two-sample t statistic has the t(m+k−2,(μ1−μ2)/(σ1/m+1/k)) distribution.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let Y∼Nn(μ1,σ2{(1−ρ)I+ρ11⊤}) with −1/(n−1)<ρ<1, and let Y¯ and S2 be the sample mean and variance. Show that (n−1)S2/{σ2(1−ρ)}∼χ2(n−1) and that S2 is independent of Y¯. Deduce that n(Y¯−μ)/S has the law of {(1+(n−1)ρ)/(1−ρ)}1/2 times a t(n−1) variable, and describe what a positive ρ does to the coverage of the usual 95% t interval for μ.

Exercise (B2)

Let Σ=diag(1,0), 𝛍μ=(0,1)⊤, A=I2 and 𝛍Y∼N2(μ,Σ). Show that AΣ is idempotent but Y⊤AY is not chi-squared. Which of the conditions of Theorem 4.3.2(c) fail? What changes if 𝛍μ=(m,0)⊤? Repeat the first part with A=(1110), where the shift enters through a linear term.

Solution. AΣ=Σ is idempotent. But Y2=1 with probability one, so Y⊤AY=Y12+1≥1, while every χ2(r,γ) with r≥1 gives positive probability to (0,1). Conditions (i) and (ii) hold, since Σ3=Σ and 𝛍𝛍Σ2μ=0=Σμ. Condition (iii) fails, since 𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍μ⊤Σμ=0≠1=μ⊤μ. The part of the mean outside C(Σ) adds a constant. If 𝛍μ=(m,0)⊤∈C(Σ), then Y⊤AY=Y12∼χ2(1,m2), as Theorem 4.3.2(c) predicts. For A=(1110) and 𝛍μ=(0,1)⊤, AΣ=(1010) is again idempotent, and Y⊤AY=Y12+2Y1Y2=Y12+2Y1=(Y1+1)2−1, which is negative with positive probability. Conditions (i) and (ii) hold, since ΣAΣ=Σ and 𝛍𝛍ΣAΣAμ=ΣAμ=(1,0)⊤, but (iii) fails because 𝛍𝛍𝛍𝛍μ⊤AΣAμ=1≠0=μ⊤Aμ. The shift now enters through the linear term 2Y1.

Exercise (B3)

In Corollary 4.3.3(b), show that Y⊤GY takes the same value for every generalized inverse G of Σ, with probability one. Hint: Theorem 2.2.3.

Solution. By Theorem 2.2.3, 𝛍Y−μ∈C(Σ) with probability one. Since 𝛍μ∈C(Σ), also Y∈C(Σ), so Y=Σv for some (random) v. For any generalized inverse G, Y⊤GY=v⊤ΣGΣv=v⊤Σv, which does not involve G.

Exercise (B4)

Let Y∼Nn(0,Σ) with Σ positive definite, and let A≠0 be nonnegative definite. Show that the two-moment approximation aχ2(ν) to Y⊤AY has ν={tr(AΣ)}2/tr{(AΣ)2}, and that ν≤rank(A), with equality iff all nonzero eigenvalues of AΣ are equal.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) with Σ possibly singular, and suppose Y⊤AY∼χ2(r,γ) with r≥1. Show that ΣAΣAΣ=ΣAΣ. Hint: in Corollary 4.2.2, compare the coefficients of tk for k≥3 with those of Eq. 4.1.2, and adapt the argument of Theorem 4.3.1.

4.5  The Cochran theorem

An analysis of variance starts from an identity Y⊤Y=Y⊤A1Y+⋯+Y⊤AkY,A1+⋯+Ak=I, that splits the total sum of squares into pieces attributed to sources of variation. Theorem 4.4.4 says the pieces are independent chi-squared variables when the Ai are mutually orthogonal projections. Checking that directly means checking k idempotence conditions and (k2) orthogonality conditions. Cochran’s theorem shows that one count of ranks does all of it.

4.5.1. The algebra

The matrix algebra behind this was done in Chapter 1. For symmetric matrices, any two of the three properties “each Ai is idempotent”, “AiAj=0 for i≠j” and “the sum is idempotent” imply the third, and symmetric matrices that add to I with ranks adding to n are idempotent and mutually orthogonal (Theorem 1.9.3). The next theorem adds the rank count for a sum that is idempotent but not necessarily I, which is what nested models need.

Theorem 4.5.1 (Cochran, algebraic form)

Let A1,…,Ak be symmetric n×n matrices and A=∑iAi. Consider

  • (a) each Ai is idempotent;

  • (b) AiAj=0 for all i≠j;

  • (c) A is idempotent;

  • (d) rank(A)=∑irank(Ai).

Then any two of (a), (b), (c) imply all four, and (c) together with (d) implies all four.

Proof. Any two of (a), (b), (c). By Theorem 1.9.3, any two of them imply the third. When all three hold, rank equals trace for symmetric idempotent matrices (Theorem 1.9.2), so rank(A)=tr(A)=∑itr(Ai)=∑irank(Ai), which is (d).

(c) and (d) imply (a) and (b). Let r=rank(A) and ri=rank(Ai). Every Av=∑iAiv lies in C(A1)+⋯+C(Ak), whose dimension is at most ∑iri=r=dim⁡C(A). So the two spaces coincide, and in particular C(Ai)⊆C(A). By Theorem 1.9.2(a), A=QQ⊤ with Q of size n×r and Q⊤Q=Ir. Since A is symmetric and idempotent it fixes every vector of its column space, so AAi=Ai, and transposing, AiA=Ai. Hence Ai=AAiA=QBiQ⊤ with Bi=Q⊤AiQ. The r×r matrices Bi are symmetric, satisfy ∑iBi=Q⊤AQ=Ir, and have rank(Bi)=ri because Q has orthonormal columns. By the last statement of Theorem 1.9.3, Bi2=Bi and BiBj=0 for i≠j, and multiplying by Q on the left and Q⊤ on the right transfers both identities to the Ai.

4.5.2. The distributional statement

Theorem 4.5.2 (Cochran)

Let 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,σ2I), and let A1,…,Ak be symmetric with ranks r1,…,rk, whose sum A=∑iAi is idempotent of rank r (for example A=I, r=n). The following are equivalent:

  • (i) the forms Y⊤AiY/σ2 are mutually independent, and 𝛍𝛍Y⊤AiY/σ2∼χ2(ri,μ⊤Aiμ/σ2) for each i;

  • (ii) r1+⋯+rk=r;

  • (iii) each Ai is idempotent;

  • (iv) AiAj=0 for all i≠j.

Proof. Condition (c) of Theorem 4.5.1 holds by assumption, so that theorem shows that (ii), (iii) and (iv) are equivalent, and each implies the other two. If (iii) and (iv) hold, Theorem 4.4.4 gives (i). If (i) holds, apply Theorem 4.3.1 to 𝛍σ−1Y∼Nn(σ−1μ,I). Each Ai with ri≥1 is idempotent, and an Ai of rank zero is 0. This is (iii).

In words: if the degrees of freedom add up, the sums of squares are independent chi-squared variables. Independence in (i) is not needed to reach (iii). The marginal chi-squared laws alone force the whole structure.

Example (The one-way layout)

Let observations fall into g groups of sizes n1,…,ng, with Yℓj independent N(μℓ,σ2). Let M replace each observation by its group mean, and P1=n−111⊤ by the grand mean. Then I=P1+(M−P1)+(I−M) splits ‖Y‖2 into nY¯2, the between-group sum of squares ∑ℓnℓ(Y¯ℓ−Y¯)2 and the within-group sum of squares ∑ℓ,j(Yℓj−Y¯ℓ)2. The three matrices are symmetric with ranks 1, g−1 and n−g, which add to n. Cochran’s theorem needs nothing more. The three sums of squares, divided by σ2, are independent noncentral chi-squared variables. Their noncentralities are nμ¯2/σ2, ∑ℓnℓ(μℓ−μ¯)2/σ2 with μ¯=∑ℓnℓμℓ/n, and 0. The last is zero because 𝛍𝛍Mμ=μ. The ratio of the between and within mean squares is F(g−1,n−g,γ) by Theorem 4.4.6, and it is central iff all group means are equal.

The script uses g=3 groups of sizes 3,5,4, means 2,3,1.5, and σ=1, and draws 200,000 data sets. Table 4.5.1 compares the theoretical means ri+γi and variances 2ri+4γi with the simulated ones. The largest absolute correlation between the three sums of squares is 0.0013. As a check that goes beyond correlation, the proportion of data sets in which both the between and the within sums of squares exceed their medians is 0.2497, against 0.25 for independent variables.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(5150)

sizes, means, sigma = [3, 5, 4], [2.0, 3.0, 1.5], 1.0
groups = np.repeat(np.arange(3), sizes)
n = len(groups)
Z = (groups[:, None] == np.arange(3)).astype(float)    # group indicators
M = Z @ np.diag(1 / np.array(sizes)) @ Z.T             # group-mean projection
J = np.ones((n, n)) / n
pieces = [J, M - J, np.eye(n) - M]
ranks = [np.linalg.matrix_rank(P) for P in pieces]
print("ranks", ranks, "sum", sum(ranks), "n", n)

mu = np.array(means)[groups]
gammas = [mu @ P @ mu / sigma**2 for P in pieces]
Y = mu + sigma * rng.standard_normal((200_000, n))
Q = np.stack([np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Y, P, Y) for P in pieces], axis=1) / sigma**2
print(np.corrcoef(Q, rowvar=False).round(3))

Remark (What the theorem does not cover). Two assumptions carry the weight. The first is normality. Without it, Theorem 4.2.3(d) fails, and with independent non-normal errors the between and within sums of squares are in general correlated, with a correlation driven by the fourth cumulant (Exercise (B2)). The second is the spherical covariance σ2I. With Cov(Y)=σ2V, V positive definite, apply the theorem to V−1/2Y, whose forms have matrices V1/2AiV1/2. Their ranks are those of the Ai, so the rank count is unchanged. What changes is the condition on the sum: V1/2AV1/2 is idempotent iff AVA=A (for example ∑iAi=V−1), and conditions (iii) and (iv) become AiVAi=Ai and AiVAj=0. These are the generalized least squares decompositions of Chapter 31.

Most analysis of variance tables in this book come from a chain of nested model spaces. The successive differences of their projections are mutually orthogonal projections whose ranks add up, so Cochran’s theorem applies without further work (Exercise (B2), Theorem 9.1.1).

4.5.3. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let A1=diag(2,0) and A2=diag(−1,1), so that A1+A2=I2. Which hypotheses of Theorem 4.5.2 fail, and what are the laws of the two forms when Y∼N2(0,I)?

Solution. The sum I2 is idempotent, but every one of the conditions (ii)–(iv) fails. The ranks are 1 and 2, which add to 3≠2. Neither matrix is idempotent, since A12=diag(4,0) and A22=I2. And A1A2=diag(−2,0)≠0. The forms are 2Y12, which is twice a χ2(1) variable, and Y22−Y12, the difference of two independent χ2(1) variables, which takes negative values. Neither is chi-squared. They are dependent, because Cov(2Y12,Y22−Y12)=2Cov(Y12,Y22)−2Var(Y12)=0−2⋅2=−4≠0.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

A two-way layout has one observation Yij for each of a rows and b columns. Write ‖Y‖2 as the sum of the grand-mean, row, column and residual sums of squares, identify the four matrices, and use Theorem 4.5.2 to show that under Yij∼N(μ+αi+βj,σ2) independently the four sums of squares are independent, with degrees of freedom 1, a−1, b−1 and (a−1)(b−1).

Exercise (B2)

Let P1,…,Pm be symmetric idempotent with C(P1)⊂C(P2)⊂⋯⊂C(Pm), and put P0=0, Pm+1=I. Show that the matrices Pi−Pi−1, i=1,…,m+1, satisfy the conditions of Theorem 4.5.2, so that the sequential sums of squares ‖(Pi−Pi−1)Y‖2 are independent noncentral chi-squared variables when 𝛍Y∼Nn(μ,σ2I).

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let Y∼Nn(0,I), and Q=Q1+Q2 with Q=Y⊤AY∼χ2(r), Q1=Y⊤A1Y∼χ2(r1) and Q2=Y⊤A2Y for a nonnegative definite A2. Show that Q1 and Q2 are independent and Q2∼χ2(r−r1).

Solution. By Theorem 4.3.1, A and A1 are idempotent, with ranks r and r1. If Av=0, then 0=v⊤A1v+v⊤A2v with both terms nonnegative, so ‖A1v‖2=v⊤A1v=0. Thus N(A)⊆N(A1), hence C(A1)⊆C(A) and AA1=A1=A1A. Then A22=(A−A1)2=A−2A1+A1=A2, so A2 is idempotent, and A1A2=A1A−A1=0. By Theorem 4.4.4, Q1 and Q2 are independent and Q2∼χ2(trA2)=χ2(r−r1).

4.4  Independence of quadratic forms and linear forms

An F or t statistic is a ratio, and its distribution is known only if numerator and denominator are independent. For normal vectors, uncorrelated linear functions are independent (Theorem 3.4.1). Quadratic forms are not linear, but the following observation brings them within reach of that result.

Idea. A quadratic form is a function of a linear form: for any generalized inverse A− of A, Y⊤AY=Y⊤AA−AY=(AY)⊤A−(AY). So Y⊤AY is independent of anything that is independent of the vector AY.

4.4.1. The independence theorems

Theorem 4.4.1 (Linear and quadratic forms)

Let Y∼Nn(μ,Σ), let B be m×n and A symmetric n×n. If BΣA=0, then BY and Y⊤AY are independent. Conversely, if BY and Y⊤AY are independent for every μ∈Rn, with Σ fixed, then BΣA=0.

Proof. The vector (BY,AY) is a linear function of Y, hence jointly normal (Theorem 3.3.1), and its cross-covariance is Cov(BY,AY)=BΣA⊤=BΣA (Theorem 2.2.1). If this is zero, BY and AY are independent by Theorem 3.4.1, and so are BY and the function (AY)⊤A−(AY) of AY, which is Y⊤AY by the key idea above. Conversely, independent variables with finite variances are uncorrelated, so Theorem 4.2.3(c) gives 2BΣAμ=0 for every μ, that is, BΣA=0.

Theorem 4.4.2 (Two quadratic forms)

Let Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) and let A,B be symmetric. If AΣB=0, equivalently BΣA=0, then Y⊤AY and Y⊤BY are independent.

Proof. The two conditions are transposes of each other. As before, AY and BY are jointly normal with Cov(AY,BY)=AΣB=0, hence independent, and each form is a function of one of them.

The condition is sufficient for every μ and every Σ. When Σ is singular one might hope that the weaker requirement ΣAΣBΣ=0 suffices, since only directions in C(Σ) carry noise. For nonnegative definite A and B the two requirements turn out to be equivalent (Exercise (B2)). In the other direction, the condition is necessary under mild assumptions, a result usually credited to Craig (1943). Its general proof is surprisingly delicate, and the history of incorrect published proofs is recounted by Driscoll and Gundberg (1986). The case that matters most for sums of squares has a short proof.

Proposition 4.4.3 (Necessity for nonnegative definite forms)

Let Y∼Nn(0,I) and let A,B be nonnegative definite. If Y⊤AY and Y⊤BY are independent, then AB=0.

Proof. Independence makes the covariance zero, and by Theorem 4.2.3(d) the covariance is 2tr(AB). With the square roots of Theorem 1.7.6, tr(AB)=tr(A1/2A1/2B1/2B1/2)=tr{(A1/2B1/2)⊤(A1/2B1/2)}, the sum of squares of the entries of A1/2B1/2. So A1/2B1/2=0, and AB=A1/2(A1/2B1/2)B1/2=0.

For nonnegative definite forms in a standard normal vector, then, zero correlation and independence coincide, just as for jointly normal linear forms. This fails for general symmetric matrices (Exercise (B1)).

4.4.2. Orthogonal projections of a spherical normal vector

The situation in regression is the tidiest one: a spherical normal vector and several projections onto mutually orthogonal subspaces.

Theorem 4.4.4 (Independent projections)

Let Y∼Nn(μ,σ2I), and let P1,…,Pk be symmetric idempotent matrices with PiPj=0 for i≠j, and ranks r1,…,rk. Then P1Y,…,PkY are mutually independent normal vectors, PiY∼Nn(Piμ,σ2Pi), and the sums of squares ‖PiY‖2σ2∼χ2(ri,‖Piμ‖2σ2),i=1,…,k, are mutually independent.

Proof. Stack the vectors PiY into one nk-vector. It is a linear function of Y, so it is normal, and the covariance between blocks i and j is Pi(σ2I)Pj⊤=σ2PiPj. This is 0 for i≠j and σ2Pi for i=j. By the block form of Theorem 3.4.1 the blocks are mutually independent, and functions of them, the squared lengths, are then mutually independent. Their laws are Theorem 4.3.2(a).

In geometric language, which Chapter 6 develops: the coordinates of a spherical normal vector in mutually orthogonal subspaces are independent. It is the reason the rows of an analysis of variance table can be treated separately.

Corollary 4.4.5 (Sample mean and sample variance)

Let Y1,…,Yn (n≥2) be independent N(μ,σ2), with sample mean Y¯ and sample variance S2=(n−1)−1∑i(Yi−Y¯)2. Then

  • (a) Y¯ and S2 are independent, and Y¯∼N(μ,σ2/n);

  • (b) (n−1)S2/σ2∼χ2(n−1);

  • (c) for any μ0, T=n(Y¯−μ0)/S∼t(n−1,δ) with δ=n(μ−μ0)/σ. In particular T∼t(n−1) when μ=μ0.

Proof. Here Y∼Nn(μ1,σ2I). Let P1=n−111⊤ and C=I−P1. Both are symmetric and idempotent, P1C=0, and rank(C)=tr(C)=n−1. Since P1Y=Y¯1 and ‖CY‖2=(n−1)S2, Theorem 4.4.4 gives (a), which Example (Sample mean and deviations) obtained directly, and, because Cμ1=0, also (b). For (c), X=n(Y¯−μ0)/σ∼N(δ,1) and V=(n−1)S2/σ2∼χ2(n−1) are independent, and T=X/V/(n−1). Apply Definition 4.1.3.

The independence in (a) characterizes the normal distribution: for n≥2 independent identically distributed observations with finite variance, Y¯ and S2 are independent only under normality (Lukacs 1942). We will not need this, but it shows that normality is used essentially here. It is not a technicality that more careful arguments could remove.

4.4.3. Preview: the F statistic for nested models

The main use of these results comes in Chapter 11. Its core fits in a few lines.

Theorem 4.4.6 (F statistic for nested subspaces)

Let Y∼Nn(μ,σ2I), and let P0 and P be symmetric idempotent matrices of ranks r0<r<n with PP0=P0. Suppose that Pμ=μ. Then F=Y⊤(P−P0)Y/(r−r0)Y⊤(I−P)Y/(n−r)∼F(r−r0,n−r,‖(P−P0)μ‖2σ2), and the noncentrality is zero iff P0μ=μ.

Proof. Transposing PP0=P0 gives P0P=P0. Hence (P−P0)2=P−2P0+P0=P−P0, so P−P0 is symmetric idempotent with rank tr(P)−tr(P0)=r−r0. Also (P−P0)(I−P)=P−P−P0+P0=0, and I−P is symmetric idempotent of rank n−r. By Theorem 4.4.4 the numerator and denominator sums of squares are independent, with σ−2 times them distributed as χ2(r−r0,‖(P−P0)μ‖2/σ2) and χ2(n−r,‖(I−P)μ‖2/σ2). The second noncentrality is 0 since Pμ=μ. The unknown σ2 cancels in the ratio, and Definition 4.1.2 applies. Finally, (P−P0)μ=μ−P0μ.

In a linear model, P=M projects onto the column space of the full model matrix X and P0=M0 onto that of a submodel X0. The numerator is the drop in residual sum of squares from the submodel to the full model (Chapter 6). The theorem says three things. The test that rejects for large F has an exactly known null distribution. Its power depends on the unknown parameters only through γ=‖(M−M0)Xβ‖2/σ2. And, by Theorem 4.1.4, that power increases with γ.

Example (Detecting curvature)

Let x1,…,xn, n=15, be equally spaced on [0,1], and suppose Yi=1+0.5xi+β2xi2+εi with independent N(0,1) errors. To test for curvature, compare the straight line (X0=[1,x]) with the quadratic (X=[1,x,x2]). The F statistic has 1 and 12 degrees of freedom, and the 5% critical value is 4.747. For β2=8.0, the listing finds γ=6.873 and power 0.673 from the noncentral F, and the rejection rate in 100,000 simulated data sets is 0.674.

The noncentrality has a clear meaning. It is β22 times the squared length of the part of x2 orthogonal to 1 and x, divided by σ2. It grows roughly in proportion to n for a fixed design pattern. Keeping the same design on [0,1], the smallest n with power at least 0.8 is 21, where γ=8.97. Figure 4.4.1 places the example on the power curve and shows the loss of power when the same noncentrality is spread over more numerator degrees of freedom r. At γ=10 the power is 0.827, 0.606 and 0.421 for r=1,3,6.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(1104)
alpha, s = 0.05, 12
def power(gamma, q, s, alpha=0.05):
    crit = stats.f.ppf(1 - alpha, q, s)
    return stats.ncf.sf(crit, q, s, np.maximum(gamma, 1e-12))
gammas = np.linspace(0, 30, 301)
gamma_ref, rs = 10.0, (1, 3, 6)                        # r = numerator degrees of freedom
curves = {q: power(gammas, q, s) for q in rs}

n, sigma, beta0, beta1, beta2, reps = 15, 1.0, 1.0, 0.5, 8.0, 100_000
x = np.linspace(0, 1, n)
X0 = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])                  # straight line
X = np.column_stack([X0, x**2])                        # adds curvature

def proj(Z):
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(Z)
    return Q @ Q.T

M, M0 = proj(X), proj(X0)
mean = X @ np.array([beta0, beta1, beta2])
gamma = mean @ (M - M0) @ mean / sigma**2              # noncentrality
q, df_resid = 1, n - 3
crit = stats.f.ppf(0.95, q, df_resid)
exact_power = stats.ncf.sf(crit, q, df_resid, gamma)

Y = mean + sigma * rng.standard_normal((reps, n))
num = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Y, M - M0, Y) / q
den = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Y, np.eye(n) - M, Y) / df_resid
print(gamma, exact_power, np.mean(num / den > crit))

4.4.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In Example (Two estimators of σ2 under a drifting mean) let Y∼Nn(μ1,σ2I) with n≥3. Use Theorem 4.2.3(d) to show that Cov(S2,Q)=Var(S2), and deduce that S2 and Q are dependent with correlation {2(n−1)/(3n−4)}1/2. Which linear forms a⊤Y are independent of Q for every mean vector μ∈Rn?

Solution. With Σ=σ2I, part (d) of Theorem 4.2.3 gives Cov(S2,Q)=2σ4tr(CD⊤D)/{2(n−1)2}. Since D1=0, CD⊤D=D⊤D, whose trace is 2(n−1), so Cov(S2,Q)=2σ4/(n−1)=Var(S2). The mean terms vanish because Cμ=Dμ=0. Using Var(Q) from Example, the correlation is {Var(S2)/Var(Q)}1/2={2(n−1)/(3n−4)}1/2, which is nonzero, so the forms are dependent. By Theorem 4.4.1, a⊤Y is independent of Q for every μ iff a⊤D⊤D=0⊤. Multiplying by a gives ‖Da‖2=0, so Da=0 and a is a multiple of 1; conversely such a work. The only such linear forms are the multiples of Y¯.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let Y∼N2(0,I). Show that Y12−Y22 and 2Y1Y2 are uncorrelated but not independent. Why does Proposition 4.4.3 not apply?

Solution. With A=diag(1,−1) and B=(0110), tr(AB)=0, so the covariance 2tr(AB) is zero. In polar coordinates Y1=Rcos⁡Θ, Y2=Rsin⁡Θ, with R2∼χ2(2) independent of Θ uniform on [0,2π), the two forms are R2cos⁡2Θ and R2sin⁡2Θ. Now E(R4)=8 and E(R8)=384, so E[(R2cos⁡2Θ)2(R2sin⁡2Θ)2]=384/8=48, but the product of the expected squares is (8/2)2=16. The forms are dependent. Proposition 4.4.3 needs nonnegative definite matrices, and A and B are indefinite.

Exercise (B2)

Let Y∼Nn(μ,Σ) and let A,B be nonnegative definite with ΣAΣBΣ=0. Show that AΣB=0, so that Y⊤AY and Y⊤BY are independent. Show by an example that the implication can fail when A is not nonnegative definite.

Solution. Write A=RR⊤ and B=SS⊤, for instance with symmetric square roots, and Σ=LL⊤ with L of full column rank. Cancelling L on both sides of L(L⊤AL)(L⊤BL)L⊤=0 gives L⊤ALL⊤BL=0. The trace of this matrix is tr(L⊤RR⊤LL⊤SS⊤L)=tr{(R⊤ΣS)(R⊤ΣS)⊤}=‖R⊤ΣS‖F2, so R⊤ΣS=0. Hence AΣB=R(R⊤ΣS)S⊤=0, and Theorem 4.4.2 applies. For nonnegative definite matrices the two conditions are therefore equivalent, since AΣB=0 trivially implies ΣAΣBΣ=0. Without nonnegative definiteness the argument breaks at the trace step. Take Σ=diag(1,0), A=(0110) and B=I2. Then ΣAΣ=0, so ΣAΣBΣ=0, but AΣB=(0010)≠0. With μ=(0,m)⊤ and m≠0 we have Y2=m with probability one, so the forms are 2mY1 and Y12+m2. The second is a nonconstant function of the first, so they are dependent.

Exercise (B3)

Let Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I) with X of full column rank p<n, let β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤Y, M=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤ and s2=‖(I−M)Y‖2/(n−p). With vjj the jth diagonal entry of (X⊤X)−1, show that (β^j−b)/(svjj)∼t(n−p,(βj−b)/(σvjj)).

Solution. Here M is symmetric and idempotent with trM=tr{(X⊤X)−1X⊤X}=p, and MX=X. So (n−p)s2/σ2=‖(I−M)Y‖2/σ2∼χ2(n−p) by Theorem 4.3.2(a), because (I−M)Xβ=0. With B=(X⊤X)−1X⊤, B(σ2I)(I−M)=σ2(X⊤X)−1(X⊤−X⊤M)=0, so β^=BY is independent of s2 (Theorem 4.4.1). Since β^∼Np(β,σ2(X⊤X)−1), the variable X=(β^j−b)/(σvjj) is N((βj−b)/(σvjj),1). The statistic is X/V/(n−p) with V=(n−p)s2/σ2, and Definition 4.1.3 applies.

Exercise (B4)

Show that Fα(r,s)→χα2(r)/r as s→∞, where χα2(r) is the upper α point of χ2(r), and that the power Pr{F(r,s,γ)>Fα(r,s)} tends to Pr{χ2(r,γ)>χα2(r)}, the power of the test that knows σ2.

4.1  Noncentral chi-squared, t and F distributions

A regression analysis reports a handful of numbers whose sampling behaviour decides everything: the residual sum of squares, the reduction in it when a term is added, the ratio of the two. Each is a function of the response vector of the form Y⊤AY=∑i=1n∑j=1naijYiYj, a quadratic form in Y. The sample variance is one, with A=(n−1)−1(I−n−111⊤). So is the squared length ‖PY‖2=Y⊤PY of any symmetric idempotent image of Y, and this is how the sums of squares of Chapter 6 will arise.

Since Y⊤AY is a scalar, it equals its own transpose Y⊤A⊤Y, and so also Y⊤12(A+A⊤)Y. Replacing A by its symmetric part changes nothing, and throughout this chapter the matrix of a quadratic form is symmetric. Several formulas below, the variance formula among them, are false for a non-symmetric A (Exercise (A1)).

When Y∼Nn(0,I) and A is symmetric idempotent, Y⊤AY has a chi-squared distribution, which is the null distribution of a test. Under an alternative the mean is not zero, and a slightly larger family appears. We start with that family.

4.1.1. Noncentral chi-squared

Recall that χ2(r), the chi-squared distribution with r degrees of freedom, is the law of Z12+⋯+Zr2 for independent standard normal Zi. It is the gamma distribution with shape r/2 and scale 2, with density

gr(x)=xr/2−1e−x/22r/2Γ(r/2),x>0,
(4.1.1)

mean r, variance 2r and moment generating function (1−2t)−r/2 for t<1/2. By convention χ2(0) is the point mass at zero. Everything about the noncentral version follows from one computation.

Lemma 4.1.1

Let W∼N(0,1) and ν∈R. For t<1/2, E[et(W+ν)2]=(1−2t)−1/2exp⁡(ν2t1−2t).

Proof. With u=w+ν the expectation is (2π)−1/2∫exp⁡{tu2−(u−ν)2/2}du. Completing the square in the exponent, tu2−12(u−ν)2=−1−2t2(u−ν1−2t)2+ν22(11−2t−1). The last term equals ν2t/(1−2t). The remaining Gaussian integral is (2π)1/2(1−2t)−1/2 when 1−2t>0.

Definition 4.1.1 (Noncentral chi-squared)

Let Z∼Nr(μ,Ir). The distribution of Z⊤Z=∑iZi2 is the noncentral chi-squared distribution with r degrees of freedom and noncentrality γ=μ⊤μ=‖μ‖2, written χ2(r,γ).

The definition hides a claim: the law of ‖Z‖2 depends on the mean vector μ only through its length. Theorem 4.1.2 proves it. Intuitively, a rotation QZ of a spherical normal is again spherical normal, with mean Qμ, and it has the same length as Z, so every mean vector of a given length gives the same answer.

Remark (Convention). In this book the noncentrality is γ=μ⊤μ, with no factor 12. Then Eχ2(r,γ)=r+γ, which is easy to remember, and γ agrees with the parameter nc of scipy.stats.ncx2. Several texts, including Rencher and Schaalje (2008) and Christensen (2020), use λ=μ⊤μ/2 instead. When reading them, replace their λ by γ/2. The same convention applies to the noncentral F distribution below.

Theorem 4.1.2 (Properties of the noncentral chi-squared)

Let U∼χ2(r,γ).

  • (a) The moment generating function is

    MU(t)=(1−2t)−r/2exp⁡(γt1−2t),t<12,
    (4.1.2)

    so the law depends on μ only through γ, and χ2(r,0)=χ2(r).

  • (b) E(U)=r+γ and Var(U)=2r+4γ.

  • (c) If U1,…,Uk are independent with Ui∼χ2(ri,γi), then ∑iUi∼χ2(∑iri,∑iγi).

  • (d) (Poisson mixture.) Let K∼Poisson(γ/2) and, given K=k, let V∼χ2(r+2k). Then V∼χ2(r,γ). Consequently U has density

    fr,γ(x)=∑k=0∞e−γ/2(γ/2)kk!gr+2k(x),x>0.
    (4.1.3)

Proof.

  • (a) With Z∼Nr(μ,I), the Zi are independent, and Zi=Wi+μi with Wi∼N(0,1). By Lemma 4.1.1, EetZ⊤Z=∏i=1r(1−2t)−1/2exp⁡(μi2t1−2t)=(1−2t)−r/2exp⁡(γt1−2t). A moment generating function that is finite on an open interval around zero determines the distribution, so the law depends only on (r,γ). Setting γ=0 gives the central mgf.

  • (b) The cumulant generating function is K(t)=log⁡MU(t)=−r2log⁡(1−2t)+γt/(1−2t). Differentiating, K′(t)=r(1−2t)−1+γ(1−2t)−2 and K″(t)=2r(1−2t)−2+4γ(1−2t)−3. The mean and variance are K′(0) and K″(0).

  • (c) By independence the mgf of the sum is the product of the mgfs Eq. 4.1.2, which has the same form with ∑ri and ∑γi.

  • (d) Conditioning on K, EetV=∑k=0∞e−γ/2(γ/2)kk!(1−2t)−(r+2k)/2=(1−2t)−r/2e−γ/2exp⁡(γ/21−2t), and −γ2+γ2(1−2t)=γt1−2t. This is Eq. 4.1.2. The density of a mixture is the mixture of the densities.

The mixture in (d) is more than a formula. It says that noncentrality acts like a random number of extra degrees of freedom, two at a time, with γ/2 of them on average. Since each extra pair of degrees of freedom pushes the distribution to the right, a larger γ should give a stochastically larger variable. That is the next result, and it is what makes tests based on these distributions sensible.

Example (A noncentral chi-squared with four degrees of freedom)

Take r=4 and γ=6. The Poisson(3) weights of the central densities g4,g6,g8,g10 in Eq. 4.1.3 are 0.050, 0.149, 0.224 and 0.224, and the mean and variance are 10 and 32. The listing checks the mixture against a library implementation of the density. The script also squares normal vectors with a mean of length 6 in an arbitrary direction, and their average squared length is 10.002. A test that rejects when a χ2(4) statistic exceeds its upper 5% point 9.488 rejects with probability 0.470 when the statistic is in fact χ2(4,6). Figure 4.1.1 shows how the density moves right and spreads out as γ grows.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(20240401)

def ncx2_pdf_mixture(x, r, gamma, terms=200):
    """Density of chi^2(r, gamma) as a Poisson(gamma/2) mixture of central densities."""
    k = np.arange(terms)
    weights = stats.poisson.pmf(k, gamma / 2)            # P(K = k)
    dens = stats.chi2.pdf(np.asarray(x)[:, None], r + 2 * k)
    return dens @ weights

r, gamma = 4, 6.0
x = np.linspace(0.05, 40, 400)
print(np.max(np.abs(ncx2_pdf_mixture(x, r, gamma) - stats.ncx2.pdf(x, r, gamma))))
Proposition 4.1.3 (Monotonicity in the noncentrality)

For fixed r≥1 and x>0, the tail probability γ↦Pr{χ2(r,γ)>x} is continuous and strictly increasing on [0,∞), and tends to 1 as γ→∞.

Proof. Let h(k)=Pr{χ2(r+2k)>x} and pk(λ)=e−λλk/k!. By Theorem 4.1.2(d), the tail probability is ϕ(λ)=∑kpk(λ)h(k) with λ=γ/2. First, h is strictly increasing: if V∼χ2(r+2k) and E∼χ2(2) are independent, then V+E∼χ2(r+2k+2) and h(k+1)−h(k)=Pr(V≤x<V+E)>0, since (V,E) has a positive joint density on (0,∞)2. Next, pk′(λ)=pk−1(λ)−pk(λ) (with p−1=0). Because 0≤h≤1, the series of derivatives converges uniformly on bounded intervals, so term-by-term differentiation is valid and ϕ′(λ)=∑k≥0(pk−1(λ)−pk(λ))h(k)=∑k≥0pk(λ)(h(k+1)−h(k))>0. For the limit, fix ε>0. Since χ2(r+2m) has mean r+2m and standard deviation 2r+4m, Chebyshev’s inequality gives h(m)>1−ε for some m. Then ϕ(λ)≥h(m)Pr(K≥m) for K∼Poisson(λ), and Pr(K≥m)→1 as λ→∞.

4.1.2. Noncentral F and t

Test statistics divide one quadratic form by another, to remove the unknown scale. The two ratio distributions that result are defined next.

Definition 4.1.2 (Noncentral F)

Let U∼χ2(r,γ) and V∼χ2(s) be independent, r,s≥1. The law of F=U/rV/s is the noncentral F distribution F(r,s,γ), with numerator and denominator degrees of freedom r and s and noncentrality γ. The central F(r,s)=F(r,s,0) has upper α point Fα(r,s).

Definition 4.1.3 (Noncentral t)

Let X∼N(δ,1) and V∼χ2(s) be independent. The law of T=X/V/s is the noncentral t distribution t(s,δ), and t(s)=t(s,0).

Only the numerator is allowed to be noncentral. A noncentral denominator gives a doubly noncentral F, which appears when the model used for the error sum of squares is itself wrong (Exercise (B3)). Two relations are immediate. First, X2∼χ2(1,δ2), so T2∼F(1,s,δ2): a two-sided t test is an F test with one numerator degree of freedom. Second, since E(V−1)=1/(s−2) for s>2, independence gives E(F)=r+γr⋅ss−2,s>2.

The central F is the null distribution of the tests of Chapter 11, and F(r,s,γ) is their distribution under an alternative. The power of such a test is therefore Pr{F(r,s,γ)>Fα(r,s)}, and the following fact is what guarantees that it behaves as a test should.

Theorem 4.1.4 (Power of the F test increases with γ)

For fixed r,s≥1 and c>0, β(γ)=Pr{F(r,s,γ)>c} is continuous and strictly increasing in γ≥0, and β(γ)→1 as γ→∞. In particular, the level-α test that rejects when F>Fα(r,s) has power greater than α at every γ>0.

Proof. With U,V as in Definition 4.1.2, F>c iff U>crV/s. Condition on V, which is independent of U: β(γ)=E[ψγ(crV/s)],ψγ(x)=Pr{χ2(r,γ)>x}. Since V>0 with probability one, Proposition 4.1.3 gives, for γ<γ′, ψγ(crV/s)<ψγ′(crV/s) almost surely, so β(γ)<β(γ′). Continuity and the limit follow from the same proposition by dominated convergence, since 0≤ψγ≤1.

Two further monotonicity facts hold for fixed γ>0 and fixed level. The power decreases as the numerator degrees of freedom r grow, and increases as the denominator degrees of freedom s grow (Ghosh 1973). The first says that spreading a fixed amount of signal over more dimensions makes it harder to detect. The second says that estimating σ2 better helps. We use both only qualitatively, and Figure 4.4.1 in Section 4.4 shows the first. Because T2∼F(1,s,δ2), Theorem 4.1.4 also shows that the power of the two-sided t test increases with |δ| (Exercise (A2)).

4.1.3. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that χ2(2) is the exponential distribution with mean 2. Use Theorem 4.1.2(d) to show that Pr{χ2(2,γ)>x}=∑k≥0e−γ/2(γ/2)kk!e−x/2∑j=0k(x/2)jj!.

Exercise (A2)

Show that T∼t(s,δ) implies T2∼F(1,s,δ2). Deduce that the power of the two-sided level-α t test depends on δ only through |δ| and increases with it. Show directly, by conditioning on the denominator, that the power of the one-sided test that rejects for T>c increases with δ.

Solution. T2=X2/(V/s) with X2∼χ2(1,δ2) independent of V, which is Definition 4.1.2. The two-sided test rejects when |T|>tα/2(s), that is, when T2>Fα(1,s). Its power is therefore Pr{F(1,s,δ2)>Fα(1,s)}, a strictly increasing function of δ2 by Theorem 4.1.4. For the one-sided test, Pr(T>c)=EPr{X>cV/s∣V}=EΦ(δ−cV/s), and Φ is strictly increasing.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that the kth cumulant of χ2(r,γ) is 2k−1(k−1)!(r+kγ). Deduce that its skewness tends to zero like 3/γ as γ→∞ with r fixed.

Solution. Using −12log⁡(1−2t)=∑k≥1(2t)k/(2k) and t/(1−2t)=∑k≥12k−1tk, the cumulant generating function is K(t)=∑k≥1tk2k−1(rk+γ)=∑k≥1tkk!2k−1(k−1)!(r+kγ). The kth cumulant is k! times the coefficient of tk. In particular κ2=2(r+2γ) and κ3=8(r+3γ), so the skewness is κ3/κ23/2=8(r+3γ)/{2(r+2γ)}3/2. As γ→∞ this behaves like 24γ/(4γ)3/2=3/γ.

Exercise (B2)

Show that χ2(1,γ) has density (2πx)−1/2e−(x+γ)/2cosh⁡γx for x>0. Verify that expanding cosh in a power series reproduces Eq. 4.1.3. Hint: Γ(k+12)=π(2k)!/(4kk!).

Solution. Let X=(W+γ)2 with W∼N(0,1). For x>0, X≤x iff −x−γ≤W≤x−γ. Differentiating with ϕ the standard normal density, f(x)=ϕ(x−γ)+ϕ(x+γ)2x=e−(x+γ)/22πx⋅eγx+e−γx2. Expanding, f(x)=(2πx)−1/2e−(x+γ)/2∑k(γx)k/(2k)!. The kth term of Eq. 4.1.3 is e−γ/2(γ/2)kxk−1/2e−x/2k!2k+1/2Γ(k+12). By the hint the denominator is k!2k+1/2π(2k)!/(4kk!)=2π2−k(2k)!, and the kth term becomes (2πx)−1/2e−(x+γ)/2(γx)k/(2k)!, as required.

Exercise (B3)

Let Uγ∼χ2(r,γ) with r fixed. Show that (Uγ−r−γ)/2r+4γ converges in distribution to N(0,1) as γ→∞. Hint: Uγ has the law of (W1+γ)2+W22+⋯+Wr2 with W1,…,Wr independent standard normal.

Exercise (B4)

Let U∼χ2(a) and V∼χ2(b) be independent. Show that B=U/(U+V) has the Beta(a/2,b/2) distribution and is independent of U+V, and that F=(U/a)/(V/b)=(b/a)B/(1−B). Deduce the null distribution of R2 asked for in Chapter 6.

Solution. The joint density of (U,V) is proportional to ua/2−1vb/2−1e−(u+v)/2. Change variables to w=u/(u+v)∈(0,1) and s=u+v>0, so that u=ws, v=(1−w)s and the Jacobian is s. The density of (W,S) is proportional to wa/2−1(1−w)b/2−1⋅s(a+b)/2−1e−s/2, a product of a function of w and a function of s. So B and U+V are independent, B∼Beta(a/2,b/2) and U+V∼χ2(a+b). Also U/V=B/(1−B). For R2 in a model with intercept, R2=SSR/(SSR+SSE), where SSR=‖(M−P1)Y‖2 and SSE=‖(I−M)Y‖2 come from orthogonal projections of ranks r−1 and n−r. If μ=μ1, both noncentralities vanish, and Theorem 4.4.4 with a=r−1, b=n−r gives R2∼Beta((r−1)/2,(n−r)/2).

Exercise (B5)

Show that fr,γ(x)/gr(x) is strictly increasing in x>0 for every γ>0. Conclude that among tests based on one observation U∼χ2(r,γ) of γ=0 against a fixed γ>0, rejecting for large U is most powerful.

35.6  Summary and Notes

35.6.1. Summary

Idea.

  1. A binary response is a binomial exponential dispersion family with variance function π(1−π), dispersion one and natural parameter the log odds; the canonical link is the logit (Definition 35.1.1).

  2. A logistic coefficient is a log odds ratio, constant over the other covariates; |βj|/4 bounds its effect on the probability scale, and the average marginal effect converts it to a change in probability (Proposition 35.1.1).

  3. Grouping observations with identical covariates changes the log-likelihood by a constant, so the estimate, its covariance and every likelihood ratio are unchanged; the deviance is not (Proposition 35.1.2).

  4. Fisher scoring is iteratively reweighted least squares with weights miπi(1−πi); observed and expected information coincide, and the log-likelihood is strictly concave when rank(X)=p (Proposition 35.1.3).

  5. Every binary regression is a threshold model: logistic, normal and smallest extreme-value latent errors give the logit, probit and complementary log–log links, and a proportional hazards process observed at a fixed time gives the last of them and no other (Definition 35.2.1 and Proposition 35.2.1).

  6. The latent scale is not identified, so probit and logit coefficients are comparable only after rescaling, by 1.8138 or 1.5958 depending on the convention (Proposition 35.2.2).

  7. The maximum likelihood estimate exists if and only if the data are in overlap; under separation some coefficients diverge and the Wald test collapses while the likelihood ratio does not (Definition 35.3.1 and Theorem 35.3.1).

  8. Firth’s penalty 12log⁡det(X⊤WX) removes the leading bias and gives finite estimates; a ridge or Cauchy penalty does the same more crudely (Proposition 35.3.2).

  9. Grouped Pearson and deviance statistics have χ2(N−p) limits as the group sizes grow. The ungrouped deviance is exactly −2∑i{π^ilog⁡π^i+(1−π^i)log⁡(1−π^i)}, a function of the fit alone with no χ2 limit; differences of deviances remain valid (Proposition 35.4.1).

  10. In-sample calibration is automatic, so calibration must be judged out of sample, and the area under the ROC curve is a rank statistic, blind to calibration (Proposition 35.4.2 and Proposition 35.4.3).

  11. Retrospective sampling on Y alone shifts the intercept by log⁡(ρ1/ρ0) and leaves the slopes alone, and the logit is the only link with that property (Theorem 35.5.1).

  12. Matched sets are handled by conditioning; fitting the stratum intercepts instead is inconsistent, and for matched pairs gives exactly twice the conditional estimate (Proposition 35.5.2).

35.6.2. Notes and sources

Coverage. The topics follow Agresti (2015, chapter 5) and Fahrmeir, Kneib, Lang and Marx (2021, sections 5.1–5.2), with proofs, examples and exercises of this book’s own. Collett (2003) and Hosmer, Lemeshow and Sturdivant (2013) are the standard applied accounts, and the source of the events-per-variable guidance alluded to in Section 35.1; McCullagh and Nelder (1989, chapter 4) is the compact theoretical reference.

Origins. The logistic function entered statistics through bioassay: probit analysis came first, with Bliss (1934) and Finney (1971), and Berkson (1944) argued for the logistic alternative against considerable resistance. Cox (1958a) gave the modern regression treatment of binary sequences and Cox (1970) the first book on binary data. The complementary log–log link is the binary face of the proportional hazards model of Cox (1972), with the discrete-time version of Exercise (B2) due to Prentice and Gloeckler (1978); the link family of Exercise (C1) is Aranda-Ordaz’s (1981). The “divide by four” rule is from Gelman and Hill (2007).

Separation. Silvapulle (1981) proved existence of the estimate under a convexity condition equivalent to overlap; Albert and Anderson (1984) introduced the terminology of complete and quasi-complete separation and the linear-programming diagnostic, and Santner and Duffy (1986) corrected a gap in their argument. The proof of Theorem 35.3.1 given here is the direct one: strict concavity leaves only coercivity to establish, and coercivity is exactly overlap. The counting formula of Exercise (C1) is Cover’s (1965). Firth (1993) introduced the Jeffreys-prior penalty as a bias-reduction device; Heinze and Schemper (2002) recognized it as a remedy for separation, and Kosmidis and Firth (2021) proved the resulting estimate always finite. The Cauchy prior is from Gelman, Jakulin, Pittau and Su (2008); exact conditional inference is due to Cox (1970) and Hirji, Mehta and Patel (1987); and the Wald pathology of Exercise (B3) is Hauck and Donner’s (1977).

Goodness of fit. That the ungrouped deviance depends on the data only through the fitted values is standard and appears in McCullagh and Nelder (1989, section 4.4); the exact identity Eq. 35.4.2 and the simulation of Example (Three deviances that mean nothing) are this book’s way of making the point. The Hosmer–Lemeshow statistic is from Hosmer and Lemeshow (1980), its instability discussed at length in Hosmer, Lemeshow and Sturdivant (2013); a smoothed alternative is le Cessie and van Houwelingen (1991). Calibration in the sense of Proposition 35.4.2 goes back to Cox (1958b). The ROC curve entered medical statistics through Hanley and McNeil (1982), and Pepe, Janes, Longton, Leisenring and Newcomb (2004) documented how insensitive its area is to genuinely informative covariates.

Case–control studies. The retrospective likelihood argument that justifies using the prospective fit is Prentice and Pyke (1979); Breslow and Day (1980) is the classical monograph and Breslow (1996) a readable retrospective on the design. The conditional likelihood of Proposition 35.5.2 comes from the tradition of Andersen (1970), itself an instance of the incidental-parameter problem of Neyman and Scott (1948) met in Example (Many means, one variance). Noncollapsibility is analysed by Greenland, Robins and Pearl (1999), who separate it carefully from confounding.

What comes next. Chapter 38 takes up quasi-likelihood and overdispersion, which matter for grouped binary data whose variance exceeds mπ(1−π); Chapter 39 treats fitting in practice and the Bayesian thread, including the priors touched on in Section 35.3; Chapter 40 joins generalized linear models to the random effects of Chapter 32; and Chapter 41 covers missing data. Responses with more than two categories are the subject of Chapter 36. The 1996 American National Election Studies survey used throughout is public domain; it illustrates method only, and nothing here should be read as a claim about the election.

18.4  Randomized complete blocks

A covariate removes nuisance variation by regression. Blocking removes it by design: similar units (plots in one part of a field, animals from one litter, strips cut from one board) are grouped into blocks, and treatments are compared within blocks. This section analyses the randomized complete block design, explains how randomization justifies its model, and measures what blocking gains.

18.4.1. The design and its model

Definition 18.4.1 (Randomized complete block design)

There are t treatments and b blocks of t units each. In every block, the t treatments are assigned to the t units by a random permutation, chosen uniformly and independently for different blocks. The response of the unit in block j that received treatment i is written yij.

The usual model is the additive two-way model with one observation per cell,

yij=μ+τi+βj+εij,εij ∼iid N(0,σ2),
(18.4.1)

with treatment effects τi and block effects βj. Its geometry is Example (Additive two-factor model, one observation per cell) and its analysis of variance is that of Theorem 16.1.2; we restate it in the form the design needs.

Theorem 18.4.1 (Analysis of the randomized complete block design)

Assume Eq. 18.4.1 with t,b≥2. Let y¯i⋅, y¯⋅j and y¯⋅⋅ be the treatment, block and grand means.

  • (a) The sums of squares SST=b∑i(y¯i⋅−y¯⋅⋅)2,SSB=t∑j(y¯⋅j−y¯⋅⋅)2,SSE=∑i,j(yij−y¯i⋅−y¯⋅j+y¯⋅⋅)2 are squared lengths of projections onto mutually orthogonal subspaces of dimensions t−1, b−1 and (t−1)(b−1), and with the correction for the mean they add up to ∑i,jyij2.

  • (b) A function ∑iciτi is estimable iff ∑ici=0. Its least squares estimate is ∑iciy¯i⋅, and ∑iciy¯i⋅=∑iciτi+∑iciε¯i⋅ identically, whatever the block effects. Its variance is σ2∑ici2/b.

  • (c) The treatment F statistic satisfies FT=SST/(t−1)SSE/((t−1)(b−1))∼F(t−1,(t−1)(b−1),γ), with γ=bσ2∑i(τi−τ¯)2.

  • (d) E(SSB/(b−1))=σ2+t∑j(βj−β¯)2/(b−1), and E(SSE/((t−1)(b−1)))=σ2.

Proof. Model Eq. 18.4.1 is the additive two-way model of Theorem 16.1.2 with factor A the treatments, factor B the blocks, a=t and m=1, so that n−a−b+1=(t−1)(b−1). Parts (a), (b) and (c) are Theorem 16.1.2(a), (b) and (c), with (e) for the form of the residual space. The only new statement is the identity in (b): averaging Eq. 18.4.1 over j gives y¯i⋅=μ+τi+β¯+ε¯i⋅, and ∑ici(μ+β¯)=0 for a contrast. Part (d) is Theorem 9.6.1 applied to the decomposition in (a); the block projection sends the mean vector to the vector with entries βj−β¯, whose squared length is t∑j(βj−β¯)2.

Part (b) is the reason to block. However large the block effects, they cancel exactly from every treatment comparison and from the error mean square, which estimates the variation within blocks. The between-block variation moves into its own line, SSB. A test of blocks is rarely of interest, since blocks were chosen because they differ; a large MSB measures what blocking achieved (Proposition 18.4.4 below).

Any block-by-treatment interaction is absorbed into the error, and without replication within cells it cannot be separated from it. Tukey’s one-degree-of-freedom test (Theorem 16.1.2(f)) checks for its most common form. If treatment differences genuinely vary between blocks, the comparisons refer to the average over the blocks used.

18.4.2. Randomization as the justification of the model

The units of an experiment are not a sample from a normal population; they are the boards or patients at hand. What the experimenter controls is the randomization, and it alone supports much of the analysis. Give each unit u a fixed value ξu, and assume unit–treatment additivity: unit u responds to treatment i with ξu+τi. The only probability below is that of the randomization.

Proposition 18.4.2 (Randomization moments of the block analysis)

Let the units of block j have values ξj1,…,ξjt, with mean ξ¯j and Wj=∑s(ξjs−ξ¯j)2, and put SW2=1b(t−1)∑jWj, the average within-block variance of the unit values. Under unit–treatment additivity and the randomization of Definition 18.4.1:

  • (a) every contrast estimate ∑iciy¯i⋅ has randomization mean ∑iciτi and randomization variance SW2∑ici2/b;

  • (b) E(SSE/((t−1)(b−1)))=SW2;

  • (c) E(SST/(t−1))=SW2+b∑i(τi−τ¯)2/(t−1).

So the error mean square is unbiased for the variance that the model attributes to σ2, and when the treatments have no effect the treatment and error mean squares have the same expectation.

Proof. Let πj(i) be the unit of block j that receives treatment i, and wij=ξjπj(i)−ξ¯j. Then yij=ξ¯j+τi+wij. For fixed j, the vector (w1j,…,wtj) is a uniformly random permutation of the deviations ξjs−ξ¯j, which sum to zero. Hence Ewij=0, Ewij2=Wj/t, and, since ∑kwkj=0, Ewijwkj=−Wj/{t(t−1)} for i≠k. Different blocks are independent.

  • (a) ∑iciy¯i⋅=∑iciτi+b−1∑j∑iciwij, because ∑ici=0 removes the ξ¯j. The mean follows. For the variance, Var(∑iciwij)=Wjt∑ici2−Wjt(t−1)∑i≠kcick=Wjt−1∑ici2, since ∑i≠kcick=(∑ici)2−∑ici2=−∑ici2. Summing over the independent blocks and dividing by b2 gives SW2∑ici2/b.

  • (b) The block means are y¯⋅j=ξ¯j+τ¯, and the treatment means are y¯i⋅=ξ¯+τi+w¯i⋅, with ξ¯=b−1∑jξ¯j. So the residual is wij−w¯i⋅, and SSE=∑i,jwij2−b∑iw¯i⋅2. The first sum is ∑jWj for every randomization. By independence across blocks, Ew¯i⋅2=b−2∑jEwij2=b−2∑jWj/t, so E(b∑iw¯i⋅2)=b−1∑jWj. Hence E(SSE)=(1−1/b)∑jWj=(t−1)(b−1)SW2.

  • (c) SST=b∑i(τi−τ¯+w¯i⋅)2, using ∑iw¯i⋅=0. The cross terms have mean zero, and E(b∑iw¯i⋅2)=(t−1)SW2 by the computation in (b).

Independent measurement errors of variance σe2 add σe2 to every expectation. The proposition does not make FT exactly F-distributed, but it shows that the estimates and their estimated variances are unbiased because of the randomization, whatever made the units differ. A systematic assignment could line treatments up with the ξ’s, and then neither statement would hold.

Randomization tests. Under the sharp null hypothesis that every unit would respond the same under every treatment, the responses are fixed and only the labels are random. The proportion of the (t!)b equally likely randomizations giving an FT at least as large as the observed one is an exact p-value, estimated by sampling randomizations when there are too many. Chapter 23 develops permutation tests for linear models.

18.4.3. An example

Example (Five wood adhesives on six boards)

In a synthetic experiment, five adhesives are compared for the shear strength of glued joints. Timber varies from board to board, so each of six boards is cut into five strips, assigned to the adhesives by a random permutation. The strengths (MPa) are:

adhesive board 1 board 2 board 3 board 4 board 5 board 6
1 7.89 8.42 8.55 8.30 9.26 9.15
2 8.68 8.69 8.73 8.62 9.72 9.51
3 9.13 9.28 9.81 9.43 10.30 9.41
4 8.48 8.60 8.46 8.95 9.42 9.02
5 8.90 9.17 9.79 9.57 10.24 9.09

The analysis of variance is:

source df sum of squares mean square F
adhesives 4 4.106 1.0266 14.88
boards 5 4.068 0.8136 11.79
error 20 1.380 0.0690
corrected total 29 9.555

The adhesives differ clearly (p=8.6×10−6). The adhesive means are 8.595, 8.992, 9.560, 8.822 and 9.460, and every difference has standard error 2×0.0690/6=0.152. Tukey’s honestly significant difference at level 0.05, q0.05(5,20)0.0690/6, is 0.454 (Theorem 13.3.2 applies, with the error mean square of the block analysis, as in Exercise (B1)). It separates adhesives 3 and 5 from adhesives 1, 2 and 4: the significant pairs are 1–3, 1–5, 2–3, 2–5, 3–4 and 4–5. Tukey’s test for nonadditivity gives F=0.07 (p=0.80), so there is no sign that the adhesive differences depend on the strength of the board.

Of 20000 randomizations sampled from the 2.99×1012 possible plans, 1 gave an adhesive F at least as large as 14.88: the randomization p-value is (1+1)/(20000+1)=0.00010, in line with the normal theory.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(606)
t, b = 5, 6                                           # adhesives, boards
plan = np.array([rng.permutation(t) for _ in range(b)])   # which strip of each board gets which adhesive
tau = np.array([0.0, 0.40, 0.65, 0.15, 0.80])         # adhesive effects (unknown in practice)
board = rng.normal(0, 0.75, b)                        # board effects
y = np.round(9 + tau[:, None] + board[None, :] + rng.normal(0, 0.3, (t, b)), 2)

grand = y.mean()
trt_mean, blk_mean = y.mean(axis=1), y.mean(axis=0)
resid = y - trt_mean[:, None] - blk_mean[None, :] + grand
ss_trt = b * np.sum((trt_mean - grand) ** 2)
ss_blk = t * np.sum((blk_mean - grand) ** 2)
ss_err = np.sum(resid ** 2)
df_err = (t - 1) * (b - 1)
ms_trt, ms_blk, ms_err = ss_trt / (t - 1), ss_blk / (b - 1), ss_err / df_err
F_trt = ms_trt / ms_err
print(f"treatments SS {ss_trt:.2f}, blocks SS {ss_blk:.2f}, error SS {ss_err:.2f} on {df_err} df")
print(f"F for adhesives = {F_trt:.2f}, p = {stats.f.sf(F_trt, t - 1, df_err):.2g}")
print("adhesive means", trt_mean.round(2), " se of a difference", np.sqrt(2 * ms_err / b).round(3))
def f_stat(yy):
    tm, bm, gm = yy.mean(axis=1), yy.mean(axis=0), yy.mean()
    sst = b * np.sum((tm - gm) ** 2)
    sse = np.sum((yy - tm[:, None] - bm[None, :] + gm) ** 2)
    return (sst / (t - 1)) / (sse / df_err)

rng_perm = np.random.default_rng(7)
draws = 20_000
F_perm = np.empty(draws)
for r in range(draws):
    # re-randomize: permute the adhesive labels independently within every board
    perm = np.array([rng_perm.permutation(t) for _ in range(b)]).T
    F_perm[r] = f_stat(np.take_along_axis(y, perm, axis=0))
p_rand = (1 + np.sum(F_perm >= F_trt)) / (draws + 1)
print(f"randomization p-value {p_rand:.4f}  (F-test p-value {stats.f.sf(F_trt, t - 1, df_err):.2g})")

18.4.4. How much did blocking gain?

Had the adhesives been assigned to the thirty strips completely at random, the completely randomized design (CRD) would have had the board-to-board variation in its error. The block experiment itself can estimate how large that error would have been, using a fact about sampling from a finite population.

Lemma 18.4.3 (Sample variance without replacement)

Let u1,…,uN be fixed numbers with mean u¯ and S2=∑a(ua−u¯)2/(N−1). If S is a simple random sample of m≥2 of the indices, drawn without replacement, then E∑a∈S(ua−u¯S)2=(m−1)S2, where u¯S is the sample mean.

Proof. For any m numbers, ∑a(ua−u¯S)2=12m∑a,c(ua−uc)2, the sum being over ordered pairs of sample members. The m(m−1) ordered pairs with a≠c are each a uniformly random ordered pair of distinct population members. For such a pair, E(ua−uc)2=1N(N−1)∑a≠c(ua−uc)2=2N∑a(ua−u¯)2N(N−1)=2S2. So the expectation is 12mm(m−1)2S2=(m−1)S2.

Proposition 18.4.4 (What a completely randomized design would have given)

Let the N=bt units follow Eq. 18.4.1, with fixed block effects βj, and write Sβ2=t∑j(βj−β¯)2/(N−1) for the variance of the block effects over the N units.

  • (a) If instead the treatments were assigned to the same units completely at random, b units each, the within-treatment mean square of the resulting one-way analysis would have expectation σ2+Sβ2, over the errors and the randomization.

  • (b) In the block design,

    σ^CR2=(b−1)MSB+b(t−1)MSEbt−1
    (18.4.2)

    is unbiased for σ2+Sβ2.

Proof.

  • (a) The b units given treatment i form a simple random sample of the N units. Their responses are μ+τi+βj(u)+εu, so the within-treatment sum of squares of treatment i is that of the numbers βj(u)+εu over the sample. Given the assignment, the errors contribute (b−1)σ2 in expectation, and the cross terms have mean zero. By Lemma 18.4.3 the block effects contribute (b−1)Sβ2 in expectation over the assignment. Summing over the t treatments and dividing by t(b−1) gives σ2+Sβ2.

  • (b) By Theorem 18.4.1(d), E[(b−1)MSB]=(b−1)σ2+t∑j(βj−β¯)2 and E[b(t−1)MSE]=b(t−1)σ2. The sum has expectation (bt−1)σ2+(N−1)Sβ2.

The relative efficiency σ^CR2/MSE estimates how many units a CRD would need per unit of the block design for the same precision. Some authors multiply it by Fisher’s factor (νB+1)(νC+3)/{(νB+3)(νC+1)}, with νB=(t−1)(b−1) and νC=t(b−1) the two error degrees of freedom, to allow for estimating σ2 with fewer degrees of freedom; this is a convention, not a theorem.

For the adhesives, σ^CR2=5×0.8136+24×0.069029=0.1974, and the relative efficiency is 0.1974/0.0690=2.86 (2.81 with Fisher’s factor). Blocking was worth almost three times as many strips; without it the adhesive F would have been about 1.0266/0.1974=5.20.

s2_crd = ((b - 1) * ms_blk + b * (t - 1) * ms_err) / (b * t - 1)   # estimated CRD error variance
re = s2_crd / ms_err
nu_b, nu_c = df_err, b * t - t                        # error df: blocked, completely randomized
re_fisher = re * (nu_b + 1) * (nu_c + 3) / ((nu_b + 3) * (nu_c + 1))
F_crd = ms_trt / s2_crd                               # what the treatment F would roughly have been
print(f"estimated CRD error variance {s2_crd:.2f} against {ms_err:.2f}:"
      f" relative efficiency {re:.2f} ({re_fisher:.2f} with Fisher's df factor)")

Remark (Fixed or random blocks). Treating the boards as a random sample of boards changes nothing about treatment comparisons in a complete block design, since block effects cancel from every contrast (Theorem 18.4.1(b)). It matters for incomplete blocks (Section 18.5) and for predicting a new block; Chapter 32 treats such mixed models.

18.4.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

An experiment has t=4 treatments in b=8 blocks. Give the degrees of freedom of the block design’s error, and of the error of a completely randomized design with the same 32 units. How large must the relative efficiency (without Fisher’s factor) be for blocking to be worthwhile?

Exercise (A2)

Verify from the table in Example (Five wood adhesives on six boards) that the treatment mean of adhesive 3 is 9.560, and compute the difference between adhesives 3 and 5 with its standard error. Is it significant by Tukey’s criterion?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

The strips cut from a board differ systematically: strips from the outer edge are stronger. How would you modify the design and analysis to remove this source of variation as well? Which design of the next section does your answer lead to?

Exercise (B2)

In Proposition 18.4.2, show that Cov(∑iciy¯i⋅,∑idiy¯i⋅)=SW2∑icidi/b for two contrasts. Conclude that orthogonal contrasts are uncorrelated over the randomization, as they are under the normal model.

Solution. As in the proof, Cov(∑iciwij,∑kdkwkj)=Wjt∑icidi−Wjt(t−1)∑i≠kcidk, and ∑i≠kcidk=(∑ici)(∑kdk)−∑icidi=−∑icidi. So the covariance is Wj∑icidi/(t−1). Summing over blocks and dividing by b2 gives SW2∑icidi/b, which is zero for orthogonal contrasts.

Exercise (B3)

Suppose the block effects are random, βj ∼iid N(0,σβ2), independent of the errors. Show that E(σ^CR2)=σ2+σβ2t(b−1)/(bt−1), and interpret the factor t(b−1)/(bt−1).

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In Eq. 18.4.1 let the block effects be random, βj ∼iid N(0,σβ2), independent of the errors. Show that the treatment means y¯i⋅ are equicorrelated, with variance (σ2+σβ2)/b and covariance σβ2/b, while every contrast estimate keeps the distribution given by Theorem 18.4.1(b). Show that E(MSB)=σ2+tσβ2, and that (MSB+(t−1)MSE)/(tb) is unbiased for Var(y¯i⋅). Why is s/b the right scale for comparing treatments but the wrong standard error for a single treatment mean?

Solution. Averaging over blocks, y¯i⋅=μ+τi+β¯+ε¯i⋅, where β¯∼N(0,σβ2/b) is common to all treatments and the ε¯i⋅ are independent N(0,σ2/b) and independent of β¯. Hence Var(y¯i⋅)=(σ2+σβ2)/b and Cov(y¯i⋅,y¯k⋅)=Var(β¯)=σβ2/b for i≠k. In a contrast β¯ is multiplied by ∑ici=0, so ∑iciy¯i⋅=∑iciτi+∑iciε¯i⋅ exactly as with fixed blocks. The block means are y¯⋅j=μ+τ¯+βj+ε¯⋅j, and the βj+ε¯⋅j are independent with variance σβ2+σ2/t, so E(SSB)=t(b−1)(σβ2+σ2/t) and E(MSB)=σ2+tσβ2. The error sum of squares is unchanged, because the residual projection annihilates every vector that is constant within blocks, so E(MSE)=σ2. Then E(MSB+(t−1)MSEtb)=tσ2+tσβ2tb=σ2+σβ2b.

A comparison of treatments uses only within-block information, in which β¯ cancels, so s/b is its scale and the Tukey intervals of Exercise (B1) remain exact. A single treatment mean is a statement about the population of blocks, and its uncertainty includes the sampling of the b blocks: s2/b misses the term σβ2/b. The estimate above mixes two mean squares with different degrees of freedom, so an interval built on it is only approximately t; the proper treatment is the subject of Chapter 32 (Proposition 32.5.1).

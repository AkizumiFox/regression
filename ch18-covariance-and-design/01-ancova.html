18.1  Analysis of covariance as a projection problem

In many experiments something about each unit is known before the treatments are applied: a pretest score, the weight of an animal at the start of a feeding trial, the yield of a plot in the previous season, a patient’s blood pressure at enrolment. Such a measurement is a covariate. If it predicts the response, much of the variation that the one-way analysis (Chapter 15) calls error is not error at all. Part of it is the covariate at work, and it can be removed.

The analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adds covariates to a factor model. Section 8.6 treated one factor and one covariate (Proposition 8.6.3). This section states the result for any design part, rank deficient or not, and any number of covariates, and then asks what a covariate does in a randomized experiment.

18.1.1. The model

Write the mean of the response as the sum of a design part and a covariate part,

𝛃𝛄E(Y)=Xβ+Zγ,Cov(Y)=σ2In.
(18.1.1)

Here X is n×p with rank r, built from the indicator columns of the treatment factor and of any blocking factors. It is usually overparameterized. The n×q matrix Z holds the covariate values, and 𝛄γ∈Rq their coefficients. Let M be the orthogonal projection onto C(X). Everything below is written with the three matrices

Ezz=Z⊤(I−M)Z,Ezy=Z⊤(I−M)y,Eyy=y⊤(I−M)y.
(18.1.2)

The letter E stands for error or within: in the one-way layout (I−M)y holds the deviations from the group means (Example (The one-way layout)), so these are within-group sums of squares and products. We assume throughout that

rank[X,Z]=rank(X)+q,equivalently(I−M)Z has full column rank q.
(18.1.3)

The two forms are equivalent by Lemma 9.2.1, since C([X,Z])=C(X)⊕C((I−M)Z). The condition fails exactly when some combination of the covariates lies in the design space. With one factor and one covariate, it fails when the covariate is constant within every group. Then the covariate cannot be separated from the group effects (Exercise (B1)).

18.1.2. The main theorem

Theorem 18.1.1 (Analysis of covariance)

Assume Eq. 18.1.1 and Eq. 18.1.3. Let MW be the orthogonal projection onto C([X,Z]).

  • (a) MW=M+(I−M)ZEzz−1Z⊤(I−M), and the two terms are orthogonal projections with orthogonal ranges.

  • (b) 𝛄γ is estimable, with the unique least squares estimate 𝛄γ^=Ezz−1Ezy.

  • (c) A function 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β is estimable in Eq. 18.1.1 iff it is estimable in the model 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ without covariates. If 𝛌𝛒λ=X⊤ρ, its least squares estimate is

    𝛌𝛃𝛒𝛄λ⊤β^=ρ⊤M(y−Zγ^),
    (18.1.4)

    so the fitted design part is 𝛃𝛄Xβ^=M(y−Zγ^): the covariance-adjusted response 𝛄y−Zγ^ analysed by the formulas of the model without covariates.

  • (d) The residual sum of squares is

    SSE=Eyy−Ezy⊤Ezz−1Ezy,
    (18.1.5)

    on n−r−q degrees of freedom.

  • (e) 𝛄Cov(γ^)=σ2Ezz−1, 𝛌𝛃𝛄𝛒Cov(λ⊤β^,γ^)=−σ2ρ⊤MZEzz−1, and

    𝛌𝛃𝛒𝛒𝛒𝛒Var(λ⊤β^)=σ2(ρ⊤Mρ+ρ⊤MZEzz−1Z⊤Mρ).
    (18.1.6)

  • (f) Let C(X0)⊆C(X) with rank r0<r and projection M0, and define Tzz,Tzy,Tyy as in Eq. 18.1.2 with M0 in place of M. Then Eq. 18.1.3 holds for X0 as well, and the hypothesis that the design part of the mean lies in C(X0), with 𝛄γ unrestricted, has the adjusted sum of squares

    SSH=(Tyy−Tzy⊤Tzz−1Tzy)−(Eyy−Ezy⊤Ezz−1Ezy)
    (18.1.7)

    on r−r0 degrees of freedom. If Y is normal, F=SSH/(r−r0)SSE/(n−r−q)∼F(r−r0,n−r−q,γ), where the noncentrality γ, a scalar not to be confused with the coefficient vector 𝛄γ, is given by σ2γ equal to the squared distance from 𝛃𝛄Xβ+Zγ to C([X0,Z]).

Proof.

  • (a) By Lemma 9.2.1, C([X,Z])=C(X)⊕C(Z~) with Z~=(I−M)Z, and the summands are orthogonal. Z~ has full column rank by Eq. 18.1.3, so its projection is Z~(Z~⊤Z~)−1Z~⊤ (Theorem 6.3.6), and Z~⊤Z~=Ezz because I−M is symmetric and idempotent. Theorem 6.3.7 adds the two projections.

  • (b) and (c). Put 𝛄γ^=Ezz−1Ezy. By (a), 𝛄𝛄𝛄MWy=My+(I−M)Zγ^=M(y−Zγ^)+Zγ^. The first term lies in C(X), so it equals Xb for some b, and 𝛄(b,γ^) is a least squares estimate (Theorem 6.4.1). If 𝛄(b′,γ′) is another one, then 𝛄𝛄X(b−b′)=Z(γ′−γ^). Applying I−M gives 𝛄𝛄Z~(γ′−γ^)=0, and so 𝛄𝛄γ′=γ^. The coefficient vector of the covariates is therefore the same in every least squares estimate, and so 𝛄γ is estimable (Theorem 8.3.1(g)). The same argument shows that 𝛃𝛄Xβ^=M(y−Zγ^) for every least squares estimate.

For estimability of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β: in the covariance model this function has coefficient vector 𝛌(λ⊤,0⊤), which is estimable iff 𝛌a⊤[X,Z]=(λ⊤,0⊤) for some a (Theorem 8.2.2). Such an a gives 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤). Conversely, if 𝛌𝛒λ=X⊤ρ, put 𝛒𝛒a=Mρ−Z~Ezz−1Z⊤Mρ. Then 𝛒𝛒𝛌a⊤X=ρ⊤MX−ρ⊤MZEzz−1Z~⊤X=λ⊤, because Z~⊤X=Z⊤(I−M)X=0, and 𝛒𝛒a⊤Z=ρ⊤MZ−ρ⊤MZEzz−1Z~⊤Z=0⊤, because Z~⊤Z=Ezz. So 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β is estimable. Since a lies in C([X,Z]), its least squares estimate is a⊤MWy=a⊤y (Proposition 8.3.2(a)): 𝛒𝛒𝛒𝛄a⊤y=ρ⊤My−ρ⊤MZEzz−1Ezy=ρ⊤M(y−Zγ^).

  • (d) SSE=‖(I−MW)y‖2=y⊤(I−M)y−y⊤Z~Ezz−1Z~⊤y by (a), and Z~⊤y=Ezy. The rank of MW is r+q.

  • (e) 𝛄γ^=Ezz−1Z~⊤Y has covariance σ2Ezz−1Z~⊤Z~Ezz−1=σ2Ezz−1. The estimate of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β is a⊤Y, and 𝛒Mρ is orthogonal to Z~. So Var(a⊤Y)=σ2‖a‖2 is the sum of the two squared lengths in Eq. 18.1.6, and 𝛄𝛒Cov(a⊤Y,γ^)=σ2a⊤Z~Ezz−1=−σ2ρ⊤MZEzz−1.

  • (f) If (I−M0)Zc=0, then Zc∈C(X0)⊆C(X), so (I−M)Zc=0 and c=0. So Eq. 18.1.3 holds for X0, and (d) applied to [X0,Z] gives the first bracket in Eq. 18.1.7 as the residual sum of squares of the reduced model. The rank of the reduced model is r0+q, and C([X0,Z])⊆C([X,Z]). The distribution is Theorem 11.1.1.

The proof is the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem (Theorem 6.6.2) with the covariates as the residualized columns, stated through M so that the design part may be rank deficient.

Idea. Analysis of covariance is two ordinary analyses joined by one projection. The within-group regression of y on the covariates gives 𝛄γ^, and the analysis of variance of the adjusted response 𝛄y−Zγ^ gives the treatment estimates. Their variances Eq. 18.1.6 carry an extra term for the uncertainty in 𝛄γ^. Tests must compare models, as in Eq. 18.1.7: the between-groups sum of squares of the adjusted response is not the numerator of any F statistic, because the reduced model must be allowed to re-estimate 𝛄γ.

18.1.3. The one-way layout with one covariate

For a single factor with g levels and a single covariate x, the matrices of Eq. 18.1.2 are scalars: Exx=∑k∑i∈k(xi−x¯k)2,Exy=∑k∑i∈k(xi−x¯k)(yi−y¯k), and Eyy is the within-groups sum of squares of y. With X0=1, the T quantities are the same sums around the grand means. Theorem 18.1.1 gives:

  • (i) slope β^=Exy/Exx;

  • (ii) SSE=Eyy−Exy2/Exx on n−g−1 degrees of freedom;

  • (iii) adjusted treatment sum of squares SStrt=(Tyy−Txy2/Txx)−(Eyy−Exy2/Exx) on g−1 degrees of freedom;

  • (iv) level means adjusted to a common covariate value, y¯k−β^(x¯k−x0), which differ by (y¯k−y¯l)−β^(x¯k−x¯l), as in Proposition 8.6.3;

  • (v) the test of β=0, with sum of squares Exy2/Exx on one degree of freedom (Corollary 9.2.3).

Example (Four study formats with a pretest)

In a synthetic randomized experiment, n=40 students are assigned at random, ten to each of four study formats for an introductory statistics unit. A pretest score x is recorded before the assignment, and the response y is the score on the final examination. The data were generated from a parallel lines model. Randomization balanced the pretest only on average: the four groups have pretest means 62.1, 57.2, 59.9 and 60.5, and final means 72.86, 75.15, 69.09 and 78.09. Figure 18.1.1 shows the data, and the sums of squares and products are:

xx xy yy df
between formats 124.9 −31.4 432.94 3
within formats 3643.9 3159.2 4146.41 36
total 3768.8 3127.8 4579.35 39

Without the covariate, the one-way analysis has error mean square 4146.41/36=115.2 and treatment F=1.25 on 3 and 36 degrees of freedom (p=0.305). There is no evidence that the formats differ.

With the covariate, the within-groups slope is β^=3159.2/3643.9=0.8670 points on the final per point on the pretest. The residual sum of squares falls to SSE=4146.41−2738.94=1407.47 on 35 degrees of freedom, so the error mean square falls from 115.2 to 40.2. The residual standard deviation falls from 10.73 to 6.34. The reduced model, one line for all forty students, has residual sum of squares 4579.35−2595.83=1983.52. The adjusted treatment sum of squares is therefore 1983.52−1407.47=576.05, and F=576.05/31407.47/35=4.77 on 3 and 35 degrees of freedom, with p=0.0068: clear differences between the formats. The test of β=0 has F=68.1. The adjusted treatment sum of squares, 576.05, is even larger than the unadjusted 432.94: format 2 had the weakest students on entry and still finished second, and adjustment gives it credit for that.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(84)
g, m = 4, 10                                          # four formats, ten students each
n = g * m
group = rng.permutation(np.repeat(np.arange(g), m))   # the randomization
x = np.round(rng.normal(60, 10, n))                   # pretest, measured before assignment
mu_true = np.array([70.0, 74.0, 71.0, 77.0])
y = np.round(mu_true[group] + 0.8 * (x - 60) + rng.normal(0, 6, n), 1)

Z = np.eye(g)[group]                                  # indicator matrix of the formats
n_k = Z.sum(axis=0)
x_bar, y_bar = Z.T @ x / n_k, Z.T @ y / n_k           # group means

def within(v):
    """(I - M) v: deviations from the group means (M projects onto C(Z))."""
    return v - (Z.T @ v / n_k)[group]

E_xx, E_xy, E_yy = within(x) @ within(x), within(x) @ within(y), within(y) @ within(y)
beta = E_xy / E_xx                                    # slope from within-group variation only
sse = E_yy - E_xy ** 2 / E_xx                         # ANCOVA residual sum of squares
nu = n - g - 1

T_xx = np.sum((x - x.mean()) ** 2)                    # the same with I - M0, M0 = mean only
T_xy = np.sum((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean()))
T_yy = np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2)
sse0 = T_yy - T_xy ** 2 / T_xx                        # reduced model: one line for everyone
ss_trt = sse0 - sse                                   # adjusted treatment sum of squares
F_adj = (ss_trt / (g - 1)) / (sse / nu)
F_unadj = ((T_yy - E_yy) / (g - 1)) / (E_yy / (n - g))
print(f"slope {beta:.4f};  SSE: ANOVA {E_yy:.2f} on {n - g} df, ANCOVA {sse:.2f} on {nu} df")
print(f"treatments: unadjusted F = {F_unadj:.2f} (p = {stats.f.sf(F_unadj, g - 1, n - g):.3f}),"
      f" adjusted F = {F_adj:.2f} (p = {stats.f.sf(F_adj, g - 1, nu):.4f})")

18.1.4. What a covariate does in a randomized experiment

In a randomized experiment the groups differ on the covariate only by chance. Adjustment still helps, for two reasons.

Precision. The error variance of the covariance model is the variance of y given x, which is smaller whenever the two are correlated. The price is the estimated slope, which adds the second term in Eq. 18.1.6. The next result balances gain against price when the covariate is random, as it is in practice.

Proposition 18.1.2 (The precision of adjustment)

Consider a one-way layout with g groups of m units, N=gm. Suppose the covariate is measured before randomization, so that the xi are independent N(ξ,τ2) whatever their group, and that given all the x’s the yi are independent and normal, with E(yi∣xi)=μk+βxi for unit i in group k and Var(yi∣xi)=σ2. Let ρ be the within-group correlation of x and y. For two groups k≠l, the unadjusted estimate y¯k−y¯l and the adjusted estimate (y¯k−y¯l)−β^(x¯k−x¯l) are both unbiased for μk−μl. If N−g>2,

Var(adjusted)Var(unadjusted)=(1−ρ2)(1+1N−g−2).
(18.1.8)

Proof. Write y¯k=μk+βx¯k+ε¯k, where εi=yi−μk−βxi has mean zero and variance σ2 and is independent of all the x’s. Unconditionally y¯k−y¯l has mean μk−μl, because Ex¯k=Ex¯l, and variance (2/m)(β2τ2+σ2). The within-group variance of y is β2τ2+σ2, and σ2=(1−ρ2)(β2τ2+σ2).

Given all the x’s, the covariance model holds with fixed covariates. By Theorem 18.1.1(e), with 𝛒ρ the difference of the two scaled group indicators (so 𝛒𝛒Mρ=ρ and 𝛒ρ⊤Z=x¯k−x¯l), the adjusted estimate is conditionally unbiased with conditional variance σ2{2/m+(x¯k−x¯l)2/Exx}. Its unconditional mean is therefore μk−μl, and its unconditional variance is the mean of the conditional variance. The group means of x are independent of the within-group sum of squares Exx (Corollary 4.4.5, applied group by group), with x¯k−x¯l∼N(0,2τ2/m) and Exx/τ2∼χ2(N−g). For V∼χ2(ν) with ν>2, E(1/V)=1/(ν−2) (Exercise (B1)). Hence E(x¯k−x¯l)2Exx=2τ2m⋅1τ2(N−g−2),Var(adjusted)=2σ2m(1+1N−g−2). Dividing by the unadjusted variance gives Eq. 18.1.8.

The factor 1−ρ2 is what adjustment would achieve if the slope were known. The factor 1+1/(N−g−2) is the cost of estimating it. For the design of Example (Four study formats with a pretest) the cost is 1.0294. Adjustment pays whenever ρ2>1/(N−g−1), that is, when |ρ| exceeds 0.169. At ρ=0.8, the correlation used to generate the tutoring data, the adjusted comparison has 0.371 of the variance of the unadjusted one (simulation: 0.372; Figure 18.1.2). Without the covariate the experiment would need almost three times as many students.

Chance imbalance. Randomization makes y¯k−y¯l unbiased over repetitions of the experiment. In the experiment actually run, the proof above shows that, given the covariates, E(y¯k−y¯l∣x’s)=μk−μl+β(x¯k−x¯l), which is not μk−μl unless the covariate means agree. Once the pretests are known, the raw difference has an error of predictable sign. In Example (Four study formats with a pretest), format 1 started 4.9 points ahead of format 2 on the pretest, which predicts 0.8670×4.9≈4.25 points of the final difference. The raw difference between formats 1 and 2 is −2.29, and the conditionally unbiased adjusted difference is −2.29−4.25=−6.54.

All this assumes the covariate was chosen in advance. Choosing, after seeing the data, the covariates that make the effect look best is a form of multiple testing, which is why trial protocols name their covariates beforehand.

Remark (Random covariates). The theorem (Theorem 18.1.1) treats Z as fixed. If, given random covariates, the model holds with normal errors, every F test and t interval of the theorem keeps its level unconditionally (Theorem 14.1.2, applied after reparameterizing the design part to full rank, which changes neither the tests nor the estimable functions). Only the power depends on the covariate values that occur.

18.1.5. Adjusting for a covariate that the treatment affects

Warning. A covariate must not be affected by the treatment. If it is measured after assignment and the treatment changes it, adjusting for it removes the part of the treatment effect that acts through the covariate, and the adjusted comparison no longer estimates the effect of assigning the treatment.

If hours of study were used as a covariate, a format that works by getting students to study more would be compared with the others at equal hours of study, which removes the very thing it does. In a synthetic randomized experiment with two arms of 100, let the treatment raise a post-assignment variable w by 2 units and let E(y)=θT+0.75w with direct effect θ=1. The effect of assigning the treatment is 1+0.75×2=2.50. Over 20000 replications, the unadjusted difference of means averages 2.500, and the coefficient of the treatment after adjustment for w averages 1.000, the direct effect, which is not what a decision about the treatment needs.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2025)
n, reps = 200, 20_000
theta, delta, gamma = 1.0, 2.0, 0.75                  # direct effect; T -> w; w -> y
total = theta + gamma * delta                          # effect of assigning treatment
est_unadj, est_adj = np.empty(reps), np.empty(reps)
for r in range(reps):
    T = rng.permutation(np.repeat([0.0, 1.0], n // 2))    # randomized assignment
    w = rng.normal(size=n) + delta * T                    # measured after treatment
    y = theta * T + gamma * w + rng.normal(size=n)
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), T, w])
    est_unadj[r] = y[T == 1].mean() - y[T == 0].mean()
    est_adj[r] = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0][1]
print(f"total effect {total:.2f}: unadjusted mean {est_unadj.mean():.3f},"
      f" adjusted for w mean {est_adj.mean():.3f}")

Chapter 25 develops the language for this in observational data: mediators, on the causal path from treatment to response, must not be adjusted for when the total effect is wanted. In a randomized experiment the safe rule is simple: adjust only for variables fixed before randomization. By Proposition 18.1.2 this costs little even when the covariate turns out to be useless. A test of whether the groups are “balanced” on such a covariate is pointless, since its null hypothesis is true by construction. What matters is whether the covariate predicts the response.

18.1.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In the one-way layout with one covariate, suppose x is constant within each group. Show that Eq. 18.1.3 fails, and describe the set of least squares estimates of β. Is the adjusted difference of two group means estimable?

Solution. If xi=ck for every unit in group k, then x is a combination of the group indicators, so x∈C(X), (I−M)x=0, and Eq. 18.1.3 fails. The mean of group k is θk=μk+βck, and only the θk are estimable. Every value of β belongs to some least squares estimate: for any b, the group parameters y¯k−bck together with b reproduce the fitted group means. The adjusted difference μk−μl=(θk−θl)−β(ck−cl) is therefore estimable iff ck=cl. The data cannot separate a covariate that varies only between groups from the group effects.

Exercise (A2)

From the numbers in Example (Four study formats with a pretest), compute the F statistic for β=0 and check that its square root is the t statistic β^/(s/Exx).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let V∼χ2(ν) with ν>2. Show that E(1/V)=1/(ν−2), and that E(1/V)=∞ for ν≤2.

Solution. The density of V is fν(v)=vν/2−1e−v/2/{2ν/2Γ(ν/2)}. Then E(1/V)=∫0∞vν/2−2e−v/22ν/2Γ(ν/2)dv=2ν/2−1Γ(ν/2−1)2ν/2Γ(ν/2)=12(ν/2−1)=1ν−2, using Γ(s+1)=sΓ(s). The integral converges at 0 iff ν/2−2>−1, that is, iff ν>2.

Exercise (B2)

In the one-way layout with one covariate, write Bxx=Txx−Exx, and similarly Bxy and Byy, for the between-groups sums of squares and products. Show that the adjusted treatment sum of squares equals Byy−(Txy2Txx−Exy2Exx), and deduce that it exceeds Byy iff Exy2/Exx>Txy2/Txx. Show that this happens in Example (Four study formats with a pretest), and explain it in terms of Bxy.

Solution. The formula is Eq. 18.1.7 with Tyy−Eyy=Byy. In the example Exy2/Exx=2738.94 and Txy2/Txx=2595.83, so the adjusted sum of squares is larger, by 143.11. The total regression uses both the within-group and the between-group variation of x. Here the between-group cross-product Bxy=−31.4 is negative: across the four formats, higher pretest means go with lower final means, the opposite of the within-group relationship. The single line for all students therefore explains less than the within-group lines do, and the difference is attributed to the formats.

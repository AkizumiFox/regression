18.6  Missing observations in designed experiments

Designed experiments lose observations: a plot floods, a sample is dropped, a strip of timber splits. The balanced formulas of the last two sections then no longer apply. The correct response is simple: the model still holds for the remaining observations, so fit it to them and test by comparing models (Chapter 11). Before computers, experimenters instead filled in the missing value so that the balanced formulas give the exact least squares answer. The trick shows exactly what the balanced formulas get right and wrong with a filled-in value, and it is the simplest instance of the EM algorithm.

18.6.1. Filling in, exactly

Let X be the n×p model matrix of the complete design, of rank r, and suppose the observations in a set of m rows are missing. Reorder so that X=(XoXm) and y=(yo⋅), with yo the n−m observed values. Let M be the projection onto C(X) for the complete design, partitioned conformably into blocks Moo,Mom,Mmo,Mmm, and let D=(0Im) be the n×m matrix of indicators of the missing rows. The key assumption is that the loss does not destroy estimability:

rank(Xo)=rank(X)=r.
(18.6.1)

Since N(X)⊆N(Xo) always, Eq. 18.6.1 says that N(Xo)=N(X): every function estimable in the complete design remains estimable. A single missing observation in a randomized block design with t,b≥2, or in a Latin square with a≥3, never violates it. Losing every observation on some treatment does, and so does losing the only observation of a cell in an unreplicated factorial fitted with all its interactions.

Theorem 18.6.1 (Missing observations)

Assume Eq. 18.6.1, and let 𝛃β^o be any least squares estimate from the observed data alone, that is, for the model 𝛃E(Yo)=Xoβ. Put 𝛃z∗=Xmβ^o, the fitted values at the missing positions, and for z∈Rm let y(z)=(yoz) be the data completed with z.

  • (a) (Filling in.) 𝛃β^o is a least squares estimate for the complete data y(z∗), and the residual sum of squares of the complete-data analysis of y(z∗) equals SSEo, the residual sum of squares of the observed data.

  • (b) (Characterization.) The eigenvalues of Mmm lie in [0,1), and z∗ is the unique solution of

    z=Mmoyo+Mmmz,
    (18.6.2)

    that is, the unique z that equals its own complete-data fitted value.

  • (c) (Iteration.) For any starting value z0, the sequence zs+1=Mmoyo+Mmmzs, which fills in the current values, fits the complete-data model and replaces the missing values by their fitted values, converges to z∗. The error zs−z∗ equals Mmms(z0−z∗).

  • (d) (Covariate method.) For any w∈Rm, the model 𝛃𝛄Ey(w)=Xβ+Dγ has rank r+m. Its least squares fit has 𝛃𝛃Xoβ^=Xoβ^o, 𝛄γ^=w−z∗, and residual sum of squares SSEo, on n−m−r degrees of freedom.

  • (e) (Tests.) Let C(X0)⊆C(X) with rank(X0,o)=rank(X0). The correct sum of squares for the reduced model is SSE0,o−SSEo, the difference of the residual sums of squares of the two models fitted to the observed data, on r−r0 degrees of freedom, with error degrees of freedom n−m−r. The sum of squares computed from the complete-data formulas applied to y(z∗) is at least as large.

Proof.

  • (a) For any b, the complete-data criterion splits as ‖y(z∗)−Xb‖2=‖yo−Xob‖2+‖z∗−Xmb‖2≥‖yo−Xob‖2 ≥ SSEo. At 𝛃b=β^o both inequalities are equalities, because 𝛃z∗=Xmβ^o and 𝛃β^o minimizes the observed-data criterion. So 𝛃β^o minimizes the complete-data criterion, and the minimum is SSEo.

  • (b) By (a), the complete-data fitted vector of y(z∗) is 𝛃Xβ^o, whose missing part is z∗. The fitted vector is My(z∗), whose missing part is Mmoyo+Mmmz∗. So z∗ solves Eq. 18.6.2. For uniqueness, note that Mmm=D⊤MD is symmetric and nonnegative definite, and for u∈Rm, u⊤Mmmu=‖MDu‖2≤‖Du‖2=‖u‖2, with equality iff Du∈C(X), that is, iff Du=Xb for some b. Such a b has Xob=0 and Xmb=u. Under Eq. 18.6.1, Xob=0 implies Xb=0, so u=0. Hence u⊤Mmmu<‖u‖2 for u≠0, every eigenvalue of Mmm is less than 1, and I−Mmm is nonsingular. Equation Eq. 18.6.2 is (I−Mmm)z=Mmoyo, which has exactly one solution.

  • (c) Subtracting Eq. 18.6.2 for z∗ from the recursion gives zs+1−z∗=Mmm(zs−z∗). The spectral radius of the symmetric matrix Mmm is its largest eigenvalue, which is less than 1 by (b), so Mmms→0.

  • (d) The argument of (b) shows that C(X)∩C(D)={0}, so rank[X,D]=r+m. For any b and g, ‖y(w)−Xb−Dg‖2=‖yo−Xob‖2+‖w−Xmb−g‖2. For each b the second term is minimized, at zero, by g=w−Xmb. What remains is the observed-data criterion, so the minimizing b are the observed-data least squares estimates, 𝛃𝛃Xoβ^=Xoβ^o, and the minimum is SSEo. Since Xmb is the same for every least squares estimate (by Eq. 18.6.1, every row of X is an estimable function in the observed-data model), 𝛄𝛃γ^=w−Xmβ^o=w−z∗.

  • (e) The first statement is Theorem 11.1.1 for the observed-data model, of rank r with n−m observations. For the second, the complete-data reduced-model fit of y(z∗) has residual sum of squares minb(‖yo−X0,ob‖2+‖z∗−X0,mb‖2)≥SSE0,o, while by (a) the complete-data full-model fit has residual sum of squares exactly SSEo. Their difference is therefore at least SSE0,o−SSEo.

Part (a) is the classical claim. Part (e) gives the two classical corrections. The residual sum of squares is right, but it has n−m−r degrees of freedom, not the n−r that the complete-data formulas assign, since the filled-in values fit perfectly. And the treatment sum of squares is biased upward, because the filled-in value suits the full model and the reduced model cannot choose its own. Part (d) is Theorem 18.1.1 with indicator covariates: each gap gets its own parameter, the model fits it exactly and learns nothing from it, and the indicator’s coefficient measures how far the number in the gap was from the least squares value.

Idea. A missing observation can be filled in with the value that the model, fitted to the remaining data, predicts for it. Then the balanced analysis of the completed data gives the exact least squares estimates and residual sum of squares. Subtract one error degree of freedom for each filled-in value, and compute tests by fitting the reduced model to the observed data.

18.6.2. The randomized block design with one missing value

For the randomized complete block design, Eq. 18.6.2 can be solved by hand. The projection of the complete design is M=PT+PB−P0 (Example (Additive two-factor model, one observation per cell)), so the entry of M for two units is muv=[same treatment]b+[same block]t−1bt, where [⋅] is 1 if the condition holds and 0 otherwise.

Corollary 18.6.2 (Yates’s missing value formula)

In a randomized complete block design with t treatments and b blocks, suppose the observation on treatment i in block j is missing. Let T′ and B′ be the totals of the observed values of treatment i and of block j, and G′ the total of all observed values. The least squares estimate of the missing value is

z∗=tT′+bB′−G′(t−1)(b−1).
(18.6.3)

The iteration of Theorem 18.6.1(c) reduces the error by the factor 1/b+1/t−1/(bt) at every step.

Proof. Here m=1, and Mmm=1/b+1/t−1/(bt), so 1−Mmm=(b−1)(t−1)/(bt). The row of M belonging to the missing unit, restricted to the observed units, sums the observed values of treatment i with weight 1/b−1/(bt), those of block j with weight 1/t−1/(bt), and all others with weight −1/(bt). No observed unit shares both the treatment and the block. So Mmoyo=T′/b+B′/t−G′/(bt). Solving Eq. 18.6.2, z∗=bt(b−1)(t−1)(T′b+B′t−G′bt)=tT′+bB′−G′(t−1)(b−1). The rate is Mmm by Theorem 18.6.1(c).

Example (A strip that split)

Suppose that in Example (Five wood adhesives on six boards) the strip from board 4 for adhesive 2 had split while being cut, so its strength (in fact 8.62) was never measured. The observed totals are T′=45.33 for adhesive 2, B′=36.25 for board 4, and G′=263.95 overall. Yates’s formula gives z∗=5×45.33+6×36.25−263.954×5=9.010. The iteration started from the mean of the observed values, 9.102, gives 9.041, 9.020, 9.013, …. The distance to 9.010 shrinks by the factor 1/6+1/5−1/30=1/3 at each step. Filling the gap with 0 and adding an indicator column gives γ^=−9.010, so the covariate method recovers the same value.

The completed table gives residual sum of squares 1.2788, exactly that of the 29 observed values, but on 19 degrees of freedom: the error mean square is 1.2788/19=0.0673, not 0.0639. Its adhesive sum of squares, 4.0534, exceeds the correct 4.0512 by 0.0022. The correct F is 15.05 on 4 and 19 degrees of freedom; the naive analysis would report 15.85, mostly because of the degrees of freedom.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(606)
t, b = 5, 6                                           # adhesives, boards
plan = np.array([rng.permutation(t) for _ in range(b)])   # which strip of each board gets which adhesive
tau = np.array([0.0, 0.40, 0.65, 0.15, 0.80])         # adhesive effects (unknown in practice)
board = rng.normal(0, 0.75, b)                        # board effects
y = np.round(9 + tau[:, None] + board[None, :] + rng.normal(0, 0.3, (t, b)), 2)

n = t * b
trt = np.repeat(np.arange(t), b)                      # y.ravel() lists adhesive 1 first
blk = np.tile(np.arange(b), t)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), np.eye(t)[trt], np.eye(b)[blk]])   # complete-data design
miss = np.array([1 * b + 3])                          # adhesive 2, board 4
obs = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(n), miss)
yv = y.ravel().copy()
yv[miss] = np.nan                                     # the value is lost

def fit(Xm, yy):
    coef = np.linalg.lstsq(Xm, yy, rcond=None)[0]
    return coef, np.sum((yy - Xm @ coef) ** 2)

coef_obs, sse_obs = fit(X[obs], yv[obs])              # exact least squares, 29 observations
df_obs = len(obs) - np.linalg.matrix_rank(X[obs])     # 29 - 10 = 19
X_red = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), np.eye(b)[blk]])  # blocks only
_, sse_red = fit(X_red[obs], yv[obs])
F_exact = ((sse_red - sse_obs) / (t - 1)) / (sse_obs / df_obs)
print(f"exact: SSE {sse_obs:.3f} on {df_obs} df, adhesive SS {sse_red - sse_obs:.3f},"
      f" F = {F_exact:.2f};  fitted value for the lost cell {X[miss] @ coef_obs}")
Y_obs = np.where(np.isnan(yv), 0.0, yv).reshape(t, b)
T_i = Y_obs[1].sum()                                  # observed total of adhesive 2
B_j = Y_obs[:, 3].sum()                               # observed total of board 4
G = Y_obs.sum()
z_yates = (t * T_i + b * B_j - G) / ((t - 1) * (b - 1))
print(f"Yates's value {z_yates:.3f}")

d = np.zeros(n)
d[miss] = 1.0                                         # one indicator column per missing cell
y_zero = np.where(np.isnan(yv), 0.0, yv)              # any number will do in the gap
coef_cov, sse_cov = fit(np.column_stack([X, d]), y_zero)
print(f"covariate method: gamma-hat = {coef_cov[-1]:.3f}, so the filled value is {-coef_cov[-1]:.3f};"
      f" SSE {sse_cov:.3f}")

z = np.array([np.nanmean(yv)])                        # start from the grand mean
history = [z[0]]
for step in range(30):
    y_fill = yv.copy()
    y_fill[miss] = z
    coef_fill, _ = fit(X, y_fill)                     # complete-data analysis
    z = X[miss] @ coef_fill                           # replace the gap by its fitted value
    history.append(z[0])
print("iterates", np.round(history[:6], 3))

With several missing values, Eq. 18.6.2 is an m×m system, and the iteration converges at the rate of the largest eigenvalue of Mmm. In the adhesive experiment with a second value also lost (adhesive 5 on board 1), that rate is 0.367, a little slower than 1/3, because the two gaps are coupled through the grand mean (Exercise (B2)).

18.6.3. Why the method works, and when it does not

The iteration of Theorem 18.6.1(c) is the EM algorithm for the normal linear model with missing responses: the E-step replaces each missing value by its current fitted value, and the M-step refits. Part (b) proves convergence directly.

Everything assumes that the loss is unrelated to what the value would have been. A strip that split before gluing qualifies; a joint that failed in handling because its glue was weak does not, and then both filling in and dropping make the adhesive look stronger than it is. The observed data cannot reveal this. Chapter 41 treats missing data in general, including the kinds of missingness and multiple imputation.

Warning. Never analyse filled-in values as if they were data without the corrections of Theorem 18.6.1(e). The residual degrees of freedom must be reduced by one for each filled-in value, and tests must be computed by comparing models fitted to the observed data. Standard errors computed from the completed data with the balanced formulas are also too small, because they treat z∗ as observed.

18.6.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Verify the arithmetic of Yates’s formula in Example (A strip that split), and check that the filled-in value 9.010 makes the residual of that cell in the completed table exactly zero.

Exercise (A2)

In a randomized block design with t=3 and b=2, suppose both observations on treatment 1 are lost. Show that Eq. 18.6.1 fails, and that Mmm has an eigenvalue equal to 1. What happens to the iteration?

Exercise (A3)

Derive the analogue of Yates’s formula for one missing value in an a×a Latin square: z∗={a(R′+C′+T′)−2G′}/{(a−1)(a−2)}, where R′, C′ and T′ are the observed totals of the row, column and treatment of the missing cell.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In the randomized block design with one missing value, show that the treatment sum of squares computed from the completed table exceeds the correct one by {B′−(t−1)z∗}2t(t−1). Check the formula against the numbers in Example (A strip that split).

Solution. The correct treatment sum of squares is SSE0,o−SSEo, where SSE0,o is the residual sum of squares of the blocks-only model on the observed data. The complete-data sum of squares is SSE0(y(z∗))−SSEo by Theorem 18.6.1(a). So the bias is SSE0(y(z∗))−SSE0,o. In the blocks-only model, the missing cell’s fitted value from the observed data is the mean of the observed values of its block, B′/(t−1), and by Theorem 18.6.1(a) applied to that model, filling the gap with B′/(t−1) would give SSE0,o exactly. Only the within-block sum of squares of block j depends on the filled value z. It equals a constant plus z2−(B′+z)2/t, a quadratic in z with leading coefficient 1−1/t and minimum at z=B′/(t−1). Hence SSE0(y(z))−SSE0,o=(1−1/t){z−B′/(t−1)}2={(t−1)z−B′}2/(t(t−1)). With B′=36.25, t=5 and z∗=9.010: (36.25−36.04)2/20=0.0022, as in the example.

Exercise (B2)

Suppose two values are missing in a randomized block design, in different treatments and different blocks. Write Mmm explicitly and find its eigenvalues. Check the rate 0.367 quoted for the adhesive experiment.

Solution. With the two missing cells in different treatments and different blocks, they share neither, so Mmm has diagonal entries d=1/b+1/t−1/(bt) and off-diagonal entries −1/(bt). Its eigenvalues are d±1/(bt), and the larger is 1/b+1/t=1/6+1/5=0.367 for t=5, b=6.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that the covariance method of Theorem 18.6.1(d) gives correct standard errors for estimable functions of 𝛃β, without any correction, when the software’s estimated covariance matrix of 𝛃𝛄(β^,γ^) is used. Why does this not contradict the warning at the end of the section?

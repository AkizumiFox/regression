6.2  The subspaces we need

This section gathers the vector-space facts used in the rest of the chapter. Readers who have worked through Chapter 1 can skim the definitions. The results on orthogonal complements, especially Lemma 6.2.1 and Theorem 6.2.3, are used constantly afterwards and are proved in full.

6.2.1. Subspaces, spans and dimension

A nonempty set S⊆Rn is a subspace if it is closed under addition and scalar multiplication: u,v∈S and a,b∈R imply au+bv∈S. Every subspace contains 0. The span of vectors v1,…,vk is the set of all their linear combinations. It is the smallest subspace containing them. A set of vectors is linearly independent if the only linear combination equal to 0 is the one with all coefficients zero. A basis for S is a linearly independent set that spans S. All bases of S have the same number of elements, the dimension dim⁡S.

For an n×p matrix A there are two subspaces we use constantly: C(A)={Ab:b∈Rp}⊆Rn(column space),N(A)={b∈Rp:Ab=0}⊆Rp(null space). The rank of A is rank(A)=dim⁡C(A). Two standard facts from Chapter 1: row rank equals column rank, so rank(A)=rank(A⊤); and the rank–nullity theorem

rank(A)+dim⁡N(A)=p.
(6.2.1)

In regression, C(X) is the model space. N(X) is the set of directions in which β can move without changing Xβ. It is {0} exactly when X has full column rank.

Example (A rank-deficient layout)

Suppose n observations fall into g groups and X=[1,z1,…,zg], where zk is the indicator of group k. Since z1+⋯+zg=1, the vector (1,−1,…,−1)⊤ lies in N(X). The g indicators are linearly independent (their supports are disjoint), so rank(X)=g while p=g+1. By Eq. 6.2.1, N(X) is exactly the line spanned by (1,−1,…,−1)⊤. The model space C(X)=C([z1,…,zg]) is the set of vectors that are constant within groups.

6.2.2. Inner products, orthogonality and complements

Unless stated otherwise Rn carries the Euclidean inner product ⟨u,v⟩=u⊤v and norm ‖u‖=(u⊤u)1/2. Vectors are orthogonal, written u⊥v, if u⊤v=0. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality |u⊤v|≤‖u‖‖v‖, with equality iff one vector is a multiple of the other, lets us define the angle θ∈[0,π] between nonzero vectors by

cos⁡θ=u⊤v‖u‖‖v‖.
(6.2.2)

The angle comes back in Section 6.7, where R2 turns out to be a squared cosine. The Pythagorean identity

u⊥v⟹‖u+v‖2=‖u‖2+‖v‖2
(6.2.3)

follows by expanding (u+v)⊤(u+v). The cross terms are the ones that vanish.

Definition 6.2.1 (Orthogonal complement)

The orthogonal complement of a subspace S⊆Rn is S⊥={v∈Rn:v⊤u=0 for all u∈S}.

It is immediate that S⊥ is a subspace and that S∩S⊥={0}: a vector orthogonal to itself has zero length. To test whether v∈S⊥ it is enough to check orthogonality to a spanning set of S. In particular, v∈C(A)⊥ iff A⊤v=0.

Lemma 6.2.1

For any n×p matrix A, N(A⊤)=C(A)⊥andN(A)=C(A⊤)⊥.

Proof. v∈N(A⊤) means A⊤v=0, that is, v is orthogonal to every column of A, which is the same as v⊥C(A). The second identity is the first applied to A⊤.

6.2.3. Orthonormal bases and Gram–Schmidt

Vectors u1,…,uk are orthonormal if ui⊤uj is 1 for i=j and 0 otherwise. Orthonormal vectors are linearly independent: taking the inner product of ∑jajuj=0 with ui gives ai=0. The coordinates of a vector v∈span(u1,…,uk) are simply the inner products ai=ui⊤v, with no linear system to solve. This is why orthonormal bases are so convenient.

Proposition 6.2.2 (Gram–Schmidt)

Let v1,…,vk be linearly independent. Define recursively wj=vj−∑i<j(ui⊤vj)ui,uj=wj/‖wj‖,j=1,…,k. Then each wj≠0. The vectors u1,…,uk are orthonormal, and span(u1,…,uj)=span(v1,…,vj) for every j.

Proof. Induction on j. Suppose the claims hold for j−1. The vector wj is vj minus an element of span(u1,…,uj−1)=span(v1,…,vj−1). If wj were zero, vj would lie in that span, contradicting independence. For l<j, ul⊤wj=ul⊤vj−∑i<j(ui⊤vj)ul⊤ui=ul⊤vj−ul⊤vj=0, so uj is orthogonal to its predecessors. Finally, uj∈span(u1,…,uj−1,vj)=span(v1,…,vj) and vj∈span(u1,…,uj), so the two spans agree.

Two consequences are used repeatedly. Every subspace has an orthonormal basis (apply Gram–Schmidt to any basis). And any orthonormal basis of a subspace S can be extended to an orthonormal basis of Rn: extend it to a basis of Rn in any way, then apply Gram–Schmidt, which leaves the first vectors unchanged. Section 6.10 shows that Gram–Schmidt, written in matrix form, is the QR factorization.

6.2.4. Direct sums and the orthogonal decomposition

For subspaces S,T, the sum S+T={u+v:u∈S,v∈T} is a subspace. When S∩T={0} the sum is direct, written S⊕T. Every element then decomposes as u+v in exactly one way: if u+v=u′+v′ then u−u′=v′−v lies in both subspaces and so is zero. When in addition S⊥T, the sum is an orthogonal direct sum.

Theorem 6.2.3 (Orthogonal decomposition)

For every subspace S⊆Rn, Rn=S⊕S⊥,dim⁡S+dim⁡S⊥=n,(S⊥)⊥=S. Hence every y∈Rn can be written uniquely as y=u+v with u∈S and v⊥S.

Proof. Let u1,…,ur be an orthonormal basis of S, extended to an orthonormal basis u1,…,un of Rn. A vector y=∑i=1naiui is orthogonal to S iff a1=⋯=ar=0, so S⊥=span(ur+1,…,un) has dimension n−r. Writing y=∑i≤raiui+∑i>raiui exhibits the decomposition, and it is unique because S∩S⊥={0}. By the same argument with the roles of the two index sets swapped, (S⊥)⊥=span(u1,…,ur)=S.

Combining Theorem 6.2.3 with Lemma 6.2.1 gives a matrix fact used in almost every later chapter.

Corollary 6.2.4

For any n×p matrix A:

  • (a) N(A⊤A)=N(A);

  • (b) C(A⊤A)=C(A⊤) and C(AA⊤)=C(A);

  • (c) rank(A⊤A)=rank(AA⊤)=rank(A);

  • (d) C(A)⊥=N(A⊤) and Rp=C(A⊤)⊕N(A).

Proof.

  • (a) If Ab=0 then A⊤Ab=0. Conversely, A⊤Ab=0 gives b⊤A⊤Ab=‖Ab‖2=0, so Ab=0.
  • (b) By Lemma 6.2.1 applied to the symmetric matrix A⊤A, and then (a) and Lemma 6.2.1 again, C(A⊤A)=N(A⊤A)⊥=N(A)⊥=(C(A⊤)⊥)⊥=C(A⊤), using (S⊥)⊥=S in the last step. The second identity is the first applied to A⊤.
  • (c) Take dimensions in (b) and use rank(A⊤)=rank(A).
  • (d) This is Lemma 6.2.1 and Theorem 6.2.3.

Part (b) is the reason the normal equations X⊤Xb=X⊤y can always be solved: the right-hand side lies in C(X⊤)=C(X⊤X). We come back to this in Section 6.4.

Lemma 6.2.5

For subspaces S1,S2⊆Rn, (S1+S2)⊥=S1⊥∩S2⊥and(S1∩S2)⊥=S1⊥+S2⊥.

Proof. A vector is orthogonal to S1+S2 iff it is orthogonal to every u1+u2, which (taking u2=0 or u1=0) is the case iff it is orthogonal to both subspaces. For the second identity, apply the first to S1⊥ and S2⊥: (S1⊥+S2⊥)⊥=S1∩S2, and take complements of both sides.

Lemma 6.2.5 is what we need to describe reduced models defined by linear constraints (Theorem 6.5.2).

6.2.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that S⊥ is a subspace for any set S (not necessarily a subspace), and that S⊆T implies T⊥⊆S⊥. Show that S⊥=span(S)⊥.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

A one-way layout has three groups of sizes 2,2,1 and model matrix X=[1,z1,z2,z3]. Find bases for C(X), N(X), C(X⊤) and C(X)⊥, and check that the dimensions agree with Eq. 6.2.1 and Theorem 6.2.3.

Exercise (B2)

Show that C(AB)⊆C(A), and deduce rank(AB)≤min{rank(A),rank(B)}. Show that C(AB)=C(A) whenever B has full row rank.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

For subspaces S,T⊆Rn, prove dim⁡(S+T)=dim⁡S+dim⁡T−dim⁡(S∩T). Use it to show that two r-dimensional subspaces of Rn with 2r>n must share a nonzero vector.

6.7  Reparameterization, centring and R²

6.7.1. Same subspace, same fit

The key idea of Section 6.1 now becomes a theorem.

Theorem 6.7.1 (Invariance under reparameterization)

Let X (n×p) and W (n×q) satisfy C(X)=C(W). Then the two linear models 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ and 𝛄E(Y)=Wγ have the same projection M, the same fitted values and residuals for every y, and the same residual sum of squares. There are a p×q matrix T with W=XT and a q×p matrix S with X=WS. If 𝛄γ^ is a least squares estimate for W, then 𝛄Tγ^ is one for X, and 𝛃Sβ^ is one for W whenever 𝛃β^ is one for X. If both matrices have full column rank, then p=q, T=S−1, and 𝛄𝛃γ^=Sβ^.

Proof. A projection is determined by its range (Theorem 6.3.3), so both models have projection M, and fitted values and residuals follow from Theorem 6.4.1. Each column of W lies in C(X), so W=XT for some T, and likewise X=WS. If 𝛄Wγ^=My, then 𝛄X(Tγ^)=My, so 𝛄Tγ^ is a least squares estimate for X. The other direction is symmetric. With full column rank, X=XTS gives X(I−TS)=0 and so TS=I, and similarly ST=I. Uniqueness of least squares estimates then gives 𝛄𝛃γ^=Sβ^.

Many operations that change the printed coefficient table leave the fit exactly unchanged:

  • Changing units of a regressor (metres to centimetres, dollars to thousands of dollars) rescales one column: W=XD with D diagonal. The coefficient is divided by the scale factor, and nothing else changes.

  • Centring regressors, xj↦xj−x¯j1, when the model has an intercept. The slopes are unchanged, and the intercept becomes the fitted value at the mean of the regressors instead of at zero, which is often far outside the data.

  • Recoding a factor. Reference-level dummies, sum-to-zero effect coding, and cell-means coding without an intercept all span the space of vectors that are constant within levels.

  • Replacing polynomial terms by orthogonal polynomials (Chapter 42, Theorem 42.2.2), which span the same space with much better numerical behaviour (Section 6.10).

Operations that do change the fit are exactly those that change C(X). Examples are dropping the intercept when no other combination of columns produces 1, replacing a regressor by its logarithm, and adding an interaction.

6.7.2. Centring as a projection

Suppose 1∈C(X). By Theorem 6.5.1 with X0=1, the fitted vector splits as My=y¯1+(M−n−111⊤)y, and the second projection is onto C(X)∩1⊥, the vectors in the model space with entries summing to zero. By Lemma 6.6.1 with X2=1, that subspace is spanned by the centred columns (I−n−111⊤)X. Applying Pythagoras to the orthogonal pieces Y^−y¯1 and 𝛆ε^ of y−y¯1 gives the corrected decomposition

∑i(yi−y¯)2⏟SST=∑i(y^i−y¯)2⏟SSR+∑i(yi−y^i)2⏟SSE,
(6.7.1)

with dimensions n−1=(r−1)+(n−r). Chapter 9 builds the analysis of variance on this identity.

6.7.3. R2 is a squared cosine

The coefficient of determination of a model with 1∈C(X) is R2=SSRSST=1−SSESST. It depends on the model only through C(X), so by Theorem 6.7.1 it is invariant under every reparameterization. Its geometric meaning is simple.

Theorem 6.7.2

Assume 1∈C(X) and y∉span(1). Let θ be the angle between the centred response y−y¯1 and the centred fitted vector Y^−y¯1. Then R2=cos2⁡θ=corr(y,Y^)2, where corr is the sample correlation. For simple regression on one regressor x, R2=corr(y,x)2.

Proof. Write u=y−y¯1 and v=Y^−y¯1. Then 𝛆u=v+ε^ with 𝛆v⊥ε^, so u⊤v=‖v‖2 and cos2⁡θ=(u⊤v)2‖u‖2‖v‖2=‖v‖2‖u‖2=SSRSST. (If v=0 the angle is undefined, but R2=0 and the correlation is taken to be zero.) Since Y^ has mean y¯ (Proposition 6.4.3(b)), cos⁡θ is the sample correlation of y and Y^. In simple regression v=(Sxy/Sxx)(x−x¯1) by Example (Intercept inside simple regression), a scalar multiple of the centred regressor. Rescaling a vector by a nonzero constant does not change a squared cosine, so cos2⁡θ is the squared correlation of y and x. When Sxy=0 both sides are zero.

So R2 measures how closely the model space lines up with the centred data. Figure 6.7.1 draws the case n=3. For the three-regressor model of Example (Poverty and murder rates), R2=0.6419, meaning the centred response makes an angle of 36.8∘ with the model space. The simple regression on poverty alone has R2=0.3599, the square of the correlation 0.5999.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

def r_squared_as_cosine(y, X):
    """X must contain the intercept column. Returns (R^2 from sums of squares, cos^2 of angle)."""
    beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
    y_hat = X @ beta
    yc, yhc = y - y.mean(), y_hat - y.mean()          # centre both vectors
    r2 = 1 - np.sum((y - y_hat) ** 2) / np.sum(yc ** 2)
    cos = (yc @ yhc) / (np.linalg.norm(yc) * np.linalg.norm(yhc))
    return r2, cos ** 2

Three consequences follow directly from the geometry.

  1. R2 never decreases when the model space grows. If C(X0)⊆C(X) and both contain 1, then SSE0−SSE=‖(M−M0)y‖2≥0 by Eq. 6.5.2. Adding a regressor of pure noise increases R2 almost surely. This is why R2 cannot be used for model selection (Chapter 29).

  2. R2=1 once the model space contains y, which always happens when rank(X)=n. A model with as many free parameters as observations interpolates, and its R2 says nothing.

  3. Without an intercept, the definition changes. If 1∉C(X), the corrected decomposition Eq. 6.7.1 fails, because Y^−y¯1 and 𝛆ε^ need not be orthogonal. Software then usually reports the uncentred R02=‖Y^‖2/‖y‖2, the squared cosine of the angle between y and C(X) itself. It is typically much larger and cannot be compared with the centred R2 of a model with an intercept (Exercise (B1)).

6.7.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

A factor with three levels is coded with an intercept and indicators for levels 2 and 3. Write down the matrices T and S of Theorem 6.7.1 that convert to the coding in which level 3, rather than level 1, is the omitted baseline, and to sum-to-zero effect coding. Express each new coefficient in terms of the old ones.

Exercise (A2)

Show that, for a model with 1∈C(X), R2 is unchanged when y is replaced by a1+by with b≠0, and when any column of X is replaced by an affine function of itself. Is it unchanged when a regressor is replaced by its logarithm?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that if 1∈C(X) then R02=‖Y^‖2/‖y‖2=(ny¯2+SSR)/(ny¯2+SST)≥R2. Construct a data set and a model without an intercept for which R02>0.95 but the model’s residual sum of squares exceeds that of the intercept-only model.

Solution. With 1∈C(X), the fitted vector splits orthogonally as y¯1+(Y^−y¯1), so ‖Y^‖2=ny¯2+SSR, and likewise ‖y‖2=ny¯2+SST. For a≥0 and 0≤SSR≤SST, (a+SSR)/(a+SST)≥SSR/SST. For the example, take responses near 100 with small noise and a regressor unrelated to them, and fit through the origin. The fitted line captures the large mean, so R02 is near 1, but its residuals exceed those around y¯.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Suppose Y∼Nn(μ1,σ2I) and 1∈C(X) with rank(X)=r. Using the results of Chapter 4, show that R2∼Beta((r−1)/2,(n−r)/2), so that E(R2)=(r−1)/(n−1) even though no regressor is related to the response. Use this to motivate the adjusted R2=1−(1−R2)(n−1)/(n−r).

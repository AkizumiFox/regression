6.8  Leverage as geometry

The projection M is an n×n matrix whose entries have a direct interpretation. Since y^i=∑jmijyj, the entry mij is the weight observation j receives in the fitted value for observation i. So mij=∂y^i∂yj. The diagonal entries measure how strongly each observation pulls the fitted surface towards itself.

Definition 6.8.1 (Leverage)

The leverage of observation i is hii=mii, the ith diagonal entry of M. In this context M is often called the hat matrix and written H, because it “puts the hat on” y.

Leverage is a property of the design, not of the response. It depends on y not at all, and on X only through C(X), so it is unchanged by reparameterization.

Proposition 6.8.1 (Properties of leverage)

Let rank(X)=r, and let ei denote the ith standard basis vector. Then:

  • (a) hii=‖Mei‖2=∑jmij2, the squared length of the projection of ei onto C(X);

  • (b) 0≤hii≤1, and ∑ihii=r, so the average leverage is r/n;

  • (c) if 1∈C(X), then hii≥1/n;

  • (d) hii=1 iff ei∈C(X). In that case y^i=yi for every y, the ith residual is always zero, and mij=0 for j≠i;

  • (e) if X=QR with Q having orthonormal columns spanning C(X), then hii is the squared length of the ith row of Q.

Proof.

  • (a) hii=ei⊤Mei=ei⊤M⊤Mei, and Mei is the ith column of M.
  • (b) By Proposition 6.3.4(d), 0≤‖Mei‖2≤‖ei‖2=1. The sum is tr(M)=r.
  • (c) With M0=n−111⊤, the difference M−M0 is a projection (Theorem 6.5.1), hence nonnegative definite, so hii≥(M0)ii=1/n.
  • (d) Equality holds in ‖Mei‖≤‖ei‖ iff (I−M)ei=0, that is, iff ei∈C(X). Then Mei=ei, so column i of M is ei, and by symmetry so is row i. Hence y^i=yi.
  • (e) M=QQ⊤, so mii=∑kqik2.

Part (d) describes the extreme case. An observation with leverage one has a coordinate direction of its own in the model space. This happens, for example, when it is the only member of a group in a one-way layout, or when a column of X is an indicator for that single observation. The model then fits the observation exactly, whatever its value, and the data give no check on it. High leverage is a weaker form of the same thing. If hii is close to one, then y^i≈yi, and a gross error in yi barely shows up in its residual.

This shows up in the residual variances. If 𝛆Cov(ε)=σ2I, then by Proposition 6.4.3(e) Var(ε^i)=σ2(1−hii), so residuals at high-leverage points are less variable than others, even though those are exactly the points where an error would do the most damage. Chapter 20 standardizes residuals to correct for this (Definition 20.1.1).

6.8.1. Leverage as distance from the centre

When the model has an intercept, leverage has a second description in variable space: distance from the centroid of the regressors. Write X=[1,X1], let x¯ be the vector of column means of X1, and let X~1=(I−n−111⊤)X1 be the centred regressors, assumed to have full column rank.

Proposition 6.8.2

Under these assumptions, hii=1n+(x(i)−x¯)⊤(X~1⊤X~1)−1(x(i)−x¯), where x(i) is the ith row of X1, written as a column vector.

Proof. By Lemma 6.6.1 with X2=1, M=n−111⊤+X~1(X~1⊤X~1)−1X~1⊤. The ith row of X~1 is (x(i)−x¯)⊤.

The quadratic form is (n−1)−1 times the squared Mahalanobis distance of x(i) from the centroid, measured in the metric of the sample covariance matrix of the regressors. Leverage is therefore high for points that are unusual jointly, even when no single coordinate is extreme. A state with moderate poverty and moderate single parenthood, but an unusual combination of the two, can have high leverage. For simple regression the formula reduces to hii=n−1+(xi−x¯)2/Sxx.

Example (High-leverage states)

Return to the state data, now with all 51 jurisdictions including the District of Columbia, and the model of Example (Poverty and murder rates). The average leverage is p/n=0.078, and the smallest is 0.021, just above the bound 1/n. The three largest are

Jurisdiction hii multiple of average
District of Columbia 0.506 6.5
Mississippi 0.212 2.7
Alaska 0.177 2.3

The District of Columbia is entirely urban, with by far the highest proportion of single-parent households. It sits at a corner of the regressor cloud that no state comes near, and its leverage is about half the maximum possible. It was omitted from Example (Poverty and murder rates). Exercise (A1) asks what happens to the poverty coefficient when it is put back.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data          # District of Columbia kept here
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(data)), data["poverty"], data["single"], data["urban"]])

Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(X)                 # orthonormal basis for C(X)
h = np.sum(Q ** 2, axis=1)             # h_ii = m_ii = ||row i of Q||^2
n, p = X.shape
print("sum of leverages:", h.sum(), " (= p =", p, ")")
print("range:", h.min(), h.max(), " bounds:", 1 / n, 1)
top = np.argsort(h)[::-1][:3]
for i in top:
    print(f"{data.index[i]:22s} h = {h[i]:.3f}   ({h[i] / (p / n):.1f} x average)")

The listing computes leverages from a QR factorization rather than by forming M. For large n the n×n matrix M should never be formed. Section 6.10 explains why.

6.8.2. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For simple regression, derive hii=n−1+(xi−x¯)2/Sxx directly from Example (Intercept inside simple regression), and show that the largest leverage belongs to the observation with xi farthest from x¯.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that mij2≤mii(1−mii) for i≠j, and hence |mij|≤1/2. When is the bound attained?

Solution. From Proposition 6.8.1(a), mii=∑kmik2≥mii2+mij2. So mij2≤mii(1−mii)≤1/4. Equality needs mii=1/2 and mij the only other nonzero entry of row i. The intercept-only model with n=2, where every entry of M is 1/2, attains it.

Exercise (B2)

Show that adding a column to X cannot decrease any leverage. Show that the leverages in a one-way layout are 1/nk for members of group k, and explain why an observation forming a group on its own has leverage one.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Proposition 6.8.1(d) says that hii=1 iff ei∈C(X). Here is what that means for the design. Write X(i) for X with row i deleted, and let D be the (n−1)×n matrix that deletes the ith coordinate of a vector, so that C(X(i))=DC(X) and N(D)=span(ei).

  • (a) Show that rank(X(i))=rank(X)−dim⁡(C(X)∩span(ei)), and deduce that hii=1 iff deleting case i lowers the rank of the design.

  • (b) Deduce that at most rank(X) cases can have leverage one, and that if k of them do, the remaining leverages sum to rank(X)−k.

  • (c) Show that DMD⊤ is the orthogonal projection onto C(X(i)) when hii=1, and conclude that deleting such a case changes none of the other fitted values. In what sense does the data set then contain no information about yi?

Solution.

  • (a) The map D restricted to C(X) is linear with image C(X(i)) and kernel C(X)∩N(D)=C(X)∩span(ei), so the rank–nullity theorem (Eq. 6.2.1) applied to that restriction gives the identity. The intersection is span(ei) if ei∈C(X) and {0} otherwise, so the rank falls by one exactly when ei∈C(X), that is, when hii=1.

  • (b) Leverages are nonnegative and sum to r=rank(X) by Proposition 6.8.1(b), so k entries equal to one force k≤r and leave r−k for the rest.

  • (c) Suppose hii=1, so Mei=ei and, by Proposition 6.8.1(d), row i and column i of M are ei⊤ and ei. The matrix DMD⊤ is symmetric, and since D⊤D=I−eiei⊤, (DMD⊤)2=DM(I−eiei⊤)MD⊤=D(M−eiei⊤)D⊤=DMD⊤, because Dei=0. Its column space is DC(M)=C(X(i)), so it is the projection of Theorem 6.3.3. The reduced fit is therefore DMD⊤y(i)=DM(I−eiei⊤)y=DMy−yiDei=DMy, whose jth entry is y^j: the other fitted values do not move, and they never involved yi in the first place, since mji=0. The response yi influences no fitted value but its own, which it reproduces exactly: the model has a free coordinate reserved for case i, and the data can neither check it nor borrow strength for it.

Exercise (C2)

Replication bounds leverage. Suppose m cases share the same row of X, say x(i)=x(j) for all i,j∈D with |D|=m.

  • (a) Show that rows i and j of M are equal for i,j∈D, and hence that mij=hii=hjj for all i,j∈D.

  • (b) Deduce hii≤1/m, with equality iff mil=0 for every l∉D. Check the bound against the one-way layout of Exercise (B2).

  • (c) A design point of high leverage is to be measured again. Explain what (b) says about repeating it m times, and why this does not contradict Exercise (B2).

Solution.

  • (a) By Theorem 6.4.1, 𝛃My=Xβ^ for any least squares estimate, so the ith fitted value is 𝛃x(i)⊤β^. If x(i)=x(j), then y^i=y^j for every y, which forces rows i and j of M to be equal. Taking the kth entry with k∈D and using symmetry, mik=mkk=hkk, and also mik=mki=hii, so all these numbers coincide.

  • (b) By Proposition 6.8.1(a), hii=∑lmil2≥∑l∈Dmil2=mhii2, and hii>0 unless the row is 0; dividing gives hii≤1/m. Equality needs the discarded terms to vanish, that is, mil=0 for l∉D. In a one-way layout the m members of a group are exactly such a set, and the projection averages within groups, so mil=0 outside the group and the bound is attained: hii=1/m.

  • (c) Replication is the one design change that caps leverage: with m copies of a point, none of them can have leverage above 1/m, whatever the rest of the design, so an isolated design point stops being able to dictate its own fitted value. There is no conflict with Exercise (B2), which adds a column to a fixed set of cases; here we add cases, and the total ∑ihii=rank(X) is redistributed over more of them.

Exercise (C3)

Let X have full column rank p, write A=X⊤X, and append one more case with row x⊤, giving X+ with X+⊤X+=A+xx⊤. Put d=x⊤A−1x.

  • (a) Using Theorem 1.6.5, show that the new case has leverage d/(1+d), which is always less than one.

  • (b) Show that no old leverage increases: the new leverage of case i is hii−(x(i)⊤A−1x)2/(1+d). When is it unchanged?

  • (c) Reconcile (b) with ∑ihii=p in both designs.

Solution.

  • (a) By Eq. 1.6.7, (A+xx⊤)−1=A−1−A−1xx⊤A−1/(1+d), and 1+d>0 because A−1 is positive definite. So the new case has leverage x⊤(A+xx⊤)−1x=d−d2/(1+d)=d/(1+d)<1.

  • (b) Applying the same formula to x(i) gives hii+=hii−(x(i)⊤A−1x)2/(1+d)≤hii, with equality iff x(i)⊤A−1x=0, that is, iff the two rows are orthogonal in the metric of A−1.

  • (c) The old leverages fall by ∑i(x(i)⊤A−1x)2/(1+d)=x⊤A−1AA−1x/(1+d)=d/(1+d), which is exactly the leverage of the new case. The total stays at p: one more case shares the same p units of leverage.

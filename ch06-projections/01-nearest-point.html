6.1  Least squares as a nearest-point problem

Chapter 5 introduced least squares as a calculus problem: write down the sum of squared errors as a function of the coefficients, differentiate, set the derivative to zero. That route gets the right answer, but it hides the structure of the answer. It treats the coefficient vector 𝛃β as the basic unknown. It needs X⊤X to be invertible before it can say anything. And it gives no hint of why some quantities, such as fitted values, residuals and sums of squares, behave well in every model, while others, such as individual coefficients, can be fragile or not defined at all.

This chapter starts from a different place. Instead of the p-dimensional space where 𝛃β lives, it works in the n-dimensional space where the data live.

6.1.1. Two pictures of the same data

A regression data set with n observations and a single regressor is usually drawn as a scatterplot: n points in the plane, one for each observation, with the regressor on one axis and the response on the other. Call this the variable space picture. It is the right picture for seeing the shape of a relationship, but it scales badly. With k regressors it needs k+1 dimensions, and it has no natural place for the model’s coefficients.

The other picture turns this around. Stack the n responses into a single vector y=(y1,…,yn)⊤, and regard it as one point in Rn. Each column of the model matrix X is also a vector in Rn: the intercept column is 1, and the column for a regressor holds its n observed values. This is the observation space picture. Its dimension is the sample size, which is usually far more than three. But it contains only p+1 vectors of interest, and those always span a subspace of dimension at most p+1. Most of the geometry happens in a subspace small enough to draw.

In observation space, the linear model 𝛃𝛃E(Y)=Xβ,β∈Rp, says one thing: the mean vector E(Y) lies in the set C(X)={Xb:b∈Rp}, the column space of X. That set is a subspace of Rn. The coefficients 𝛃β are the coordinates of E(Y) with respect to the columns of X, but the claim itself is only about where E(Y) is. It does not depend on which spanning vectors are used to describe the subspace.

6.1.2. The least squares principle, restated

The observed y is almost never in C(X), because noise moves it off the subspace. A natural estimate of E(Y) is the point of C(X) that is closest to y. With Euclidean distance, this is exactly least squares: ‖y−Xb‖2=∑i=1n(yi−x(i)⊤b)2, where x(i)⊤ is the ith row of X. Minimizing over b is the same as searching C(X) for the point nearest to y.

Definition 6.1.1 (Least squares)

A vector 𝛃β^∈Rp is a least squares estimate of 𝛃β if 𝛃‖y−Xβ^‖≤‖y−Xb‖for every b∈Rp. The vector 𝛃Y^=Xβ^ is the fitted value vector, and 𝛆ε^=y−Y^ is the residual vector.

The definition does not assume that the minimizer is unique, and deliberately so. In Section 6.4 we will see that the nearest point Y^ is always unique. The coefficient vector 𝛃β^ that produces it is unique only when the columns of X are linearly independent.

6.1.3. A picture with three observations

The smallest case that still shows all the geometry has n=3 and p=2. Then C(X) is a plane through the origin in R3, and y is a point somewhere off the plane.

Example (Three observations)

Take responses y=(1,4,2)⊤ at regressor values x=(0,1,3)⊤, with an intercept, so that X=[1,x]. The least squares fit (computed by the listing below) is 𝛆Y^=(2.143, 2.286, 2.571)⊤,ε^=(−1.143, 1.714, −0.571)⊤, with intercept 2.143 and slope 0.143. Two facts stand out. First, the residual is orthogonal to both columns of X: the entries of 𝛆ε^ sum to zero, and 𝛆x⊤ε^=0. Second, the squared lengths add: 𝛆‖y‖2=21.000=16.429+4.571=‖Y^‖2+‖ε^‖2. Figure 6.1.1 shows the configuration. The fitted vector is the foot of the perpendicular dropped from y to the plane.

import numpy as np

y = np.array([1.0, 4.0, 2.0])
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(3), [0.0, 1.0, 3.0]])   # intercept and one regressor

beta_hat, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
y_hat = X @ beta_hat              # the point of C(X) nearest to y
e_hat = y - y_hat                 # the residual vector

print("fitted  ", y_hat)
print("residual", e_hat)
print("X^T e   ", X.T @ e_hat)    # zero: the residual is orthogonal to C(X)

The two facts in Example (Three observations) are not coincidences of this data set. They are the whole theory in miniature. The residual is orthogonal to the subspace because Y^ is the nearest point, and the squared lengths add because the two pieces are orthogonal. The rest of the chapter makes these two statements precise and follows their consequences.

Idea. Fitted values are determined by the subspace C(X). Coefficients are determined by the basis used to describe it. Anything that depends only on C(X) is unchanged by reparameterization, by rescaling or recoding regressors, and by rank deficiency. This covers fitted values, residuals, sums of squares, R2 and leverages.

6.1.4. Plan of the chapter

Section 6.2 collects the facts about subspaces that the geometry needs. Section 6.3 proves that nearest points exist and are unique, and identifies the matrices that compute them: the symmetric idempotent matrices, including X(X⊤X)−X⊤ for every generalized inverse. Section 6.4 applies this to least squares. Section 6.5 studies nested subspaces, which is where sums of squares come from. Section 6.6 proves the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem, which says what a single coefficient in a multiple regression measures. Section 6.7 covers reparameterization and R2, and Section 6.8 covers leverage. Section 6.9 replaces the Euclidean inner product with a general one, the step that leads to generalized least squares. Section 6.10 shows how the projection should be computed in floating point.

6.1.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Redo Example (Three observations) in exact arithmetic. From the simple regression formulas of Theorem 5.2.1, show that the intercept is 15/7 and the slope 1/7, so that Y^=(15,16,18)⊤/7 and 𝛆ε^=(−8,12,−4)⊤/7. Check exactly that 𝛆1⊤ε^=0, 𝛆x⊤ε^=0 and 𝛆‖Y^‖2+‖ε^‖2=‖y‖2=21.

Solution. Here ∑xi=4, ∑xi2=10, ∑yi=7 and ∑xiyi=10, so Sxx=10−42/3=14/3 and Sxy=10−4⋅7/3=2/3. The slope is Sxy/Sxx=1/7 and the intercept y¯−x¯/7=7/3−4/21=15/7. Evaluating 15/7+xi/7 at x=0,1,3 gives Y^, and 𝛆ε^=y−Y^ as stated. Then 𝛆1⊤ε^=(−8+12−4)/7=0 and 𝛆x⊤ε^=(0⋅(−8)+1⋅12+3⋅(−4))/7=0. Finally ‖Y^‖2=(225+256+324)/49=805/49 and 𝛆‖ε^‖2=(64+144+16)/49=224/49, which sum to 1029/49=21=1+16+4.

Exercise (A2)

Let y=(1,3)⊤ and let X be the 2×2 matrix whose two columns both equal 1. Show that ‖y−Xb‖2 depends on b only through b1+b2, and that the least squares estimates of Definition 6.1.1 are exactly the b with b1+b2=2. Which of 𝛃β^, Y^, 𝛆ε^ does the definition determine uniquely here?

Solution. Xb=(b1+b2)1, so with t=b1+b2 the criterion is (1−t)2+(3−t)2=2(t−2)2+2, minimized exactly at t=2. The estimates therefore form the line {b:b1+b2=2}, while Y^=(2,2)⊤ and 𝛆ε^=(−1,1)⊤ are the same for every one of them. The fitted vector and the residual are determined; the coefficient vector is not.

Exercise (A3)

Let Y^ be a nearest point to y in C(X). Show directly from Definition 6.1.1, with no formula for Y^, that cY^ is a nearest point to cy for every c∈R, and that Y^+u is a nearest point to y+u for every u∈C(X). Deduce that adding u leaves the residual unchanged.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

The nearest point is unique, and this can be proved before any of the machinery of Section 6.3. Let u,v∈C(X) both attain d=minb‖y−Xb‖. Expand inner products, or use the parallelogram law of Exercise (A2), to verify ‖y−u+v2‖2=12‖y−u‖2+12‖y−v‖2−14‖u−v‖2, and conclude that u=v. Where does the argument use that C(X) contains the midpoint of any two of its elements?

Solution. Write y−(u+v)/2=12(y−u)+12(y−v) and expand: ‖y−u+v2‖2=14‖y−u‖2+14‖y−v‖2+12(y−u)⊤(y−v). Also ‖u−v‖2=‖(y−v)−(y−u)‖2=‖y−u‖2+‖y−v‖2−2(y−u)⊤(y−v). Eliminating the cross term between the two displays gives the identity. Now ‖y−u‖=‖y−v‖=d, so the left side is d2−14‖u−v‖2. The midpoint lies in C(X), so the left side is at least d2 by the definition of d; hence ‖u−v‖=0. Without the midpoint the inequality ‖y−(u+v)/2‖≥d would not be available.

Exercise (B2)

Adding a constant to every response is the commonest change of units, and only a model with an intercept handles it in the obvious way.

  • (a) Suppose 1∈C(X). Using Exercise (A3), show that the fit for y+c1 is Y^+c1 and that the residual is unchanged.

  • (b) Take n=2, X=x=(1,2)⊤ and no intercept. Show that the fit for y=(1,1)⊤ is (3/5,6/5)⊤, and that the fit for y+1=(2,2)⊤ is (6/5,12/5)⊤, not (8/5,11/5)⊤. Which residual changes, and why is there no contradiction with (a)?

Solution.

  • (a) When 1∈C(X), the map v↦v+c1 is a bijection of C(X) onto itself, and ‖(y+c1)−(v+c1)‖=‖y−v‖, so the distances to be minimized match up in pairs. The nearest point to y+c1 is therefore Y^+c1, and subtracting leaves 𝛆ε^ unchanged. (This is Exercise (A3) with u=c1.)

  • (b) Minimizing (1−b)2+(1−2b)2 gives 10b=6, b=3/5, and bx=(3/5,6/5)⊤. For y=(2,2)⊤, 10b=12, b=6/5, and bx=(6/5,12/5)⊤. The residual changes from (2/5,−1/5)⊤ to (4/5,−2/5)⊤. There is no contradiction: 1∉C(x), so y+1 is not y plus an element of the model space, and (a) does not apply. The intercept column is what makes a shift of the response a movement inside the model space.

Exercise (B3)

Let C(X1)⊆C(X2), and let dk=min{‖y−v‖:v∈C(Xk)}.

  • (a) Show d2≤d1, with equality iff some nearest point of y in C(X1) is also a nearest point in C(X2). Conclude that appending a column to X can never raise the residual sum of squares.

  • (b) Equality does not require the new column to be redundant. With n=3, X=1 and y=(1,2,3)⊤, the fit is Y^=(2,2,2)⊤ and 𝛆ε^=(−1,0,1)⊤. Show that appending z=(1,−2,1)⊤ enlarges the column space but leaves Y^ and d unchanged.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Definition 6.1.1 takes for granted that a minimizer exists. Prove it, without orthonormal bases.

  • (a) Reduce to the case in which the columns of X are linearly independent.

  • (b) Show that c=min{‖Xb‖:‖b‖=1} is positive, so ‖Xb‖≥c‖b‖ for every b.

  • (c) Deduce that the infimum of ‖y−Xb‖ over Rp equals its infimum over a closed ball, and that it is attained.

  • (d) Show that the conclusion can fail for sets that are not subspaces: give a nonempty S⊆R2 and a y with no nearest point in S.

Solution.

  • (a) Discard columns that are linear combinations of the others until the rest are independent. This changes neither C(X) nor the set of attainable values Xb, so it changes neither the infimum nor whether it is attained.

  • (b) The function b↦‖Xb‖ is continuous and the unit sphere of Rp is closed and bounded, so the minimum c is attained at some b0 with ‖b0‖=1. If c=0 then Xb0=0 with b0≠0, contradicting independence. Homogeneity gives ‖Xb‖≥c‖b‖ for all b.

  • (c) At b=0 the criterion is ‖y‖. If ‖b‖>2‖y‖/c, then ‖y−Xb‖≥‖Xb‖−‖y‖≥c‖b‖−‖y‖>‖y‖. So the infimum over Rp equals the infimum over the closed ball ‖b‖≤2‖y‖/c, which is closed and bounded; a continuous function attains its minimum there.

  • (d) Let S={v:‖v‖<1} and y=(2,0)⊤. Distances from y to S have infimum 1, approached along v=(t,0)⊤ as t↑1, but no point of S is at distance 1. What fails is that S is not closed; it is both convex and bounded.

Exercise (C2)

Write N(X)={d∈Rp:Xd=0}, and let 𝛃β^0 be any least squares estimate.

  • (a) Using Exercise (B1), show that the set of least squares estimates is exactly 𝛃β^0+N(X).

  • (b) Deduce that the estimate is unique iff X has full column rank, and that otherwise the solution set is a translate of a subspace of dimension p−rank(X) (Theorem 1.2.2).

  • (c) Show that for c∈Rp the number 𝛃c⊤β^ is the same for every least squares estimate iff c⊤d=0 for every d∈N(X).

  • (d) Which c qualify in Exercise (A2)?

Chapter 8 takes the condition in (c) as the definition of an estimable function.

Solution.

  • (a) By Exercise (B1) there is a single vector Y^ attaining the minimum, so b is a least squares estimate iff 𝛃Xb=Y^=Xβ^0, that is, iff 𝛃X(b−β^0)=0.

  • (b) The set is a single point iff N(X)={0}, which is full column rank; its dimension is p−rank(X) by the rank–nullity theorem.

  • (c) Every estimate is 𝛃β^0+d, so the values taken by 𝛃c⊤β^ are 𝛃c⊤β^0+c⊤d as d ranges over N(X). These agree for all d iff c⊤d=0 throughout N(X); if c⊤d≠0 for some d, the two estimates 𝛃β^0 and 𝛃β^0+d give different values.

  • (d) There N(X) is spanned by (1,−1)⊤, so the condition is c1=c2: the sum β1+β2 is determined by the data, and no other linear function is, apart from its multiples.

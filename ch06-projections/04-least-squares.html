6.4  Least squares via projection

We now return to the linear model 𝛃𝛆Y=Xβ+ε, with X an n×p matrix of rank r≤p, and M the orthogonal projection onto C(X). Nothing in this section uses probability. The results hold for every data vector y and so apply whatever the distribution of the errors.

6.4.1. The characterization of least squares estimates

Theorem 6.4.1 (Least squares is projection)

A vector 𝛃β^ is a least squares estimate of 𝛃β iff 𝛃Xβ^=My. Consequently the fitted values Y^=My and residuals 𝛆ε^=(I−M)y are the same for every least squares estimate.

Proof. Apply the projection theorem (Theorem 6.3.1) with S=C(X) and u=Xb. By Eq. 6.3.1, ‖y−Xb‖2=‖y−My‖2+‖My−Xb‖2for every b∈Rp. The first term does not involve b, and the second is zero iff Xb=My. Such a b exists because My∈C(X).

The orthogonality half of the projection theorem turns this into linear equations. The residual y−Xb is orthogonal to C(X) iff it is orthogonal to every column of X, that is, iff X⊤(y−Xb)=0.

Theorem 6.4.2 (Normal equations)

The normal equations

X⊤Xb=X⊤y
(6.4.1)

are consistent for every y, and their solutions are exactly the least squares estimates. One solution is 𝛃β^=(X⊤X)−X⊤y, for any generalized inverse, and the set of all solutions is 𝛃𝛃β^+N(X)={β^+v:Xv=0}. In particular, the least squares estimate is unique iff rank(X)=p, and then 𝛃β^=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y.

Proof. A vector b solves Eq. 6.4.1 iff y−Xb⊥C(X). Since Xb∈C(X) automatically, this holds iff y=Xb+(y−Xb) is the orthogonal decomposition of y, that is, iff Xb=My. By Theorem 6.4.1 this is the same as b being a least squares estimate. For the particular solution, X(X⊤X)−X⊤y=My by Theorem 6.3.7. Finally, 𝛃Xb=My=Xβ^ iff 𝛃X(b−β^)=0, and N(X)={0} iff rank(X)=p by Eq. 6.2.1.

The logic of this proof runs opposite to the usual calculus derivation, and the difference matters. Setting a gradient to zero finds stationary points. One then has to argue separately that they are minima and that one exists. Here existence, minimality and the full solution set all come from the decomposition Rn=C(X)⊕C(X)⊥. The consistency of the normal equations, which can look surprising when X⊤X is singular, is just the statement X⊤y∈C(X⊤)=C(X⊤X) from Corollary 6.2.4.

6.4.2. Fitted values and residuals

Collecting the consequences for Y^=My and 𝛆ε^=(I−M)y:

Proposition 6.4.3

For any data vector y:

  • (a) 𝛆X⊤ε^=0 and 𝛆Y^⊤ε^=0;

  • (b) if 1∈C(X) (for example, the model has an intercept), then ∑iε^i=0 and the mean of the fitted values is y¯;

  • (c) 𝛆‖y‖2=‖Y^‖2+‖ε^‖2;

  • (d) 𝛆‖ε^‖2=y⊤(I−M)y and ‖Y^‖2=y⊤My;

  • (e) if 𝛃𝛆y=Xβ+ε for some 𝛃β, then 𝛃𝛆Y^=Xβ+Mε and 𝛆𝛆ε^=(I−M)ε.

Proof.

  • (a) is the orthogonality of 𝛆ε^ to C(X), and Y^∈C(X). For (b), 1∈C(X) gives 𝛆1⊤ε^=0, and then 1⊤Y^=1⊤y. (c) is Pythagoras. (d) uses symmetry and idempotence: ‖(I−M)y‖2=y⊤(I−M)⊤(I−M)y=y⊤(I−M)y. For (e), MX=X and (I−M)X=0.

Part (e) is simple but important. The residual vector does not depend on 𝛃β at all. It is the projection of the unobservable error vector onto the (n−r)-dimensional space C(X)⊥. Everything about σ2 that the data can reveal must therefore come from 𝛆(I−M)ε. If 𝛆E(ε)=0 and 𝛆Cov(ε)=σ2I, the expectation of a quadratic form (Chapter 2) gives 𝛆𝛆𝛆E‖ε^‖2=E[ε⊤(I−M)ε]=σ2tr(I−M)=σ2(n−r), using Proposition 6.3.4. So 𝛆‖ε^‖2/(n−r) is unbiased for σ2. The divisor is the dimension of the space in which the residuals live, not the number of observations and not n−p. Chapter 7 develops the sampling theory, and Chapter 9 develops the decomposition.

6.4.3. Coefficients are not unique; fitted values are

When rank(X)<p, Theorem 6.4.2 gives a whole affine subspace of least squares estimates, one for each generalized inverse and more besides. Statistical software has to pick one. Some programs drop columns that are linearly dependent on earlier ones. Others impose “side conditions” such as a zero coefficient for a reference level or effects that sum to zero. Others report the minimum-norm solution. These choices look very different in the printed coefficient table. The geometry says they are all describing the same fitted vector.

Example (Four answers, one fit)

Nine observations fall in three groups of sizes 3,2,4 with group means 5.700, 7.800 and 4.300. The model matrix X=[1,z1,z2,z3] has p=4 columns and rank 3, so X⊤X is singular. We compute 𝛃β^=GX⊤y for four generalized inverses G of X⊤X: the Moore–Penrose inverse; the inverse obtained by deleting the intercept row and column (Proposition 6.3.5); the one obtained by deleting the first group’s row and column; and a deliberately strange, non-symmetric one built from the general formula of Exercise (B2).

Generalized inverse β^0 β^1 β^2 β^3 β^1−β^2 max|M−MMP|
Moore–Penrose 4.450 1.250 3.350 -0.150 -2.100 0
drop intercept 0.000 5.700 7.800 4.300 -2.100 1.1×10−16
drop group 1 5.700 0.000 2.100 -1.400 -2.100 1.1×10−16
random -9.102 14.802 16.902 13.402 -2.100 1.8×10−15

The coefficient vectors disagree wildly, including in sign. Yet each produces the same projection matrix up to rounding error, and hence the same fitted values (the group means) and residuals. The difference β^1−β^2 is the same in every row, and so is β^0+β^k for each k. Individual coefficients are not. The trace of M is 3.000, the rank of X.

import numpy as np

groups = np.array([0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 2])     # three groups, sizes 3, 2, 4
y = np.array([5.1, 6.3, 5.7, 8.2, 7.4, 4.0, 4.9, 3.8, 4.5])
n, g = len(y), 3
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), np.eye(g)[groups]])  # [1, indicator columns]
A = X.T @ X
print("n =", n, " p =", X.shape[1], " rank(X) =", np.linalg.matrix_rank(X))
def g_drop(j):
    """Generalized inverse obtained by deleting row and column j of A."""
    keep = [k for k in range(A.shape[0]) if k != j]
    G = np.zeros_like(A)
    G[np.ix_(keep, keep)] = np.linalg.inv(A[np.ix_(keep, keep)])
    return G

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
G0 = np.linalg.pinv(A)                             # Moore-Penrose inverse
U, V = rng.normal(size=A.shape), rng.normal(size=A.shape)
G_random = G0 + (np.eye(4) - G0 @ A) @ U + V @ (np.eye(4) - A @ G0)

ginverses = {
    "Moore-Penrose": G0,
    "drop intercept": g_drop(0),
    "drop group 1": g_drop(1),
    "random": G_random,
}
M_ref = X @ G0 @ X.T
for name, G in ginverses.items():
    assert np.allclose(A @ G @ A, A)               # G really is a g-inverse
    beta = G @ X.T @ y
    M = X @ G @ X.T
    print(f"{name:15s} beta = {np.round(beta, 3)}",
          f" max|M - M_ref| = {np.abs(M - M_ref).max():.1e}",
          f" group1-group2 = {beta[1] - beta[2]:.3f}")

Which linear functions of 𝛃β^ come out the same for every least squares estimate? The answer is a first look at estimability, the subject of Chapter 8.

Theorem 6.4.4

For 𝛌λ∈Rp, the value 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ is the same for every least squares estimate 𝛃β^ (and every y) iff 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤), that is, iff 𝛌𝛒λ⊤=ρ⊤X for some 𝛒ρ∈Rn. In that case 𝛌𝛃𝛒λ⊤β^=ρ⊤My.

Proof. By Theorem 6.4.2 the least squares estimates form 𝛃β^+N(X). So 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ is invariant iff 𝛌λ⊤v=0 for all v∈N(X), that is, iff 𝛌λ∈N(X)⊥=C(X⊤) (Corollary 6.2.4). If 𝛌𝛒λ⊤=ρ⊤X then 𝛌𝛃𝛒𝛃𝛒λ⊤β^=ρ⊤Xβ^=ρ⊤My.

In Example (Four answers, one fit), 𝛌λ=(0,1,−1,0)⊤ is the difference between any row of X from group 1, (1,1,0,0), and any row from group 2, (1,0,1,0). So 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤) and β^1−β^2 is invariant. For 𝛌λ=(0,1,0,0)⊤, note that 𝛒ρ⊤X has first entry ∑iρi and remaining entries equal to the sums of ρi within each group. Matching (0,1,0,0) would need the group sums to be 1,0,0 and their total to be 0, which is impossible. So β^1 is not invariant.

Remark (Side conditions choose coordinates). Imposing β0=0 or β1=0 does not add information about the data. It picks one point from the affine set 𝛃β^+N(X). A side condition 𝛃c⊤β=0 picks exactly one point iff c∉C(X⊤). A condition inside C(X⊤) would constrain the fitted values and change the model rather than just its coordinates. Theorem 8.4.2 makes this precise.

6.4.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For Example (Three observations), write out the 3×3 matrix M with exact fractional entries, verify trM=2, and recover the fitted values and residuals quoted in the text.

Exercise (A2)

Show that the residuals sum to zero whenever 1∈C(X), even if no column of X is constant. Give an example of a model without such a column in which this happens, and one in which the residuals do not sum to zero.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Suppose 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ and Cov(Y)=σ2I. Show that 𝛃E(Y^)=Xβ, Cov(Y^)=σ2M, 𝛆Cov(ε^)=σ2(I−M) and 𝛆Cov(Y^,ε^)=0. Which of these statements survive when Cov(Y)=σ2V for a general positive definite V?

Exercise (B2)

Let rank(X)=r<p and let C be a (p−r)×p matrix. Show that the constrained problem “minimize ‖y−Xb‖ subject to Cb=0” has a unique solution for every y, and that its fitted values are My, iff rank(XC)=p. Show that the condition implies C(C⊤)∩C(X⊤)={0}, and interpret: the constraints must not restrict any estimable function.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Suppose X⊤y≠0. Show that every least squares estimate b can be written as GX⊤y for some generalized inverse G of A=X⊤X. Hint: with c=X⊤y, try G=G0+(b−G0c)c⊤/(c⊤c). What goes wrong when X⊤y=0?

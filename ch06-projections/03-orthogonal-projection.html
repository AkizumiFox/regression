6.3  Orthogonal projection

6.3.1. Nearest points

Theorem 6.3.1 (Projection theorem)

Let S be a subspace of Rn and y∈Rn. There is exactly one y^∈S such that ‖y−y^‖≤‖y−u‖for all u∈S. It is the unique y^∈S with y−y^⊥S. Moreover, for every u∈S,

‖y−u‖2=‖y−y^‖2+‖y^−u‖2.
(6.3.1)

Proof. By Theorem 6.2.3 there is a unique decomposition y=y^+v with y^∈S and v∈S⊥. For u∈S, y−u=v+(y^−u), where v⊥S and y^−u∈S. The Pythagorean identity Eq. 6.2.3 then gives Eq. 6.3.1. The right-hand side is at least ‖y−y^‖2, with equality iff u=y^. So y^ is the unique nearest point. If some other w∈S also had y−w⊥S, then y=w+(y−w) would be a second decomposition, contradicting uniqueness.

The theorem has two halves, and it helps to keep them apart. The variational half says y^ minimizes a distance. The orthogonality half says the error y−y^ is perpendicular to the subspace. The first half explains why least squares is a sensible estimation principle. The second is what we compute with, because orthogonality is a set of linear equations.

6.3.2. The projection as a matrix

Write PSy for the nearest point y^. The map y↦PSy is linear: if yk=y^k+vk are the decompositions of y1 and y2, then ay1+by2=(ay^1+by^2)+(av1+bv2) is a decomposition of the same kind, and by uniqueness it is the decomposition. A linear map on Rn is given by a unique n×n matrix, since two matrices that agree on every vector agree on the standard basis vectors and are therefore equal.

Definition 6.3.1 (Orthogonal projection matrix)

The orthogonal projection onto a subspace S is the unique n×n matrix PS such that, for every y, PSy∈S and y−PSy⊥S. When S=C(X) for the model matrix of a linear model, we write M=PC(X).

The most useful formula for PS uses an orthonormal basis.

Proposition 6.3.2

Let the columns of the n×r matrix Q be an orthonormal basis for S, so that Q⊤Q=Ir. Then PS=QQ⊤=∑i=1ruiui⊤, where u1,…,ur are the columns of Q.

Proof. For any y, QQ⊤y=Q(Q⊤y)∈C(Q)=S, and Q⊤(y−QQ⊤y)=Q⊤y−Q⊤y=0, so the difference is orthogonal to every column of Q and hence to S. By uniqueness, QQ⊤=PS.

Example (Projections onto a line)

For S=span(v) with v≠0, the single vector v/‖v‖ is an orthonormal basis, so PS=vv⊤v⊤v,PSy=v⊤yv⊤vv. With v=1 this gives P=n−111⊤, the matrix with every entry 1/n, and Py=y¯1. The sample mean is a projection: fitting the intercept-only model replaces every observation by y¯, and the residuals yi−y¯ form a vector in 1⊥, the set of vectors whose entries sum to zero.

6.3.3. Characterizing projection matrices

Which matrices are orthogonal projections? Proposition 6.3.2 shows that every projection has the form QQ⊤, so it is symmetric, and QQ⊤QQ⊤=QIQ⊤=QQ⊤, so it is idempotent. The converse is the basic recognition theorem.

Theorem 6.3.3

An n×n matrix P is the orthogonal projection onto some subspace iff P⊤=P and P2=P. In that case the subspace is C(P), and I−P is the orthogonal projection onto C(P)⊥.

Proof. Necessity was shown above. For sufficiency, let P be symmetric and idempotent and let y∈Rn. Certainly Py∈C(P). For any u=Pb∈C(P), u⊤(y−Py)=b⊤P⊤(I−P)y=b⊤(P−P2)y=0, so y−Py⊥C(P) and P=PC(P). For the last claim, I−P is symmetric and idempotent, since (I−P)2=I−2P+P2=I−P. It sends y to y−Py∈C(P)⊥, and y−(I−P)y=Py⊥C(P)⊥.

Before this theorem, checking that a matrix computes a projection meant thinking about subspaces. Afterwards it is two matrix identities. Also, a projection is determined by its range: if P1,P2 are symmetric idempotent with C(P1)=C(P2), then P1=P2, because both are the projection onto the same subspace.

Proposition 6.3.4 (Spectrum, rank and trace)

Let P be the orthogonal projection onto an r-dimensional subspace S. Then:

  • (a) every eigenvalue of P is 0 or 1, and 1 has multiplicity r;

  • (b) rank(P)=tr(P)=r;

  • (c) Pv=v iff v∈S, and Pv=0 iff v∈S⊥;

  • (d) v⊤Pv=‖Pv‖2≤‖v‖2, so P and I−P are nonnegative definite.

Proof. Write P=QQ⊤ as in Proposition 6.3.2, and extend Q to an orthogonal matrix U=[Q,Q⊥]. Then P=Udiag(Ir,0)U⊤ is a spectral decomposition, which gives (a). Rank and trace of a symmetric matrix are the number of nonzero eigenvalues and their sum, which gives (b). Directly, tr(QQ⊤)=tr(Q⊤Q)=tr(Ir)=r. Part (c) restates the projection theorem: the nearest point of S to a vector already in S is the vector itself, and v⊥S iff 0 is its nearest point. For (d), v⊤Pv=v⊤P⊤Pv, and ‖v‖2=‖Pv‖2+‖(I−P)v‖2 by Pythagoras.

The identity rank=tr gets a lot of use. The dimension of a subspace, which is the “degrees of freedom” of every sum of squares in this book, can be read off as a sum of diagonal entries without finding a basis.

6.3.4. Generalized inverses

Proposition 6.3.2 needs an orthonormal basis. In statistics the subspace usually comes as the column space of a model matrix X whose columns are not orthonormal and may not even be independent. To write M directly in terms of X we need a substitute for (X⊤X)−1 that exists even when X⊤X is singular.

Definition 6.3.2 (Generalized inverse)

A generalized inverse of an m×k matrix A is any k×m matrix A− such that AA−A=A.

If A is square and nonsingular, multiplying AA−A=A on both sides by A−1 gives A−=A−1, so the generalized inverse is unique and equal to the inverse. When A is singular there are infinitely many.

Proposition 6.3.5 (Existence)

Every matrix has a generalized inverse. If A has rank r and is permuted so that A=(A11A12A21A22) with A11 an r×r nonsingular block, then A−=(A11−1000) (permuted back) is a generalized inverse.

Proof. A rank-r matrix has r independent rows and r independent columns, and the submatrix where they cross is nonsingular (Chapter 1). This guarantees the block form. Since the first r columns are a basis for the column space, (A12A22)=(A11A21)B for some B, so B=A11−1A12 and A22=A21A11−1A12. Multiplying out, AA−A=(I0A21A11−10)A=(A11A12A21A21A11−1A12)=A.\qedhere

Among the many generalized inverses, one is singled out by extra symmetry conditions. The Moore–Penrose inverse A+ is the unique matrix satisfying AA+A=A, A+AA+=A+, and both AA+ and A+A symmetric (Penrose 1955). Section 6.10 computes it from the singular value decomposition. For projections we will need none of these extra conditions, and that is the point of the next result.

Lemma 6.3.6

Let X be n×p and let G be any generalized inverse of X⊤X. Then:

  • (a) XGX⊤X=X and X⊤XGX⊤=X⊤;

  • (b) XGX⊤ does not depend on the choice of generalized inverse;

  • (c) XGX⊤ is symmetric, even when G is not.

Proof. By Corollary 6.2.4(b), C(X⊤)=C(X⊤X), so there is a p×n matrix B with X⊤=X⊤XB. Transposing, X=B⊤X⊤X. Then XGX⊤X=B⊤(X⊤X)G(X⊤X)=B⊤X⊤X=X, which is the first identity in (a). The second follows the same way, using X⊤=X⊤XB on the right. For (b) and (c), XGX⊤=B⊤(X⊤X)G(X⊤X)B=B⊤X⊤XB, which involves no G at all, and (B⊤X⊤XB)⊤=B⊤X⊤XB.

Theorem 6.3.7

For any n×p matrix X and any generalized inverse (X⊤X)−, M=X(X⊤X)−X⊤ is the orthogonal projection onto C(X). In particular rank(M)=tr(M)=rank(X). If X has full column rank, M=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤.

Proof. By Lemma 6.3.6, M is symmetric and M2=X(X⊤X)−[X⊤X(X⊤X)−X⊤]=X(X⊤X)−X⊤=M. By Theorem 6.3.3, M is the projection onto C(M). Clearly C(M)⊆C(X), and MX=X (again by the lemma) shows C(X)⊆C(M). The rank statement is Proposition 6.3.4(b).

It is worth pausing on how strong Theorem 6.3.7 is. The matrix (X⊤X)− can be almost anything: symmetric or not, reflexive or not, with entries of any size. The sandwich X(⋅)X⊤ removes all of that freedom. We will see in Section 6.4 that this is exactly the difference between coefficients and fitted values, made concrete.

Example (The one-way layout)

In the setting of Example (A rank-deficient layout), sort the observations by group and let group k have nk members. The model space is spanned by the orthogonal vectors z1,…,zg, so by Proposition 6.3.2 with uk=zk/nk, M=∑k=1gzkzk⊤nk=(n1−11n11n1⊤⋱ng−11ng1ng⊤), a block-diagonal matrix of within-group averaging operators. My replaces each observation by its group mean. The intercept column adds nothing to C(X), so it does not appear, and tr(M)=∑knk/nk=g=rank(X). We got here without a generalized inverse. Theorem 6.3.7 guarantees that any generalized inverse of the singular (g+1)×(g+1) matrix X⊤X produces this same matrix. Section 6.4 checks this numerically.

6.3.5. Sums and products of projections

Complicated subspaces are often built from simpler ones, and their projections are built the same way.

Theorem 6.3.8

Let P1,P2 be orthogonal projections. Then P1+P2 is an orthogonal projection iff P1P2=0, that is, iff C(P1)⊥C(P2). In that case it projects onto C(P1)⊕C(P2).

Proof. P1+P2 is always symmetric, and (P1+P2)2=P1+P2+P1P2+P2P1. So it is idempotent iff P1P2+P2P1=0. If P1P2=0, then transposing gives P2P1=0 and the condition holds. Conversely, suppose P1P2+P2P1=0. Multiplying on the left by P1 gives P1P2+P1P2P1=0, and on the right by P1 gives P1P2P1+P2P1=0. Subtracting, P1P2=P2P1, and then 2P1P2=0. The equivalence with orthogonality of ranges holds because P1P2=0 says exactly that every vector of C(P2) is sent to 0 by P1, that is (Proposition 6.3.4(c)), lies in C(P1)⊥. Finally, C(P1+P2)⊆C(P1)+C(P2), and for u=P1u+P2u in that sum we have (P1+P2)u=u, so the ranges agree.

By induction, if P1,…,Pk are projections with PiPj=0 for i≠j, their sum projects onto the orthogonal direct sum of their ranges. When the sum is I, we get an orthogonal decomposition y=P1y+⋯+Pky,‖y‖2=‖P1y‖2+⋯+‖Pky‖2. Every analysis of variance table in this book is a table of such a decomposition.

Products behave differently. P1P2 need not be symmetric or idempotent, and it is generally not a projection. Exercise (B1) shows that it is one iff P1 and P2 commute, in which case it projects onto C(P1)∩C(P2). The most important instance is the nested case C(P2)⊆C(P1), treated in Section 6.5.

6.3.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let S=span{(1,1,0)⊤,(0,1,1)⊤}. Compute PS in two ways: by Gram–Schmidt and Proposition 6.3.2, and by Theorem 6.3.7. Verify symmetry, idempotence and trPS=2. Find S⊥ and check that I−PS projects onto it.

Exercise (A2)

Show that a symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues all lie in {0,1} is an orthogonal projection. Give a non-symmetric 2×2 matrix with eigenvalues 0 and 1 that is not idempotent, and one that is idempotent.

Exercise (A3)

Let P be an orthogonal projection and a,b real numbers. Show that the eigenvalues of aP+bI are b and a+b. Show that when b≠0 and a+b≠0, (aP+bI)−1=1b(I−aa+bP). Use this to invert the equicorrelation matrix (1−ρ)I+ρ11⊤.

Exercise (A4)

Show that if G is a generalized inverse of the symmetric matrix X⊤X, then so is G⊤, and so is GX⊤XG⊤. Which of the four Penrose conditions does the last one satisfy?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let P1,P2 be orthogonal projections. Show that P1P2 is an orthogonal projection iff P1P2=P2P1, and that in this case it projects onto C(P1)∩C(P2). Hint: for the range, show P1P2u=u iff u lies in both subspaces.

Solution. If P1P2=P2P1, then (P1P2)⊤=P2P1=P1P2, and (P1P2)2=P1P2P2P1=P1P2P1=P1P1P2=P1P2. So the product is symmetric and idempotent. Conversely, if P1P2 is an orthogonal projection, it is symmetric, so P1P2=(P1P2)⊤=P2P1. For the range: if u lies in both subspaces, then P1P2u=P1u=u. If P1P2u=u, then u∈C(P1), and also u=P2P1u∈C(P2). A projection’s range is the set of vectors it fixes, so C(P1P2)=C(P1)∩C(P2).

Exercise (B2)

Let G0 be a generalized inverse of A.

  • (a) Show that G0+(I−G0A)U+V(I−AG0) is a generalized inverse of A for all conformable U,V.

  • (b) Show that every generalized inverse G of A has this form. Hint: take U=G−G0 and V=G0A(G−G0), and use A(G−G0)A=0.

  • (c) Conclude that a matrix has a unique generalized inverse iff it is square and nonsingular.

Solution. Parts a,b. For (a), multiply out: A[G0+(I−G0A)U+V(I−AG0)]A=AG0A+(A−AG0A)UA+AV(A−AG0A)=A. For (b), put Δ=G−G0, so that AΔA=A−A=0. With U=Δ and V=G0AΔ, (I−G0A)Δ+G0AΔ(I−AG0)=Δ−G0AΔ+G0AΔ−G0(AΔA)G0=Δ, so the formula reproduces G0+Δ=G.

Exercise (B3)

Using the singular value decomposition, show that (X⊤X)+X⊤=X+ and XX+=M. Show also that X+=(X⊤X)−1X⊤ when X has full column rank.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that M is the only n×n matrix P satisfying PX=X, P⊤=P and rank(P)=rank(X). Is the rank condition necessary? Is symmetry?

15.2  Estimable contrasts

Most questions about the treatments of a one-way experiment are contrasts. Does fertilizer help at all? Is the highest rate worth its cost? Which pairs of treatments differ? Earlier chapters supplied the theory: estimability (Chapter 8), the t test and interval (Chapter 11, Chapter 12) and simultaneous methods (Chapter 13). This section collects them for the one-way analysis, adds a geometric fact about power, and applies them to the nitrogen trial.

15.2.1. Contrasts are the treatment space

Recall that a contrast is a linear function ψ=∑kckμk with ∑kck=0 and c≠0 (Definition 8.7.1). In the effects form it equals ∑kckαk, and the contrasts are exactly the estimable functions of the effects alone (Theorem 8.7.1). Their estimates are ψ^=∑kcky¯k, whatever side condition or coding was used to fit the model.

Behind this lies a simple geometry. Let T=C(Z)∩1⊥ be the treatment space: the vectors that are constant within groups and sum to zero. Its dimension is g−1 (Theorem 6.5.1). A vector u∈T takes some value vk on every observation of group k, with ∑knkvk=0. Put ck=nkvk. Then c is a contrast and u is the vector uc=ZD−1c of Eq. 8.7.2, with D=diag(n1,…,ng). Conversely every uc lies in T (Proposition 8.7.2). So contrasts and nonzero vectors of the treatment space correspond one to one, and ψ^=uc⊤y. A contrast is a direction in the treatment space, and its estimate is the component of the data along that direction.

Equal treatment means say that the mean vector 𝛉θ=E(Y) has no component in T, that is, that every contrast vanishes (Exercise (A2)). The overall test of Section 15.3 looks at all of T; a single contrast looks along one line of it.

15.2.2. The test and interval for one contrast

For a contrast c write vc=∑k=1gck2nk, so that Var(ψ^)=σ2vc (Eq. 8.7.1).

Proposition 15.2.1 (Sum of squares and test for a contrast)

In the normal one-way model, let ψ=∑kckμk be a contrast, ψ^=∑kckY¯k, and ψ0∈R. Then:

  • (a) ψ^∼N(ψ,σ2vc), and ψ^ is independent of s2;

  • (b) the statistic T=ψ^−ψ0svc∼t(n−g,δ),δ=ψ−ψ0σvc, so the test of ψ=ψ0 rejects when |T|>tn−g,α/2, and ψ^±tn−g,α/2svc is an exact 1−α confidence interval for ψ;

  • (c) the sum of squares of the contrast SS(c)=ψ^2vc=‖PcY‖2, where Pc projects onto the line spanned by uc, has one degree of freedom, and SS(c)/s2=T2 for ψ0=0 has the F(1,n−g,δ2) distribution. Replacing c by ac with a≠0 changes neither SS(c) nor the test;

  • (d) SS(c)≤∑knk(Y¯k−Y¯)2, with equality when ck=nk(Y¯k−Y¯) for every k.

Proof. Use the cell-means form, with model matrix Z of full rank g, so that (Z⊤Z)−1=D−1 and c⊤D−1c=vc. The function 𝛍c⊤μ is estimable with estimate 𝛍c⊤μ^=ψ^. (a) and (b) are Theorem 11.6.1(a) and Corollary 7.5.2 with 𝛌λ=c, r=g, and the interval is Theorem 12.1.1. The independence of ψ^ and s2 is part of Theorem 7.5.1. (c) The identity ψ^2/vc=‖PcY‖2 is Proposition 8.7.2(b), and T2 is the F statistic of the hypothesis ψ=0 by Theorem 11.6.1(b), with noncentrality δ2. Scaling c by a multiplies ψ^2 and vc by a2, and leaves the line spanned by uc unchanged. (d) is Exercise (B1).

By part (c) a contrast’s sum of squares is a projection onto a line of T, and by part (d) no line captures more than the whole space. The line that captures all of it is chosen by the data, which is why its t statistic needs Scheffé’s multiplier (Theorem 13.2.2).

15.2.3. Planned contrasts in the nitrogen trial

Before the trial the agronomist wrote down two questions. First, does nitrogen raise yield at all? The natural contrast compares the average of the four fertilized rates with the control, ψ1=14(μ2+μ3+μ4+μ5)−μ1. Second, is the highest rate better than the moderate one? That is ψ2=μ5−μ3, the difference between 160 and 80 kg N/ha.

Example (Two planned contrasts)

The pooled standard deviation is s=0.5211 on 25 degrees of freedom. For ψ1, vc=16(1+4⋅116), the estimate is 2.110 t/ha with standard error 0.238, so t=8.87 and the 95% interval is [1.620, 2.600]. Fertilizer raises yield by roughly two tonnes per hectare. Its sum of squares is 21.370.

For ψ2 the estimate is 0.595 t/ha with standard error 0.301, t=1.98, two-sided p=0.059, and the interval [−0.025, 1.215]. The data are consistent with anything from no gain to a gain of more than a tonne: with six plots per rate, half a tonne is only about 1.7 standard errors of a pairwise difference.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rates = np.array([0, 40, 80, 120, 160])            # kg N per hectare
g, m = len(rates), 6                                # 5 rates, 6 plots per rate
true_means = 6.8 - 2.7 * np.exp(-0.018 * rates)     # unknown to the analyst
rng = np.random.default_rng(1515)
group = np.repeat(np.arange(g), m)                  # plot i received rate group[i]
y = np.round(true_means[group] + rng.normal(0, 0.5, g * m), 2)   # yield, t/ha
n = len(y)

n_k = np.bincount(group).astype(float)
means = np.bincount(group, weights=y) / n_k
nu = n - g
s = np.sqrt(np.sum((y - means[group]) ** 2) / nu)

def contrast(c, psi0=0.0, mult=None):
    """Estimate, standard error, t statistic, SS and interval for sum_k c_k mu_k."""
    est = c @ means
    v = np.sum(c ** 2 / n_k)                        # Var(estimate) / sigma^2
    se = s * np.sqrt(v)
    mult = stats.t.ppf(0.975, nu) if mult is None else mult
    return est, se, (est - psi0) / se, est ** 2 / v, (est - mult * se, est + mult * se)

c_fert = np.array([-1, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25])     # fertilized (average) minus control
c_top = np.array([0, 0, -1, 0, 1])                  # 160 against 80 kg N/ha
for name, c in (("fertilized - control", c_fert), ("160 - 80", c_top)):
    est, se, t, ss, (lo, hi) = contrast(c)
    print(f"{name:21s} {est:6.3f}  se {se:.3f}  t = {t:5.2f}  SS = {ss:6.3f}"
          f"  95% interval [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]")

Each was a single planned question, so each gets an unadjusted t interval; joint coverage for the pair would use Bonferroni’s t25,0.0125. Writing the questions down in advance fixes a small family (Section 13.6).

15.2.4. Families of comparisons

Three families come up in almost every one-way analysis, and Chapter 13 supplies a method for each. The multipliers below multiply the standard error of a difference of two means, s2/m=0.3009 here.

Comparisons with a control. The four differences μk−μ1, k=2,…,5, form a planned family of size four. Bonferroni’s method (Theorem 13.4.1) uses the multiplier t25,0.05/8=2.692 in place of the unadjusted 2.060:

Rate against control Difference Bonferroni 95% interval
40 1.232 [0.422, 2.041]
80 2.052 [1.242, 2.861]
120 2.510 [1.700, 3.320]
160 2.647 [1.837, 3.456]

Every fertilized rate beats the control. The four estimates share y¯1, so they are positively correlated, with correlation 12 in a balanced design (Exercise (B4)). Dunnett (1955) computed the exact joint distribution of the four t statistics under this correlation; his multiplier here is 2.606 (Exercise (B2) characterizes it), a few percent shorter than Bonferroni’s. We do not derive its tables.

All pairs. Tukey’s method (Theorem 13.3.2) covers all ten differences μk−μl with exact joint coverage in a balanced design. With q0.05(5,25)=4.153 the half-width is qs/m=0.884, and six of the ten pairs are declared different. The four that are not are 40 and 80, 80 and 120, 80 and 160, and 120 and 160. The conclusions are not transitive: 40 is not distinguished from 80, nor 80 from 160, yet 40 and 160 are declared different, since 6.652−5.237=1.415 exceeds 0.884. “Not declared different” means “not enough evidence of a difference”, which is not an equivalence relation, so displays that sort treatments into groups of “equal” means should be read with care, especially in unbalanced designs.

Contrasts suggested by the data. Looking at Figure 15.1.1, one might ask whether the response has levelled off, by comparing the average of the two highest rates with 80 kg N/ha, 12(μ4+μ5)−μ3. The estimate is 0.527 with standard error 0.261. The unadjusted interval [−0.010, 1.063] is not legitimate, because the contrast was chosen after seeing the means. Scheffé’s interval (Eq. 13.2.4), with multiplier 4F0.05(4,25)=3.322, is [−0.339, 1.392]. It is valid however the contrast was found, and it says the question is open.

import itertools

k_ctrl = g - 1                                      # four comparisons with the control
bonf = stats.t.ppf(1 - 0.05 / (2 * k_ctrl), nu)     # Bonferroni multiplier
se_pair = s * np.sqrt(2 / m)
for k in range(1, g):
    d = means[k] - means[0]
    print(f"{rates[k]:3d} - 0: {d:6.3f}  Bonferroni interval"
          f" [{d - bonf * se_pair:.3f}, {d + bonf * se_pair:.3f}]")

q = stats.studentized_range.ppf(0.95, g, nu)        # q_{0.05}(g, nu)
hsd = q * s / np.sqrt(m)                            # Tukey half-width, all pairs
pairs = list(itertools.combinations(range(g), 2))
different = [(int(rates[k]), int(rates[l])) for k, l in pairs if abs(means[k] - means[l]) > hsd]
print(f"Tukey half-width {hsd:.3f}; pairs declared different: {len(different)} of {len(pairs)}")
print("not declared different:",
      [(int(rates[k]), int(rates[l])) for k, l in pairs if abs(means[k] - means[l]) <= hsd])

15.2.5. Why planned contrasts are powerful

A single contrast is tested with one numerator degree of freedom, the overall test with g−1. When the scientific question is sharp, the contrast is the better test, and the reason is geometric. Write PT for the projection onto the treatment space and 𝛉γT=‖PTθ‖2σ2=1σ2∑k=1gnk(μk−μ¯)2, the squared length of the treatment component of the mean in units of σ2. (Section 15.3 shows that it is the noncentrality of the overall F test.)

Proposition 15.2.2 (The noncentrality of a contrast)

Let c be a contrast with vector uc∈T, and suppose 𝛉PTθ≠0. The noncentrality of the test of ψ=0 in Proposition 15.2.1 is δ2=ψ2σ2vc=γTcos2⁡φ, where φ is the angle between uc and 𝛉PTθ. In particular δ2≤γT, with equality iff uc is parallel to 𝛉PTθ, that is, iff ck∝nk(μk−μ¯).

Proof. By Proposition 8.7.2(a) applied to the mean vector, 𝛉ψ=uc⊤θ, and vc=‖uc‖2 by the computation in the proof of that result. Since uc∈T, 𝛉𝛉uc⊤θ=uc⊤PTθ. So 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉δ2=(uc⊤PTθ)2σ2‖uc‖2=‖PTθ‖2σ2⋅(uc⊤PTθ)2‖uc‖2‖PTθ‖2=γTcos2⁡φ by Eq. 6.2.2. Equality in cos2⁡φ≤1 holds iff the two vectors are parallel. The vector 𝛉PTθ has entry μk−μ¯ in group k (Theorem 6.5.1), so it is uc for ck=nk(μk−μ¯).

If the departure from equal means lies along the planned contrast, the contrast test gets all of the noncentrality on one degree of freedom, while the overall test spreads the same noncentrality over g−1. For the nitrogen design, with 25 error degrees of freedom, the powers at level 0.05 are:

γT, all along the contrast 4 8 12
contrast test, F(1,25) 0.485 0.776 0.914
overall test, F(4,25) 0.269 0.516 0.714

The price is paid when the question is wrong: a contrast orthogonal to 𝛉PTθ has power α however large the treatment differences, and a direction chosen at random captures on average only 1/(g−1) of γT (Exercise (C1)).

def power_f(gamma, q, nu, alpha=0.05):
    return stats.ncf.sf(stats.f.ppf(1 - alpha, q, nu), q, nu, gamma)

for gamma in (4.0, 8.0, 12.0):                      # all of it along the planned contrast
    print(f"gamma = {gamma:4.1f}: contrast test {power_f(gamma, 1, nu):.3f},"
          f" omnibus F test {power_f(gamma, g - 1, nu):.3f}")

15.2.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In the nitrogen trial, compute by hand the estimate and standard error of 12(μ4+μ5)−12(μ1+μ2), the average of the two highest rates minus the average of the two lowest, from the rate means of Section 15.1 and s=0.5211.

Solution. The estimate is 12(6.515+6.652)−12(4.005+5.237)=6.5835−4.621=1.9625. With m=6, vc=4⋅14/6=1/6, so the standard error is 0.5211/6≈0.213.

Exercise (A2)

Show directly that the t statistic of Proposition 15.2.1(b) with ψ0=0 is unchanged when c is multiplied by a>0 and changes sign when a<0. What happens to the confidence interval?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In a balanced layout with m observations per group, show that the noncentrality of the test of a contrast is δ2=mψ2/(σ2∑kck2). How many observations per group are needed for the test of μ5−μ3 in the nitrogen trial to have power 0.8 at level 0.05 when the true difference is 0.5 t/ha and σ=0.5? (Use software for the noncentral F, or the normal approximation.)

Solution. Here vc=∑kck2/m, so δ2=ψ2/(σ2vc)=mψ2/(σ2∑kck2). For μ5−μ3, ∑kck2=2, ψ/σ=1, and δ2=m/2. The normal approximation asks for δ≈z0.025+z0.2=1.96+0.84=2.80, so m≈2⋅2.802≈15.7. The exact noncentral F with 5(m−1) error degrees of freedom gives m=17, since the t quantile exceeds the normal one. The trial’s m=6 is far short.

Exercise (B2)

A single contrast c matters above all. For a fixed total n, show that vc=∑kck2/nk is minimized, over positive real nk with ∑knk=n, by nk∝|ck|, with minimum (∑k|ck|)2/n. What does this say about how to allocate plots between two treatments, and between a control and the average of four treatments?

Solution. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, (∑k|ck|)2=(∑k(|ck|/nk)nk)2≤∑k(ck2/nk)∑knk=nvc, with equality iff |ck|/nk∝nk, that is, nk∝|ck|. Groups with ck=0 get no observations. For μ1−μ2, equal allocation is best. For μ1−14(μ2+⋯+μ5), the control should get as many plots as the four treatments together, n1=n/2 and nk=n/8 for the others.

Exercise (B3)

In a comparison of g−1 treatments with a control, suppose the control gets n0 observations and each treatment gets nt, with n0+(g−1)nt=n fixed. Show that ∑kVar(Y¯k−Y¯0) is minimized when n0=g−1nt. For g=5 and n=30, what allocation does this suggest?

Solution. The sum is σ2(g−1)(1/n0+1/nt). Minimizing 1/n0+1/nt subject to n0+(g−1)nt=n with a Lagrange multiplier gives 1/n02=λ and 1/nt2=(g−1)λ, so n0/nt=g−1. For g=5 the control should get twice as many observations as each treatment: n0=10 and nt=5. Contrast Exercise (B2): for the average of the four comparisons the optimal control share is n/2, that is n0=4nt. The best allocation depends on whether the four comparisons or their average is the target.

Exercise (B4)

With n0 observations on the control and nt on each treatment, show that the estimates Y¯k−Y¯0 and Y¯l−Y¯0 of two treatment-versus-control differences have correlation nt/(nt+n0). In a balanced design it is 12.

Solution. The covariance is Var(Y¯0)=σ2/n0, since the treatment means are independent of each other and of Y¯0. Each variance is σ2(1/nt+1/n0). The ratio is (1/n0)/(1/nt+1/n0)=nt/(nt+n0).

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let q=g−1≥2 and let u be uniformly distributed on the unit sphere of the q-dimensional space T (for example u=w/‖w‖ with w∼N on T with covariance PT). Show that Ecos2⁡φ=1/q, where φ is the angle between u and a fixed nonzero v∈T. Interpret this with Proposition 15.2.2.

Solution. Choose an orthonormal basis of T whose first vector is v/‖v‖. In these coordinates cos2⁡φ=u12. By symmetry the ui2 have a common mean, and they sum to 1, so Eu12=1/q. By Proposition 15.2.2, a contrast whose direction is unrelated to the truth captures on average a fraction 1/q of γT on its one degree of freedom, the same average share per degree of freedom as the overall test. Knowledge, not the count of degrees of freedom, is what makes a planned contrast more powerful.

Exercise (C2)

The Scheffé multiplier for a family of contrasts depends on the dimension of the space the family spans (Theorem 13.2.2). Show that the g−1 comparisons with a control span T, and that the family of the four “successive differences” μk+1−μk does too. Why, then, are Bonferroni or Dunnett intervals shorter than Scheffé’s for these families?

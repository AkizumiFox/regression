38.2  Quasi-likelihood estimating equations

Estimation here starts from Eq. 38.1.1 and not from a density. There are two useful ways to read it: as the derivative of a function that is not quite a log-likelihood but close enough to carry the usual arguments, and as the best member of a large family of estimating equations. This section develops both, and they meet in the middle.

38.2.1. The quasi-likelihood function

Definition 38.2.1 (Quasi-likelihood)

Under Definition 38.1.1, the quasi-likelihood of a mean μ∈M given a response y in the closure of M, with weight w, is

Q(μ;y)=wϕ∫yμy−tV(t)dt,
(38.2.1)

assumed finite, the integral being read as a limit at an endpoint of M where it converges — as it does at y=0 for V(t)=t and at y∈{0,1} for V(t)=t(1−t). The quasi-likelihood of the model is 𝛃Q(β)=∑i=1nQ(μi;yi) with 𝛃μi=h(x(i)⊤β) and weight wi.

The definition is designed to make one identity true by construction, and everything else follows from it.

Proposition 38.2.1 (What the quasi-likelihood does and does not share with a log-likelihood)

Write Q′=∂Q/∂μ. Then, for every variance specification:

  • (a) Q′(μ;y)=w(y−μ)/{ϕV(μ)}, and Q(μ;y)≤0 with equality only at μ=y: the quasi-likelihood is maximized at the observation, as a saturated fit would be;

  • (b) E{Q′(μ;Y)}=0 and Var{Q′(μ;Y)}=−E{∂2Q/∂μ2}=w/{ϕV(μ)}, the two Bartlett identities;

  • (c) if (V,ϕ,w) are those of an exponential dispersion family (Definition 34.1.1), then Q(μ;y)=ℓ(μ;y)−ℓ(y;y), so Q is the log-likelihood measured from its saturated value, and 𝛃𝛍−2ϕQ(β)=D(y,μ) is the deviance of Eq. 34.5.4.

Proof.

  • (a) Differentiating Eq. 38.2.1 in its upper limit gives the stated Q′. Because V>0, the integrand (y−t)/V(t) is positive for t<y and negative for t>y, so for μ>y the integral is of a negative function and for μ<y it is minus the integral of a positive one: both give Q≤0, with equality only for an empty interval.

  • (b) E(Y−μ)=0 gives the first identity, and Var(Y)=ϕV(μ)/w gives Var(Q′)=w/{ϕV(μ)}. Differentiating Q′ again, ∂2Q∂μ2=−wϕ⋅V(μ)+(y−μ)V′(μ)V(μ)2, whose expectation is −w/{ϕV(μ)}, since the term carrying y−μ has mean zero.

  • (c) For an exponential dispersion family the chain rule used in the proof of Theorem 34.3.1 gives ∂ℓ/∂μ=w(y−μ)/{ϕV(μ)}, which is Q′; two functions of μ with the same derivative differ by a constant, and both sides vanish at μ=y. Comparing with Eq. 34.5.4 gives D=−2ϕQ.

The converse of (c) fails: exp⁡Q is in general not a density in y (Exercise (C1)). Part (c) is nevertheless why the objects here look familiar — everything the exponential family contributed to Definition 34.5.1 is already in the integral Eq. 38.2.1 — and for a variance function from no family Eq. 38.2.1 still measures the discrepancy between a fit and an observation, the quasi-deviance

𝛍𝛃D(y,μ)=−2ϕQ(β)=2∑i=1nwi∫μiyiyi−tV(t)dt ≥0,
(38.2.2)

which does not involve ϕ and vanishes only at 𝛍μ=y. Figure 38.2.1 draws one term for four power variance functions: only V≡1 penalizes symmetrically, and under V(μ)=μ a fitted mean one unit too small costs 2.043 times as much as one unit too large, against 4.254 for V(μ)=μ2 and 9.000 for V(μ)=μ3. A variance function that grows with the mean is a statement about which errors matter.

import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate

def quasi_deviance_term(y, mu, V):
    """2 * integral from mu to y of (y - t)/V(t) dt: one observation's quasi-deviance."""
    value, _ = integrate.quad(lambda t: (y - t) / V(t), mu, y)
    return 2 * value

y0 = 2.0
closed_form = {
    "V = 1": (lambda m: (y0 - m) ** 2, lambda t: 1.0),
    "V = mu": (lambda m: 2 * (y0 * np.log(y0 / m) - (y0 - m)), lambda t: t),
    "V = mu^2": (lambda m: 2 * (-np.log(y0 / m) + (y0 - m) / m), lambda t: t ** 2),
    "V = mu^3": (lambda m: (y0 - m) ** 2 / (y0 * m ** 2), lambda t: t ** 3),
}
for name, (formula, V) in closed_form.items():
    print(f"{name:9s}  at mu = 0.8: {quasi_deviance_term(y0, 0.8, V):.6f}"
          f"   closed form {formula(0.8):.6f}")

38.2.2. The quasi-score

Theorem 38.2.2 (The quasi-score)

Let 𝛃𝛃U(β)=∂Q/∂β be the quasi-score, and keep the notation W=diag{wih′(ηi)2/V(μi)} and D=diag{h′(ηi)} of Theorem 34.3.1. Write An=ϕ−1X⊤WX. Then:

  • (a) 𝛃𝛍U(β)=ϕ−1X⊤WD−1(y−μ), which is Eq. 34.3.1 exactly. The estimating equations 𝛃U(β^)=0 are Eq. 38.1.1, they do not involve ϕ, and they are solved by the iteratively reweighted least squares of Theorem 34.4.1;

  • (b) if the mean model is correct then 𝛃EU(β0)=0, whatever the true variances are: the quasi-score is an unbiased estimating function;

  • (c) 𝛃−E{∂U/∂β⊤}=An, again whatever the true variances are;

  • (d) if in addition the variance specification Eq. 38.1.2 is correct, then 𝛃Cov{U(β0)}=An as well: sensitivity and variability agree, which is the second Bartlett identity for Q;

  • (e) if instead Var(Yi)=σi2 for arbitrary σi2, then

    𝛃Cov{U(β0)}=Bn=1ϕ2∑i=1n{wih′(ηi)V(μi)}2σi2x(i)x(i)⊤,
    (38.2.3)

    which equals An if and only if ∑i{wihi′/Vi}2{σi2−ϕVi/wi}x(i)x(i)⊤=0.

Proof.

  • (a) By Proposition 38.2.1(a) and the chain rule, ∂Q∂βj=∑i=1nQ′(μi;yi)dμidηixij=1ϕ∑i=1nxijwih′(ηi)V(μi)(yi−μi), which is the coordinate form of Eq. 34.3.1. The map from (V,h,w,ϕ) to it is the one computed in Theorem 34.3.1(a), so the matrix form, the absence of ϕ and the iteration of Theorem 34.4.1 transfer unchanged; none of the three used the density.

  • (b) Every term carries the factor yi−μi, which has mean zero when 𝛃μi=h(x(i)⊤β0). No assumption about Var(Yi) is used.

  • (c) Write a(η)=h′(η)/V{h(η)}, so that U=ϕ−1∑iwia(ηi)(yi−μi)x(i). Differentiating, 𝛃∂U∂β⊤=1ϕ∑i=1nwi{a′(ηi)(yi−μi)−a(ηi)h′(ηi)}x(i)x(i)⊤, the computation of Theorem 34.3.1(c). Expectation annihilates the first term whatever the variances, and wia(ηi)h′(ηi)=Wii leaves −An.

  • (d) and (e). The summands of U are independent with mean zero, so Cov(U)=ϕ−2∑iwi2a(ηi)2Var(Yi)x(i)x(i)⊤, which is Eq. 38.2.3. Substituting σi2=ϕV(μi)/wi turns the ith coefficient into ϕ−2wi2{hi′/Vi}2ϕVi/wi=ϕ−1wihi′2/Vi, giving An. The stated condition is the difference of the two sums.

Remark (Existence and uniqueness). Nothing above guarantees that Eq. 38.1.1 has a solution, or only one. Under the canonical link of the variance function, g′(μ)=1/V(μ), the proof of (c) has a≡1 and a′≡0, so the Jacobian is −An, negative definite whenever rank(X)=p; Q is then strictly concave and a root, if one exists, is the unique maximizer, by Theorem 34.3.3 with b″ replaced by V. For other links Q can have several stationary points, as the warning after Theorem 34.3.3 showed.

Example (Strike durations)

Kennan’s data record the length in days of 62 strikes in US manufacturing, with a measure of unanticipated industrial production at the time each strike began. Take a log link and V(μ)=μ. The production coefficient is −7.680; replacing V(μ)=μ by V(μ)=7.5μ returns the same number to ten decimal places, since ϕ cancels from Eq. 38.1.1, while V(μ)=μ2 moves it to −9.353, a quadratic variance function downweighting the long strikes.

The mean duration is 42.66 days and the sample variance 2102.7, nearly fifty times as large: a Poisson likelihood is no serious description of these data, and the question is how much of the Poisson machinery survives anyway.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

data = sm.datasets.strikes.load_pandas().data
y = data["duration"].to_numpy(float)          # length of the strike, in days
x = data["iprod"].to_numpy(float)             # unanticipated industrial production
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), x])
n, p = X.shape

def quasi_irls(X, y, V, steps=60):
    """Solve the quasi-score equations for a log link and variance function V."""
    beta = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    beta[0] = np.log(y.mean())
    for _ in range(steps):
        eta = X @ beta
        mu = np.exp(eta)
        W = mu ** 2 / V(mu)                   # working weight (dmu/deta)^2 / V(mu)
        z = eta + (y - mu) / mu               # working response
        beta = np.linalg.solve((X.T * W) @ X, (X.T * W) @ z)
    return beta

beta_lin = quasi_irls(X, y, lambda m: m)              # V(mu) = mu
beta_scaled = quasi_irls(X, y, lambda m: 7.5 * m)     # V(mu) = phi mu: same equations
beta_sq = quasi_irls(X, y, lambda m: m ** 2)          # V(mu) = mu^2: different equations
print(f"V = mu       {beta_lin}")
print(f"V = 7.5 mu   {beta_scaled}")
print(f"V = mu^2     {beta_sq}")

38.2.3. Optimality among linear estimating functions

The second view asks what else might have been used. Any function of the data and the parameter with mean zero at 𝛃β0 is an unbiased estimating function, and setting it to zero defines an estimator; the quasi-score is the best of a natural class.

Proposition 38.2.3 (Godambe optimality)

Assume Eq. 38.1.2, and consider linear estimating functions 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛍g(β)=∑i=1nai(β){yi−μi(β)}=C(β)(y−μ), where C=[a1,…,an] is p×n, differentiable in 𝛃β, and does not depend on y. Let 𝛃S=−E{∂g/∂β⊤} and suppose S is nonsingular. Define the Godambe information 𝛃J(g)=S⊤Cov{g(β0)}−1S.

  • (a) Every such g is unbiased, and S=CDX.

  • (b) J(g)⪯ϕ−1X⊤WX=J(U) in the order of nonnegative definiteness, for every linear estimating function g.

  • (c) Equality holds if and only if C⊤=Σ−1DXN for a nonsingular p×p matrix N, with Σ=Cov(Y): the coefficients are those of the quasi-score up to one common nonsingular transformation, and g then has the same roots as U.

Proof.

  • (a) Unbiasedness follows from 𝛍E(y−μ)=0. Differentiating, 𝛃𝛃𝛍𝛃∂g∂β⊤=∑i=1n∂ai∂β⊤(yi−μi)−C∂μ∂β⊤, read entry by entry. Its expectation vanishes because ai does not depend on y, and 𝛍𝛃∂μ/∂β⊤=DX, so S=CDX.

  • (b) and (c). Under Eq. 38.1.2, Σ=diag{ϕV(μi)/wi} is positive definite. Set F=Σ1/2C⊤,K=Σ−1/2DX, both n×p. Then Cov(g)=CΣC⊤=F⊤F and S=CDX=F⊤K, so J(g)=K⊤F(F⊤F)−1F⊤K=K⊤PK, where P=F(F⊤F)−1F⊤ is the orthogonal projection onto C(F) (Theorem 6.3.6), and F⊤F is nonsingular because S is. As I−P is symmetric idempotent, v⊤(I−P)v=‖(I−P)v‖2≥0, so P⪯I and J(g)⪯K⊤K. Finally K⊤K=X⊤DΣ−1DX=∑i=1nwih′(ηi)2ϕV(μi)x(i)x(i)⊤=1ϕX⊤WX, and Theorem 38.2.2(a),(d) identify this as J(U), since for the quasi-score sensitivity and variability are both An.

Equality in (b) means K⊤(I−P)K=0, that is C(K)⊆C(F). Both matrices have p columns and, because F⊤K is nonsingular, rank p; so their column spaces coincide and K=FN for a nonsingular N. Written out this is C⊤=Σ−1DXN−1, whose ith row is a fixed multiple of wih′(ηi)x(i)⊤/V(μi). Then g=N−⊤U, because 𝛍X⊤DΣ−1(y−μ) already carries the factor ϕ−1 that U has; so g vanishes exactly where U does.

Warning. The optimality is within the class of linear estimating functions. A known true density would have a score nonlinear in y with a larger Godambe information — the NB1 score Eq. 37.4.6 is an instance — so “best linear” is the honest reading, as in Theorem 7.2.1. Optimality also assumes Eq. 38.1.2; under a wrong variance function the quasi-score stays unbiased but is no longer optimal, and Section 38.3 prices the loss.

38.2.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Compute ϕw−1Q(μ;y) from Eq. 38.2.1 for V(μ)=1, V(μ)=μ, V(μ)=μ(1−μ) and V(μ)=μ2, and check that in each case it is the log-likelihood kernel of the corresponding family, up to a function of y alone.

Solution. For V=1, ∫yμ(y−t)dt=−(y−μ)2/2. For V(t)=t the integrand is y/t−1, giving the Poisson kernel ylog⁡μ−μ. For V(t)=t(1−t), partial fractions give y/t−(1−y)/(1−t), whose integral is the binomial kernel ylog⁡μ+(1−y)log⁡(1−μ). For V(t)=t2 the integrand is y/t2−1/t, giving the gamma kernel −y/μ−log⁡μ.

Exercise (A2)

Show that the NB2 variance function V(μ)=μ+μ2/κ with κ known gives ϕw−1Q(μ;y)=ylog⁡{μ/(μ+κ)}+κlog⁡{κ/(μ+κ)} up to a function of y, which is the negative binomial log-likelihood kernel of Eq. 37.4.1. NB2 with κ known is an exponential dispersion family, so Proposition 38.2.1(c) applies; explain why the quasi-Poisson specification V(μ)=μ with ϕ free does not enjoy the same conclusion, and relate this to the remark of Section 38.1.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Take n independent responses with EYi=μ (one unknown parameter, no regressors) and VarYi=ϕV(μ)/wi. Show that the quasi-score estimate of Theorem 38.2.2 is the weighted mean ∑iwiyi/∑iwi, and verify Proposition 38.2.3 directly: among estimating functions ∑iai(yi−μ) with constants ai, the Godambe information is maximized by ai∝wi.

Solution. Parameterizing by μ makes h′≡1, so Eq. 38.1.1 reads ∑iwi(yi−μ)/V(μ)=0 and μ^=∑iwiyi/∑iwi. For a general a the sensitivity is S=∑iai and the variability is ϕV(μ)∑iai2/wi, so J=(∑iai)2ϕV(μ)∑iai2/wi≤∑iwiϕV(μ) by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to ∑iai=∑i(ai/wi)wi, with equality if and only if ai∝wi. The bound is ϕ−1X⊤WX with X=1.

Exercise (B2)

Suppose the link satisfies g′(μ)=1/V(μ), the canonical link of the variance function. Show that Eq. 38.1.1 becomes ∑iwixij(yi−μ^i)=0 for every j, so that the fit reproduces the weighted column totals of X exactly, as in Corollary 34.3.2. Check the condition for V(μ)=μ with a log link and for V(μ)=μ(1−μ) with a logit link, and find the canonical link of V(μ)=μ2.

Solution. Since h is the inverse of g, h′(η)=1/g′(μ)=V(μ), so the coefficient wih′(ηi)/V(μi) in Eq. 38.1.1 is wi. For V(μ)=μ the log link has g′(μ)=1/μ; for V(μ)=μ(1−μ) the logit link has g′(μ)=1/{μ(1−μ)}; for V(μ)=μ2 the condition g′(μ)=μ−2 gives g(μ)=−1/μ, the canonical link of the gamma family. The conclusion is the quasi-likelihood form of Corollary 34.3.2, proved there for exponential dispersion families but needing only Eq. 38.1.1.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Take the quasi-binomial specification V(t)=t(1−t) on (0,1) with w=1 and any ϕ>0. (a) Show that y↦exp⁡{Q(μ;y)} is integrable over (0,1), so normalizes to a density f. (b) Show that a distribution on [0,1] with mean m has variance at most m(1−m), with equality only on {0,1}; deduce that f has variance strictly below m(1−m) at its own mean m, never ϕm(1−m) with ϕ≥1. (c) Show that f is symmetric about 1/2 when μ=1/2, and check numerically that at μ=0.3, ϕ=1 it has mean 0.4408 and variance 0.0687, against μ=0.3 and ϕμ(1−μ)=0.21. Conclude that integrability of exp⁡Q does not make a variance specification a family.

Solution.

  • (a) Q≤0 by Proposition 38.2.1(a), so exp⁡Q≤1 on a bounded interval.

  • (b) For Y∈[0,1], E{Y(1−Y)}≥0 gives EY2≤m and hence VarY≤m−m2; equality forces Y(1−Y)=0 almost surely, which a density on the open interval excludes.

  • (c) By Exercise (A1) the exponent is ϕ−1{ylog⁡μ+(1−y)log⁡(1−μ)−ylog⁡y−(1−y)log⁡(1−y)}, unchanged by y↦1−y when μ=1/2. Elsewhere neither moment is reproduced: exp⁡Q normalizes without the result being a distribution with the moments of Eq. 38.1.2.

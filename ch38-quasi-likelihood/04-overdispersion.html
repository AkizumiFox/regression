38.4  Overdispersion

The word has been used loosely so far. This section says where the extra variability comes from, how to see it, what ignoring it costs, and which repair to make.

38.4.1. Where it comes from

Proposition 38.4.1 (Sources of overdispersion, and the price of ignoring it)

  • (a) Unobserved heterogeneity. Let Y∣Λ have mean Λ and variance V∗(Λ), and let Λ have mean μ and variance τ2, with 0<τ2<∞ and E{V∗(Λ)}<∞. Then EY=μ and VarY=E{V∗(Λ)}+τ2, which for a Poisson kernel V∗(λ)=λ is μ+τ2>μ: any unmodelled variation in the mean overdisperses.

  • (b) Clustering. If the response is an average of m units with pairwise correlation ρ>0, its variance is inflated by 1+ρ(m−1), by Lemma 38.1.1; the same factor applies to a sum of m correlated indicators.

  • (c) Contagion. Let Y=∑j=1NSj, where N is Poisson with mean ν and the Sj are independent copies of a positive integer-valued S with ES2<∞, independent of N: events arrive in clusters. Then EY=νES and VarY=νES2, so

    VarYEY=ES2ES ≥ ES ≥ 1,
    (38.4.1)

    with equality throughout if and only if S≡1, that is unless the events arrive singly.

  • (d) Neither of the above. A wrong mean model, an omitted interaction or a few gross outliers all inflate X2, and no dispersion parameter repairs them; for ungrouped binary data a large X2 is always a statement about the mean model.

  • (e) The price. Suppose the mean model is correct and the true variances are σi2=ϕ0V(μi0)/wi with ϕ0≥1 unknown, while inference uses ϕ=1. Then under (Q1)–(Q5) any statistic of Proposition 34.5.3 for a q-dimensional hypothesis converges to ϕ0χ2(q), so a nominal level-α test has asymptotic size

    Pr{χ2(q)>χ1−α2(q)/ϕ0} ≥ α,
    (38.4.2)

    increasing in ϕ0, and tending to 1 as q→∞ for every fixed ϕ0>1. Standard errors are too small by the factor ϕ01/2.

Proof.

  • (a) The law of total variance gives VarY=E{V∗(Λ)}+τ2, and EΛ=μ; this is Lemma 37.4.1(a) with the mixing distribution left arbitrary.

  • (b) is Lemma 38.1.1.

  • (c) Conditioning on N gives E(Y∣N)=NES and Var(Y∣N)=NVarS, so EY=νES and VarY=E(N)VarS+Var(N)(ES)2=ν{VarS+(ES)2}=νES2, using VarN=EN=ν. Dividing gives VarY/EY=ES2/ES; the first inequality in Eq. 38.4.1 is ES2≥(ES)2, equality only for degenerate S, and the second is ES≥1. Equality in both forces S≡1, and then Y=N is Poisson.

  • (d) The binary claim is the warning of Section 38.1; the rest records what X2 measures and is not a mathematical assertion.

  • (e) By Theorem 38.3.1(a) with Bn=ϕ0An — which is Theorem 38.2.2(e) at σi2=ϕ0Vi/wi with the working ϕ=1 — 𝛃𝛃An1/2(β^−β0)→Np(0,ϕ0I). The Wald statistic computed as though the covariance were An−1 is 𝛃𝛃‖(I−Pn)An1/2(β^−β0)‖2 for a projection Pn of rank p−q, as in the proof of Proposition 34.5.3, hence converges to ϕ0 times a χ2(q) variable; the other two differ from it by op(1) by the same proof, since Proposition 38.3.2(c) extends the expansion to Q. The size Eq. 38.4.2 follows and exceeds α because ϕ0>1 lowers the threshold; the limit as q→∞ is Exercise (A1).

These sizes are worse than intuition suggests. At ϕ0=2 a nominal 5% test of one coefficient rejects with probability 0.166 and of six coefficients with probability 0.391; at ϕ0=5 the figures are 0.381 and 0.866. A dispersion of five is not unusual — the strike data of Example (Strike durations) have ϕ^P=39.89.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def naive_size(phi, q):
    """Size of a nominal 5% chi-squared test when the statistic is inflated by phi."""
    return stats.chi2.sf(stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, q) / phi, q)

print("phi   q = 1    q = 3    q = 6")
for phi in (1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 10.0):
    print(f"{phi:4.1f} " + "  ".join(f"{naive_size(phi, q):7.3f}" for q in (1, 3, 6)))
Example (A test that rejects six times too often)

Counts were simulated with a constant mean 6.0, a variance 4.0 times the mean and a true slope of zero, and a Poisson log-linear model was fitted to each of 5000 data sets of size 100. Referring the drop in deviance to χ2(1) rejects at the nominal 5% level in 0.322 of the replicates — Eq. 38.4.2 predicts 0.327 at ϕ0=4 — with the statistic averaging 3.90 against a χ2(1) mean of one. Dividing by ϕ^P and referring to F(1,98) rejects in 0.051, and the sandwich Wald test Eq. 38.3.2 in 0.058 (Figure 38.4.1).

def poisson_fit(X, y, steps=20):
    beta = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    beta[0] = np.log(max(y.mean(), 0.5))
    for _ in range(steps):
        mu = np.exp(X @ beta)
        z = X @ beta + (y - mu) / mu
        beta = np.linalg.solve((X.T * mu) @ X, (X.T * mu) @ z)
    return beta, np.exp(X @ beta)

def poisson_deviance(y, mu):
    return 2 * np.sum(np.where(y > 0, y * np.log(np.maximum(y, 1e-300) / mu), 0.0) - (y - mu))

def null_statistics(n, phi, mu0, reps, rng):
    """Three statistics for the slope, on data whose true slope is zero."""
    x = np.linspace(-1.0, 1.0, n)
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])
    shape = np.full(n, mu0 / (phi - 1.0))            # negative binomial with variance phi*mu
    out = np.empty((reps, 3))
    for r in range(reps):
        y = rng.negative_binomial(shape, shape / (shape + mu0)).astype(float)
        beta, mu = poisson_fit(X, y)
        beta0, mu_null = poisson_fit(X[:, :1], y)
        drop = poisson_deviance(y, mu_null) - poisson_deviance(y, mu)
        phi_hat = np.sum((y - mu) ** 2 / mu) / (n - 2)
        bread = np.linalg.inv((X.T * mu) @ X)
        sand = bread @ ((X * ((y - mu) ** 2)[:, None]).T @ X) @ bread
        out[r] = [drop, drop / phi_hat, beta[1] ** 2 / sand[1, 1]]
    return out

N, PHI, MU0 = 100, 4.0, 6.0
rng = np.random.default_rng(3841)
stat = null_statistics(N, PHI, MU0, 400, rng)
rates = [np.mean(stat[:, 0] > stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, 1)),
         np.mean(stat[:, 1] > stats.f.ppf(0.95, 1, N - 2)),
         np.mean(stat[:, 2] > stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, 1))]
print(f"rejection rates at the 5% level: naive {rates[0]:.3f},"
      f" quasi-F {rates[1]:.3f}, sandwich Wald {rates[2]:.3f}")

38.4.2. Seeing it

Four diagnostics, in the order in which they should be used. Check the mean model first: an omitted term can remove an apparent dispersion entirely, as Example (Twenty thousand records in thirty-two rows) showed, and only a mean model that has survived Definition 34.5.2 and the plots of Chapter 20 makes a dispersion parameter mean what it says. Compare ϕ^P=X2/(n−p) with one, remembering that it is calibrated only where Theorem 34.5.5(b) applies, noisy for ungrouped counts with small means, and meaningless for ungrouped binary data. Use a score test: for counts, Theorem 37.4.3 tests the Poisson against NB2 alternatives from the Poisson fit alone, with a one-sided alternative. Compare the two covariances: a large discrepancy between ϕ^P(X⊤W^X)−1 and the sandwich Eq. 38.3.2 says the shape of the variance function is wrong and not merely its scale, while a small one, as in Example (Four standard errors for the strike data), is evidence that the shape is right (Exercise (C1)). A plot of squared Pearson residuals against fitted means, and against group sizes where they vary, shows the same in more detail.

38.4.3. Choosing a repair

Four devices are in use, differing in what they assume and what they give.

device assumes gives does not give
quasi model with ϕ^ mean and variance shape 𝛃β^, F tests, model-based errors likelihood, AIC, predictions
quasi model with the sandwich mean model only 𝛃β^, Wald tests efficiency, small samples
NB2, NB1 or beta-binomial a full distribution likelihood, AIC, predictions robustness to that choice
random effect a full distribution and a mixing law conditional coefficients, prediction of units the marginal coefficients

Putting a dispersion parameter into a quasi model changes the standard errors and leaves 𝛃β^ exactly where it was (Theorem 38.2.2(a)); changing to a different likelihood with the same variance function can move it by more than a standard error (Example (Four analyses of the strike data)). The fourth device changes the estimand rather than the estimate: a coefficient conditional on a unit’s own random effect answers a different question from one describing a population average, a distinction Chapter 40 takes up and does not treat as small.

Example (Four analyses of the strike data)

Continuing Example (Strike durations) and Example (Four standard errors for the strike data), the production coefficient under four treatments of the extra variability is

fit coefficient standard error
Poisson, ϕ=1 −7.680 0.406
quasi-Poisson, V=ϕμ −7.680 2.564
NB1, Var=ϕμ −4.934 1.983
NB2, Var=μ+μ2/κ −9.327 2.931

The NB1 fit has ϕ^=42.78, close to the quasi-Poisson ϕ^P=39.89, and the same variance function; yet its coefficient differs by more than a standard error, because its estimating equation Eq. 37.4.6 is not Eq. 38.1.1 — the warning of Section 38.1 in numbers. The NB2 fit, with α^=1/κ^=0.954, gives −9.327 against the −9.353 of the quasi model with V(μ)=μ2: at these fitted means the quadratic term of the NB2 variance dominates, so the two nearly coincide.

The tests agree on the conclusion and not on its strength. The drop in deviance is 350.9, overwhelming as χ2(1); the quasi-F statistic Eq. 38.3.3 is 8.80 on (1,60) degrees of freedom, p=0.0043, and the score test for overdispersion Eq. 37.4.9 is 224.5. Unanticipated production predicts shorter strikes, at about the evidence a t statistic of three provides; the Poisson analysis overstates that by orders of magnitude.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats

data = sm.datasets.strikes.load_pandas().data
y = data["duration"].to_numpy(float)          # length of the strike, in days
x = data["iprod"].to_numpy(float)             # unanticipated industrial production
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), x])
n, p = X.shape

def quasi_irls(X, y, V, steps=60):
    """Solve the quasi-score equations for a log link and variance function V."""
    beta = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    beta[0] = np.log(y.mean())
    for _ in range(steps):
        eta = X @ beta
        mu = np.exp(eta)
        W = mu ** 2 / V(mu)                   # working weight (dmu/deta)^2 / V(mu)
        z = eta + (y - mu) / mu               # working response
        beta = np.linalg.solve((X.T * W) @ X, (X.T * W) @ z)
    return beta

beta_lin = quasi_irls(X, y, lambda m: m)              # V(mu) = mu
mu = np.exp(X @ beta_lin)
deviance = 2 * np.sum(y * np.log(y / mu) - (y - mu))          # quasi-deviance, V(mu) = mu
pearson = np.sum((y - mu) ** 2 / mu)                          # X^2 with V(mu) = mu
phi_pearson = pearson / (n - p)
beta_null = quasi_irls(X[:, :1], y, lambda m: m)
mu_null = np.exp(X[:, :1] @ beta_null)
deviance_null = 2 * np.sum(y * np.log(y / mu_null) - (y - mu_null))
naive_chisq = deviance_null - deviance                    # the Poisson likelihood ratio
quasi_f = (deviance_null - deviance) / phi_pearson        # referred to F(1, n - p)
print(f"naive chi-squared {naive_chisq:.1f}, p = {stats.chi2.sf(naive_chisq, 1):.2e}")
print(f"quasi F {quasi_f:.2f} on (1, {n - p}) df, p = {stats.f.sf(quasi_f, 1, n - p):.4f}")

Idea. The quasi-score does not know about the variance at all (Theorem 38.2.2(b)), so a wrong variance costs efficiency and calibration but not consistency. Whenever a colleague reports that “adding a dispersion parameter did not change the results”, the coefficients were being looked at and the standard errors were not.

38.4.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that for fixed ϕ0>1 the size Eq. 38.4.2 tends to 1 as q→∞. (Use χ2(q)/q→1 in probability, together with χ1−α2(q)/q→1, and compare with the event χ2(q)/q>χ1−α2(q)/(qϕ0).)

Solution. By the law of large numbers χ2(q)/q→1 in probability, and the same concentration gives χ1−α2(q)/q=cq→1. The event in Eq. 38.4.2 is {χ2(q)/q>cq/ϕ0}, whose threshold tends to ϕ0−1<1, so its probability tends to 1; at q=1 it is the finite Pr{χ2(1)>3.841/ϕ0}. The listing of this section shows the size increasing with q over the range tabulated, but that is a computation, not a proof.

Exercise (A2)

A Poisson regression of accident counts on three covariates gives X2/(n−p)=3.2. Give three distinct explanations, and for each one say what you would compute next.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In Proposition 38.4.1(c), let S be geometric on {1,2,…} with mean 1/θ. Compute VarY/EY and show that it equals (2−θ)/θ. For which θ does the compound Poisson have the variance-to-mean ratio of a negative binomial with κ=μ?

Exercise (B2)

Grouped binomial data have group sizes mi spread between 2 and 50. Show that under the constant-dispersion specification the Pearson terms mi(yi−μ^i)2/{μ^i(1−μ^i)} have expectation ϕ free of mi, while under Eq. 38.1.3 they have expectation 1+ρ(mi−1). Describe the plot this suggests and what each specification predicts for it.

Solution. Under Var(Yi)=ϕμi(1−μi)/mi the Pearson term at the true mean has expectation ϕ; under Eq. 38.1.3 the same calculation gives 1+ρ(mi−1), linear in mi with slope ρ. So plot squared Pearson residuals against mi with a smooth: flat supports a constant dispersion, increasing the exchangeable form.

Exercise (B3)

Both the NB1 model and the quasi-Poisson specification have Var(Yi)=ϕμi. Using Eq. 37.4.6 and Eq. 38.1.1, explain why a single very large count influences the two fits differently, and predict which of the two coefficients in Example (Four analyses of the strike data) would move more if the longest strike were deleted.

Solution. In the quasi-Poisson equation the log link cancels μi against V(μi), so observation i enters through the raw residual yi−μi: the longest strike, a count of 216 against a fitted mean of 58.99, contributes 157.0. The NB1 score Eq. 37.4.6 contains yi only inside ψ(yi+αμi), which grows like log⁡yi, so the NB1 fit is far less sensitive to it and the quasi-Poisson coefficient should move more when it is removed.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Proposition 38.4.1(a) says that unmodelled heterogeneity overdisperses. It says nothing about the shape of the excess, and the shape decides the repair. Suppose that, given an unrecorded Zi∼N(0,1) independent of the covariate, Yi is Poisson with mean Λi=exp⁡(β0+β1xi+γZi), γ≠0.

  • (a) Using Theorem 3.2.1, show that μi=E(Yi)=exp⁡(β0+γ2/2+β1xi): the marginal mean is still log-linear, with the same slope and a raised intercept. So the mean model is not at fault, and this is not case (d) of the proposition.

  • (b) Show that Var(Yi)=μi+(eγ2−1)μi2, so that Var(Yi)E(Yi)=1+(eγ2−1)μi, which increases with the mean instead of being constant.

  • (c) Which of the four devices in the table of this section is the right one, and what does the plot of squared Pearson residuals against μ^i look like under the true model and under the quasi-Poisson specification V(μ)=ϕμ?

  • (d) Show that β^1 from the quasi-Poisson fit is still consistent, and explain why no single ϕ^ makes the reported standard errors right across a design whose fitted means vary widely.

Solution.

  • (a) By Theorem 3.2.1, E(Yi)=EΛi=eβ0+β1xiEeγZi=eβ0+β1xieγ2/2. The slope of log⁡μi in xi is β1, unchanged; only the intercept moves, by γ2/2.

  • (b) By the proposition with the Poisson kernel, Var(Yi)=EΛi+Var(Λi). Now EΛi2=e2(β0+β1xi)Ee2γZi=e2(β0+β1xi)e2γ2, while μi2=e2(β0+β1xi)eγ2, so Var(Λi)=μi2(eγ2−1). Add μi and divide.

  • (c) The variance is μ+μ2/κ with 1/κ=eγ2−1, which is exactly the NB2 variance function: the negative binomial with a lognormal mixing law replaced by a gamma one has the same first two moments, and the third device in the table — a full distribution with that variance function — is the right choice, as is a quasi model with V(μ)=μ+μ2/κ. Under the true model the squared Pearson residuals (yi−μ^i)2/μ^i have expectation 1+(eγ2−1)μ^i, so a smooth through them rises linearly in μ^i; under the quasi-Poisson specification it should be flat at ϕ^. This is the diagnostic of the “Seeing it” list, and it is also what makes the sandwich covariance Eq. 38.3.2 and the model-based ϕ^P(X⊤W^X)−1 disagree, since the shape and not merely the scale of V is wrong.

  • (d) The quasi-score with a log link and V(μ)=μ is ∑ix(i)(yi−μi), whose mean is zero at the true 𝛃β because (a) says the mean model holds; consistency then follows from Theorem 38.2.2(b) and Theorem 38.3.1(a), which never use the variance. The Pearson dispersion converges to a weighted average of the ratios 1+(eγ2−1)μi, a number that is too small where the fitted means are large and too large where they are small; the intervals for observations at the ends of the design are wrong in opposite directions, and rescaling every variance by one constant cannot mend that. The idea box of this section cuts the other way here: the coefficients are safe and the standard errors are not, but the cure is a new variance function rather than a new number.

Exercise (C2)

Proposition 38.4.1(b) attributes the inflation 1+ρ(m−1) to clustering. When the individual units, and not their averages, are the data, a scalar dispersion parameter cannot represent it at all. Let the data be Yij, j=1,…,ni, i=1,…,m, with means μij, marginal variance σ2 throughout, exchangeable within-cluster correlation ρ∈(0,1) (Eq. 38.1.3) and independence across clusters, and let S=∑ijaijYij for constants aij.

  • (a) Show that Var(S)=σ2∑i{(1−ρ)∑jaij2+ρ(∑jaij)2}.

  • (b) Deduce that a cluster-level contrast, aij=ci for all j, has Var(S)=σ2∑ici2ni{1+ρ(ni−1)}: inflated by the design effect.

  • (c) Deduce that a within-cluster contrast, ∑jaij=0 for every i, has Var(S)=σ2(1−ρ)∑ijaij2: deflated, by the factor 1−ρ<1.

  • (d) Show that the Pearson dispersion ϕ^P converges to 1 here, so it registers no overdispersion at all, and that no single ϕ can correct both kinds of contrast even if one were supplied by hand. Which device in the table is then the right one?

Solution.

  • (a) Clusters are independent, so Var(S)=∑iai⊤Cov(Yi)ai with Cov(Yi)=σ2{(1−ρ)I+ρ11⊤}, and a⊤{(1−ρ)I+ρ11⊤}a=(1−ρ)‖a‖2+ρ(1⊤a)2.

  • (b) With aij=ci, ∑jaij2=ci2ni and (∑jaij)2=ci2ni2, so the bracket is ci2{(1−ρ)ni+ρni2}=ci2ni{1+ρ(ni−1)}.

  • (c) The second term vanishes for every i, leaving σ2(1−ρ)∑ijaij2, which is smaller than the independence value σ2∑ijaij2.

  • (d) The Pearson statistic uses only the marginal variances, which are correct by assumption: ϕ^P=∑ij(yij−μ^ij)2/{σ2(N−p)}→1 by the law of large numbers over clusters, whatever ρ is. Correlation is invisible to it, because it is a statement about pairs and the Pearson statistic looks at one observation at a time. Even a ϕ chosen with knowledge of ρ fails: the cluster-level contrast needs the factor 1+ρ(ni−1)>1 and the within-cluster contrast the factor 1−ρ<1, and a single multiplier cannot be on both sides of one. (With unequal ni even the cluster-level factors differ among themselves.) So the right device is the second or the fourth: a sandwich covariance with the cluster as the unit, which estimates Var(S) directly from the between-cluster variation, or an explicit model for the within-cluster covariance. The choice, and the different estimands the two carry, is the business of Chapter 40.

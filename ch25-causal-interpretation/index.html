Causal Interpretation of Coefficients

Earlier chapters treated a regression coefficient as a feature of a joint distribution. People who fit regressions usually want more: what would happen to the response if a regressor were changed, if a patient took the drug or a school hired more teachers. No amount of data on the undisturbed system answers that without further assumptions. This chapter states the assumptions and proves what they buy: potential outcomes and randomization, graphs and the back-door criterion for observational data, the bias from adjusting for mediators and colliders, and covariate adjustment in experiments, which can hurt unless the regression is interacted. This makes precise the warnings of Section 5.7, of the remark after the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem (Section 6.6) and of Section 18.1.

What you need. Chapter 5 (what a coefficient measures, Definition 5.7.1, and the omitted-variable formula, Proposition 5.7.2), Chapter 6 (the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem, Theorem 6.6.2, and projection in the population, Theorem 6.11.1 and Proposition 6.11.3), Chapter 3 and Chapter 14 (partial correlation, Proposition 3.6.1 and Proposition 14.5.2), Chapter 18 (analysis of covariance and randomization, Theorem 18.1.1, Proposition 18.1.2 and Lemma 18.4.3), and, from this part, Chapter 19 (omitted-variable bias of least squares, Theorem 19.1.2) and Chapter 21 (sandwich standard errors, Theorem 21.4.2).

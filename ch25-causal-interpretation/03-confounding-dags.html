25.3  Confounding and causal graphs

The functions and disturbances of a structural causal model are usually unknown; its parent sets, saying which variables can influence which, are qualitative and best displayed as a graph. This section shows how the graph constrains the joint distribution and identifies confounding as a property of paths. For linear models the connection is exact, and the omitted-variable bias of Proposition 5.7.2 becomes a sum over paths.

25.3.1. Graphs

Definition 25.3.1 (Directed acyclic graph)

A directed graph on nodes 1,…,m is a set of ordered pairs (l,j), l≠j, drawn as arrows l→j. It is acyclic (a DAG) if no sequence of arrows leads from a node back to itself. In a DAG:

  • (a) l is a parent of j, and j a child of l, if l→j;

  • (b) a path between l and j is a sequence of distinct nodes, starting at l and ending at j, in which consecutive nodes are joined by an arrow in either direction;

  • (c) a path is directed (from l to j) if every arrow on it points towards j; then l is an ancestor of j, and j a descendant of l;

  • (d) an interior node c of a path is a collider on the path if both arrows of the path that meet at c point into it, →c←; every other interior node is a non-collider on the path.

The graph of a structural causal model has an arrow l→j iff l∈pa(j). It is acyclic because parents have smaller indices.

Being a collider is a property of a node on a given path: in Figure 25.3.1(c), C is a collider on X→C←Y but not on a path that continues along an arrow C→D.

25.3.2. What the graph says about the distribution

Assume that for each j and each value v of the parents, the variable fj(v,Uj) has a density pj(⋅∣v) with respect to Lebesgue or counting measure.

Proposition 25.3.1 (Markov factorization and truncated factorization)

Under this assumption:

  • (a) (V1,…,Vm) has density

    p(v1,…,vm)=∏j=1mpj(vj∣vpa(j)),
    (25.3.1)

    and pj(⋅∣vpa(j)) is the conditional density of Vj given V1,…,Vj−1, which depends on them only through the parents;

  • (b) under do(Vk=x), the other variables have density ∏j≠kpj(vj∣vpa(j)), evaluated with vk=x.

Proof.

  • (a) The variables V1,…,Vj−1 are functions of U1,…,Uj−1, which are independent of Uj. For independent inputs, the conditional distribution of fj(Vpa(j),Uj) given (V1,…,Vj−1)=(v1,…,vj−1) is the distribution of fj(vpa(j),Uj) (Billingsley 1995), whose density is pj(⋅∣vpa(j)). The factorization follows by multiplying these conditional densities in the order j=1,…,m.
  • (b) The modified model is a structural causal model for the variables other than Vk, in which Vk is the constant x and every other assignment and disturbance is unchanged. Apply (a) to it.

So an intervention deletes one factor and keeps the others, which are observational conditional densities; with every variable observed, the interventional distribution is computable from the observational one.

The linear case has an explicit form. Write a linear model as V=c+BV+U, where B=(bjl) has bjl≠0 only if l∈pa(j), so B is strictly lower triangular, and Ω=Cov(U)=diag(ω1,…,ωm). The coefficient bjl labels the arrow l→j.

Proposition 25.3.2 (Path tracing)

In a linear structural causal model:

  • (a) I−B is invertible, V=A(c+U) with A=(I−B)−1, and Cov(V)=AΩA⊤;

  • (b) for j≠l, the entry ajl of A is the sum, over all directed paths from l to j, of the products of the coefficients on the arrows of the path (zero if there is none), and ajj=1;

  • (c) E(Vj∣do(Vl=x))=E(Vj∣do(Vl=0))+ajlx.

The number ajl is the total effect of Vl on Vj: the change in the interventional mean of Vj per unit change in the value imposed on Vl.

Proof.

  • (a) I−B is unit lower triangular, hence invertible, and (I−B)V=c+U. The covariance follows from Theorem 2.2.1.
  • (b) B is strictly lower triangular, so Bm=0 and (I−B)(I+B+⋯+Bm−1)=I−Bm=I. The (j,l) entry of Br is ∑bjk1bk1k2⋯bkr−1l over all k1,…,kr−1; a term is nonzero only if l→kr−1→⋯→k1→j are arrows, which is a directed path of length r (its nodes are distinct because the graph is acyclic). Summing over r gives (b).
  • (c) The intervened model is linear with B replaced by B~, in which row l is zero, and with cl+Ul replaced by x. A directed path out of l never uses an arrow into l, so the directed paths from l in the graph of B~ are those of B, and by (b) the (j,l) entry of (I−B~)−1 is ajl. By (a) applied to the intervened model, Vj is ajlx plus terms not involving x.

This is Wright’s method of path coefficients (Wright 1921, 1934); the covariance formula in (a) gives his rule for correlations (Exercise (C1)).

25.3.3. Chains, forks and colliders

Three configurations of three variables are the building blocks of everything the graph says about conditional independence (Figure 25.3.1).

Figure 25.3.1. The three structures of Proposition 25.3.3. In (a) and (b) the middle node transmits association between X and Y, and conditioning on it removes the association. In (c) the middle node transmits nothing until it is conditioned on.

Proposition 25.3.3 (Chains, forks and colliders)

Consider linear models with independent disturbances, VarUX=σX2>0 and so on, and write σXY⋅W for the partial covariance of X and Y given W (Definition 3.6.1).

  • (a) Chain X=UX, M=aX+UM, Y=bM+UY: Cov(X,Y)=abσX2 and σXY⋅M=0.

  • (b) Fork Z=UZ, X=aZ+UX, Y=bZ+UY: Cov(X,Y)=abσZ2 and σXY⋅Z=0.

  • (c) Collider X=UX, Y=UY, C=aX+bY+UC: Cov(X,Y)=0 and

    σXY⋅C=−abσX2σY2a2σX2+b2σY2+σC2.
    (25.3.2)

With normal disturbances, a zero partial covariance means conditional independence (Proposition 3.6.1), so in (a) and (b) X and Y are dependent but conditionally independent given the middle node, and in (c) they are independent but, if ab≠0, conditionally dependent given C.

Proof. The covariances follow from Proposition 25.3.2(a). (a) Y−bM=UY is uncorrelated with X and M, so L(Y∣M,X)=bM has no X term, and by Proposition 14.5.2 σXY⋅M=0. (b) The same argument with Y−bZ=UY. (c) By definition, σXY⋅C=Cov(X,Y)−Cov(X,C)Cov(Y,C)Var(C)=0−(aσX2)(bσY2)Var(C).

In the collider, independent causes become dependent once their common effect is held fixed. In the listing, with a=b=1 and unit variances, the correlations of X and Y in the chain and the fork are 0.577 and 0.500 (simulated: 0.582 and 0.505), and both partial correlations given the middle node are zero. In the collider the correlation is zero and the partial correlation given C is −0.500 (simulated −0.501).

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2503)
n = 100_000
a, b = 1.0, 1.0                                   # the two edge coefficients

def corr_and_partial(x, y, w):
    """Correlation of x and y, and their partial correlation given w (residuals on 1, w)."""
    W = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(w)), w])
    rx = x - W @ np.linalg.lstsq(W, x, rcond=None)[0]
    ry = y - W @ np.linalg.lstsq(W, y, rcond=None)[0]
    return np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1], np.corrcoef(rx, ry)[0, 1]

U = rng.normal(size=(3, n))
x = U[0]; m = a * x + U[1]; y = b * m + U[2]      # chain   X -> M -> Y
chain = corr_and_partial(x, y, m)
z = U[0]; x = a * z + U[1]; y = b * z + U[2]      # fork    X <- Z -> Y
fork = corr_and_partial(x, y, z)
x = U[0]; y = U[1]; c = a * x + b * y + U[2]      # collider X -> C <- Y
collider = corr_and_partial(x, y, c)
for name, (r, rp) in [("chain", chain), ("fork", fork), ("collider", collider)]:
    print(f"{name:9s} corr {r:6.3f}   partial corr given middle {rp:6.3f}")

25.3.4. d-separation

In a larger graph association can travel along many paths at once; d-separation combines the three structures.

Definition 25.3.2 (d-separation)

Let Z be a set of nodes. A path is blocked by Z if

  • (i) some non-collider on the path is in Z, or

  • (ii) some collider on the path is not in Z and has no descendant in Z.

Otherwise the path is open given Z. Disjoint sets of nodes A and B are d-separated by Z (disjoint from both) if every path between a node of A and a node of B is blocked by Z.

A path is open when each non-collider is left free and each collider is conditioned on, directly or through a descendant, a noisy measurement of it (Exercise (B1)).

Theorem 25.3.4 (d-separation and conditional independence)

Let V follow a structural causal model with graph G, and let A,B,Z be disjoint sets of nodes.

  • (a) If Z d-separates A and B in G, then VA and VB are conditionally independent given VZ.

  • (b) If Z does not d-separate them, there is a linear structural causal model on G with normal disturbances in which they are conditionally dependent; in fact this holds for all edge coefficients and disturbance variances outside a set of Lebesgue measure zero.

Proof. Sketch; full proofs are in the references. For (a) with densities (Lauritzen, Dawid, Larsen and Leimer 1990): the nodes of A∪B∪Z and their ancestors form a set N whose density, by Proposition 25.3.1, is a product of functions of the sets {j}∪pa(j), j∈N. These sets are complete in the moral graph on N, which joins each node to its parents and any two parents of a common child. If Z separates A from B there, the factors split into two groups, giving the conditional independence; a combinatorial lemma shows that d-separation in G is equivalent to this separation. Geiger, Verma and Pearl (1990) give another proof. For (b), partial correlations of a linear normal model are rational functions of the parameters, not identically zero along an open path, and so vanish only on a set of measure zero (Spirtes, Glymour and Scheines 2000).

Part (b) says the converse of (a) fails only by accident, as in Exercise (A1), where the effect of visits and the association through illness cancel exactly. Distributions with no such cancellation are called faithful to the graph.

25.3.5. Confounding as an open path

Definition 25.3.3 (Back-door paths and confounding)

A back-door path from X to Y is a path between them whose arrow at X points into X, X←⋯Y. The effect of X on Y is confounded if some back-door path from X to Y is open given the empty set, that is, contains no collider.

Directed paths from X to Y carry the effect of X; back-door paths carry association that reflects whatever caused X. In a linear model the split is exact.

Proposition 25.3.5 (Confounding bias in a linear model)

In a linear structural causal model with disturbances of positive variance, let X=Vk, Y=Vj, and let τ=ajk be the total effect of X on Y. For each l, let dl be the sum over the directed paths from Vl to Y that do not pass through X of the products of coefficients (so dk=0, and dj=1 from the trivial path of Y to itself).

  • (a) Y=τX+R, where R=∑ldl(cl+Ul) is the value of Y under do(X=0). Under do(X=x), Y=τx+R.

  • (b) The slope of the best linear predictor L(Y∣X) is

    τ+Cov(R,X)Var(X),Cov(R,X)=∑lωldlakl.
    (25.3.3)

  • (c) If the effect of X on Y is not confounded, the slope of L(Y∣X) is τ.

Proof.

  • (a) A directed path from Vl to Y either avoids X or passes through it exactly once, and a path of the second kind splits at X into a directed path from Vl to X and one from X to Y. Summing products over paths with Proposition 25.3.2(b), ajl=dl+aklτ for every l (for l=k, akk=1 and dk=0; for l=j, ajj=1=dj and akj=0). Hence Y=∑lajl(cl+Ul)=τ∑lakl(cl+Ul)+R=τX+R. Under do(X=x), as in the proof of Proposition 25.3.2(c), the arrows into X are removed, so the directed paths from Vl, l≠k, to Y in the intervened graph are exactly those that avoid X. Thus Y=τx+∑l≠kdl(cl+Ul)=τx+R.

  • (b) The slope is Cov(X,Y)/Var(X)=τ+Cov(R,X)/Var(X) by Theorem 6.11.1, and Cov(R,X)=∑l,l′dlakl′Cov(Ul,Ul′)=∑lωldlakl, since the disturbances are uncorrelated.

  • (c) Suppose ωldlakl≠0 for some l. Then l≠k, and there are a directed path π1 from Vl to X and a directed path π2 from Vl to Y that avoids X. Let w be the node of π1 closest to X that also lies on π2; it exists because Vl lies on both, and w≠X because π2 avoids X. Follow π1 backwards from X to w, then π2 forwards from w to Y. By the choice of w the nodes are distinct, so this is a path. Its arrow at X is the last arrow of π1, which points into X, and every arrow on it points away from w, so it has no collider. The effect is therefore confounded. Contrapositively, if it is not confounded, every term of Cov(R,X) vanishes.

Part (a) is the potential outcome Y(x)=τx+Y(0): linear structural models have constant effects. Part (b) writes the regression slope as the effect plus one contribution for each common ancestor of X and Y. In the model of Figure 25.1.1, R=cY+γZ+UY and the only term comes from Z: ωda=σZ2⋅γ⋅α, which is Eq. 25.1.3.

This is the omitted-variable bias of least squares: by Proposition 5.7.2 the short regression estimates θ+πγ, with π the sample slope of z on x, whose limit is ασZ2/Var(X) (see also Theorem 19.1.2 in Chapter 19). The graph adds that an omitted variable causes bias only if it lies on an open back-door path; an omitted mediator should stay omitted (Section 25.5).

25.3.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

A DAG has arrows A→X, A→B, B→Y, X→Y, X→C, Y→C and D→C. List every path between X and Y, mark the back-door paths, and say which paths are open given ∅, given {B} and given {C}.

Solution. There are three paths: X→Y; X←A→B→Y, a back-door path; and X→C←Y, with collider C. (The arrow D→C gives no path to Y except through C, where D→C←Y would require revisiting C.) Given ∅: the first two are open and the third is blocked by the collider. Given {B}: only X→Y is open. Given {C}: all three are open, since conditioning on the collider opens the third.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Extend the collider by D=C+UD, with VarUD=δ and all other variances one. Compute σXY⋅D and show that it lies between 0 and σXY⋅C, approaching them as δ→∞ and δ→0.

Solution. Cov(X,D)=a, Cov(Y,D)=b and VarD=a2+b2+1+δ, so σXY⋅D=−ab/(a2+b2+1+δ). At δ=0 this is Eq. 25.3.2, and it decreases in absolute value to zero as δ grows. A proxy for a collider opens the path partially, the more so the better the proxy.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show from Proposition 25.3.2(a) that in a linear model Cov(Vj,Vk)=∑lωlajlakl. Interpret each term as a sum over pairs of directed paths from Vl to Vj and to Vk, and use this to compute Cov(X,Y) in the chain of Proposition 25.3.3.

25.4  Adjustment sets and the back-door criterion

If confounding is association along back-door paths, the remedy is to block them by conditioning on variables on them, that is, by including those variables as regressors. This section says which sets do the job, proves that they do, and compares their precision.

25.4.1. The criterion

Definition 25.4.1 (Back-door criterion)

Let X and Y be nodes of a DAG. A set Z of nodes, containing neither X nor Y, satisfies the back-door criterion relative to (X,Y) if

  • (i) no node in Z is a descendant of X, and

  • (ii) Z blocks every back-door path from X to Y (Definition 25.3.3).

Such a set is called a (back-door) adjustment set.

Condition (ii) closes the non-causal routes. Condition (i) protects the causal ones: a descendant of X may be a mediator, whose conditioning removes part of the effect, or a collider, whose conditioning opens a path (Section 25.5). The empty set qualifies exactly when the effect is not confounded, and the parents of X always qualify, provided Y is not one of them, since every back-door path begins X←P with P a parent and a non-collider. The graph itself is an assumption that the data cannot check (Exercise (B3)); the criterion turns it into a precise statement about which regression to run.

Recall from Section 25.2 that Y(x) is the value of Y in the model modified by do(X=x), and that Y=Y(x) on the event X=x. Conditional independence of Y(x) and X given Z is the potential-outcome form of “no confounding given Z”, which Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983), adding positivity, called strong ignorability.

Theorem 25.4.1 (Back-door adjustment)

Let V follow a structural causal model with graph G, let X and Y be two of its variables, and let Z be the vector of the variables in a set Z of other nodes.

  • (a) Adjustment formula. Suppose X takes finitely many values, Pr(X=x∣Z)>0 with probability one, E|Y(x)|<∞, and Y(x) is conditionally independent of X given Z. Then

    E(Y∣do(X=x))=E[E(Y∣X=x,Z)],
    (25.4.1)

    where the outer expectation is over the distribution of Z.

  • (b) The criterion gives ignorability. If Z satisfies the back-door criterion relative to (X,Y), then Y(x) is conditionally independent of X given Z, for every x.

  • (c) Linear models. In a linear structural causal model whose disturbances have finite positive variances, if Z satisfies the back-door criterion, the coefficient of X in L(Y∣X,Z) is the total effect τ of X on Y. Consequently, for independent copies of V, the least squares coefficient of X in the regression of Y on 1, X and Z converges to τ with probability one.

The proofs of (b) and (c) below are complete when Z contains every parent of X; for general adjustment sets they are sketches, since the proof of (c) uses (b).

Proof.

  • (a) Using the tower rule, then the conditional independence together with positivity, then Y=Y(x) on {X=x}, EY(x)=E[E(Y(x)∣Z)]=E[E(Y(x)∣X=x,Z)]=E[E(Y∣X=x,Z)], and EY(x)=E(Y∣do(X=x)) by definition.

  • (b) When Z contains the parents of X. Let X=Vk, and write U−k for the disturbances other than Uk. A node that is not a descendant of X has no directed path from X, so solving the assignments shows that it is a function of U−k alone; by (i), so is Z. In the model modified by do(X=x), the disturbance Uk appears nowhere, so Y(x) is also a function of U−k. Hence Uk is independent of (Y(x),Z), and given Z it remains independent of Y(x), with its unconditional distribution. Finally X=fk(Zpa,Uk), where Zpa is the part of Z formed by the parents of X. Given Z, X is a function of Uk alone, and so is conditionally independent of Y(x).

    General adjustment sets (sketch). Form a graph containing both the original variables and their counterparts in the model modified by do(X=x), joined through shared disturbance nodes (a twin network); Y(x) is a node of it, and X has no arrows into the counterpart variables. A path from X to Y(x) that leaves X through a child of X must turn back somewhere, at the latest at a disturbance node shared with a counterpart variable, so it meets a collider that is a descendant of X; by (i) neither that collider nor its descendants are in Z, and the path is blocked. A path that leaves X through a parent starts like a back-door path, and one checks, using (ii) and again (i), that Z blocks it. So Z d-separates X from Y(x), and Theorem 25.3.4(a) gives the conditional independence. Pearl (1995, 2009) gives the criterion and its proof in the language of interventions, and Shpitser, VanderWeele and Robins (2010) characterize exactly which sets satisfy Eq. 25.4.1 for every model on G.

  • (c) By Proposition 25.3.5(a), Y=τX+R, where R is the value of Y under do(X=0), that is, R=Y(0). The variables R, X and Z are linear functions of the disturbances with coefficients determined by B, so their covariance matrix depends only on B and Ω. The covariance matrix of (X,Z) is positive definite, because Cov(V)=AΩA⊤ with A invertible and Ω positive definite. By Proposition 14.5.2 the coefficient of X in L(Y∣X,Z) is σYX⋅ZσXX⋅Z=τ+σRX⋅ZσXX⋅Z, with σXX⋅Z>0. Consider the linear model with the same B and Ω but normal disturbances. It has the same covariance matrix of (R,X,Z), hence the same partial covariance σRX⋅Z. In it (R,X,Z) is jointly normal and, by (b), R=Y(0) is conditionally independent of X given Z, so σRX⋅Z=0 by Proposition 3.6.1(b). Therefore the coefficient is τ. (This step is complete when Z contains the parents of X, and otherwise inherits the sketch of (b).) The last statement is Proposition 6.11.3.

Part (a) averages the conditional mean at X=x over the population distribution of Z, not over its distribution among units with X=x (direct standardization), and needs positivity: where X=x never occurs, the data say nothing about E(Y∣X=x,Z). Part (c) answers the question left open after the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem: the multiple-regression coefficient of X in a linear model is the effect of changing X whenever the other regressors form an adjustment set.

Warning. Only the coefficient of X acquires a causal meaning. The coefficients of the adjustment variables are generally not their effects, because X may mediate them; reporting them as effects is the “Table 2 fallacy” (Westreich and Greenland 2013), computed in Exercise (B2).

25.4.2. An example with eight variables

Example (Choosing an adjustment set)

Figure 25.4.1 shows a linear model with unit-variance disturbances and W2=0.8W1+U,X=0.7W1+0.6W3+0.9I+U,M=0.8X+U,Y=0.5X+0.6M+0.7W2+0.8W3+P+U, with a separate disturbance U in each equation and W1,W3,I,P parentless. Summing over the two directed paths, τ=0.5+0.8×0.6=0.98. The back-door paths X←W1→W2→Y and X←W3→Y are blocked by W1 or W2, and by W3, and M is a descendant of X. So a set is valid iff it contains W3 and one of W1,W2, and not M; I and P are optional.

The listing computes the coefficient of X in L(Y∣X,Z) exactly from Cov(V)=AA⊤, with the factor σY⋅XZ/σX⋅Z that governs precision (see below), and the standard deviation over 4000 simulated samples of size n=500:

adjustment set valid coefficient of X σY⋅XZ/σX⋅Z simulated sd
none no 1.308 1.15 0.052
W3 no 1.150 1.16 0.052
W1,W3 yes 0.980 1.25 0.056
W2,W3 yes 0.980 1.06 0.047
W2,W3,P yes 0.980 0.80 0.036
W1,W3,I yes 0.980 1.69 0.076
W1,W3,M no 0.500 1.72 0.077
W1,W2,W3,I,P yes 0.980 1.17 0.052

Every valid set gives τ; the invalid sets leave a back-door path open or, with M, keep only the direct effect 0.5. Among valid sets the standard deviation varies by a factor of two (Figure 25.4.2).

Figure 25.4.1. The graph of Example (Choosing an adjustment set). Blue arrows form the directed paths from X to Y; brown arrows form the two back-door paths. I influences only X, and P only Y.

import numpy as np

names = ["W1", "W3", "I", "P", "W2", "X", "M", "Y"]      # a topological order
k = {v: j for j, v in enumerate(names)}
B = np.zeros((8, 8))                                      # B[j, i]: coefficient of i in j's equation
for child, parent, coef in [("W2", "W1", 0.8), ("X", "W1", 0.7), ("X", "W3", 0.6), ("X", "I", 0.9),
                            ("M", "X", 0.8), ("Y", "X", 0.5), ("Y", "M", 0.6), ("Y", "W2", 0.7),
                            ("Y", "W3", 0.8), ("Y", "P", 1.0)]:
    B[k[child], k[parent]] = coef
A = np.linalg.inv(np.eye(8) - B)                          # V = A U
Sigma = A @ A.T                                           # unit disturbance variances
tau = A[k["Y"], k["X"]]                                   # total effect: sum over directed paths

def population_fit(Z):
    """Coefficient of X in L(Y | X, Z) and the large-sample sd factor sigma_{Y.XZ}/sigma_{X.Z}."""
    R = [k["X"]] + [k[z] for z in Z]
    coef = np.linalg.solve(Sigma[np.ix_(R, R)], Sigma[R, k["Y"]])
    res_y = Sigma[k["Y"], k["Y"]] - Sigma[k["Y"], R] @ coef
    Zi = [k[z] for z in Z]
    res_x = Sigma[k["X"], k["X"]] - (Sigma[k["X"], Zi] @ np.linalg.solve(Sigma[np.ix_(Zi, Zi)], Sigma[Zi, k["X"]])
                                     if Z else 0.0)
    return coef[0], np.sqrt(res_y / res_x)

sets = [[], ["W3"], ["W1", "W3"], ["W2", "W3"], ["W2", "W3", "P"], ["W1", "W3", "I"],
        ["W1", "W3", "M"], ["W1", "W2", "W3", "I", "P"]]
print(f"total effect of X on Y: {tau:.3f}")
for Z in sets:
    c, f = population_fit(Z)
    print(f"Z = {{{', '.join(Z)}}}".ljust(26) + f"coefficient {c:6.3f}   sd factor {f:5.3f}")

25.4.3. Which adjustment set is most precise

All valid sets estimate τ, so the choice among them is a question of variance. With normal disturbances, Y given (X,Z) follows a linear model with variance σY⋅XZ2, and given the regressors the coefficient of X has variance σY⋅XZ2/‖(I−MZ)x‖2, where MZ projects onto the span of 1 and the columns of Z (Theorem 6.6.2 and Eq. 6.6.2). Divided by n, the squared length in the denominator is the residual mean square of the regression of X on Z, which converges to σX⋅Z2 with probability one (Proposition 6.11.3). So n times the variance tends to

v(Z)=σY⋅XZ2σX⋅Z2,
(25.4.2)

the square of the factor in the table. A good adjustment set explains much of Y and little of X.

Proposition 25.4.2 (Adding a variable to an adjustment set)

Let Z and Z∪{W} both satisfy the back-door criterion, with all covariance matrices positive definite.

  • (a) If σYW⋅XZ=0, then v(Z∪{W})≥v(Z).

  • (b) If σXW⋅Z=0, then v(Z∪{W})≤v(Z).

Proof. Partial variances are the mean squared errors of best linear predictors, and projecting onto a larger space can only reduce them (Theorem 2.5.2(c)). (a) By Proposition 14.5.2 applied with (X,Z) as conditioning variables, σYW⋅XZ=0 means that W has coefficient zero in L(Y∣X,Z,W), so this predictor equals L(Y∣X,Z) and the numerator of Eq. 25.4.2 is unchanged; the denominator can only decrease. (b) Likewise σXW⋅Z=0 means L(X∣Z,W)=L(X∣Z), so the denominator is unchanged, while the numerator can only decrease.

In Example (Choosing an adjustment set), a direct computation from Cov(V)=AA⊤ gives σYI⋅XW1W3=0 and σXP⋅W2W3=0: the pure cause I of X satisfies (a) and the pure cause P of Y satisfies (b). Accordingly, adding I to {W1,W3} raises the factor from 1.25 to 1.69; adding P to {W2,W3} lowers it from 1.06 to 0.80. Replacing W1 by W2, closer to Y on the back-door path, also helps. Henckel, Perković and Maathuis (2022) turn these observations into a graphical rule for the valid set of smallest asymptotic variance in linear models: roughly, the parents of Y and of the mediators, other than the mediators and X.

If every adjustment set contains an unrecorded variable, as in Example (Clinic visits and health), other structure must be used: an instrumental variable (Theorem 24.4.2 in Chapter 24), or the front-door criterion of Pearl (1995), which uses an observed mediator; or a sensitivity analysis asks how strong an unobserved confounder would have to be, using formulas such as Eq. 25.3.3.

25.4.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In Example (Choosing an adjustment set), decide which of {W1,W2}, {W3,P} and {W2,W3,M} satisfy the back-door criterion, and explain the coefficients of X that the listing gives for them: 1.201, 1.150 and 0.500.

Solution. None does. {W1,W2} leaves X←W3→Y open, and {W3,P} leaves X←W1→W2→Y open, so both coefficients exceed τ=0.98. The set {W2,W3,M} blocks both back-door paths but contains the mediator M, violating (i); holding M fixed leaves only the direct arrow, with coefficient 0.5.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let X∈{0,1} and let Z take values 1,…,K. In a sample, stratum z has nz units, a fraction p^z∈(0,1) of them with X=1, and difference of mean responses δ^z=y¯1z−y¯0z. Show that the sample version of Eq. 25.4.1 for the effect E(Y∣do(X=1))−E(Y∣do(X=0)) is ∑z(nz/n)δ^z, while the coefficient of X in the least squares regression of Y on X and the K stratum indicators is β^X=∑znzp^z(1−p^z)δ^z∑znzp^z(1−p^z). When do they agree?

Solution. Replacing E(Y∣X=x,Z=z) by y¯xz and the distribution of Z by the sample fractions nz/n gives the first expression. For the second, apply Theorem 6.6.2 with X2 the stratum indicators. Projecting onto their span replaces each value by its stratum mean (Example (The one-way layout)), so x~i=xi−p^z(i). Then β^X=∑ix~iyi/∑ix~i2. Within stratum z, ∑ix~i2=nzp^z(1−p^z) and ∑ix~iyi=nzp^z(1−p^z)(y¯1z−y¯0z), since the treated contribute (1−p^z)nzp^zy¯1z and the untreated −p^znz(1−p^z)y¯0z. The two agree when the δ^z are all equal or when p^z is the same in every stratum. Otherwise the regression weights strata by the variance of X within them, not by their size, and estimates a weighted average effect that is not the average effect (Angrist 1998).

Exercise (B2)

In Example (Choosing an adjustment set), show that the coefficient of W3 in L(Y∣X,W1,W3) is 0.80, the coefficient of the arrow W3→Y, while the total effect of W3 on Y is 1.388. Which set of variables would one adjust for to estimate the effect of W3?

Solution. Write Y=0.5X+0.6(0.8X+UM)+0.7(0.8W1+UW2)+0.8W3+P+UY=0.98X+0.56W1+0.8W3+E, where E=0.6UM+0.7UW2+P+UY is uncorrelated with X, W1 and W3 (none of these depends on the disturbances in E). So L(Y∣X,W1,W3)=0.98X+0.56W1+0.8W3. The total effect of W3 is the sum over its directed paths to Y: 0.8 directly plus 0.6×0.98 through X, which is 1.388. The coefficient in the regression misses the part that goes through X, because X is held fixed. W3 has no parents and no back-door paths, so the empty set is an adjustment set for its effect: the simple regression of Y on W3 estimates 1.388.

Exercise (B3)

Suppose X is randomized, so it has no parents, and P is a pre-treatment cause of Y. Show that both ∅ and {P} are adjustment sets and that v({P})=(1−ρYP⋅X2)v(∅), where ρYP⋅X is the partial correlation of Y and P given X. Compare with Proposition 18.1.2.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Positivity is not a technicality. Let Z be 1 or 0 with probability 12 each; let X be 1 or 0 with probability 12 each when Z=0, and X=0 whenever Z=1; and let Y=τX+λZ+ψXZ+UY, with UY of mean zero and independent of (X,Z). The graph is Z→X, Z→Y, X→Y, so {Z} satisfies the back-door criterion.

  • (a) Show that the average effect E(Y∣do(X=1))−E(Y∣do(X=0)) is τ+ψ/2.

  • (b) Show that the right-hand side of Eq. 25.4.1 is not defined, because the positivity condition of Theorem 25.4.1(a) fails at z=1.

  • (c) Show that the joint distribution of (X,Z,Y) does not involve ψ at all, and that the least squares regression of Y on 1, X and Z nevertheless returns the coefficient τ exactly, in the population and in every sample in which the three covariate patterns occur.

What, then, is the regression’s answer an answer to?

Solution.

  • (a) Under do(X=x) the assignment for Z is unchanged, so E(Y∣do(X=x))=τx+λEZ+ψxEZ=τx+(λ+ψx)/2, and the difference at x=1 and x=0 is τ+ψ/2.

  • (b) Pr(X=1∣Z=1)=0, so E(Y∣X=1,Z=1) is a conditional expectation on a null event: the distribution of the data says nothing about it, and the outer expectation in Eq. 25.4.1 averages a quantity that is undefined on half of the population.

  • (c) The event {X=1,Z=1} has probability zero, so XZ=0 with probability one and Y=τX+λZ+UY almost surely: no feature of the distribution moves when ψ changes, and ψ is not identified. The covariate vector (1,X,Z) takes only the three values (1,0,0), (1,1,0) and (1,0,1), which are linearly independent, so the linear model is saturated: the best linear predictor reproduces E(Y∣X,Z) at each of the three patterns, giving β0=0, β0+β1=τ and β0+β2=λ. Hence β1=τ, and in a sample the normal equations have the same unique solution in terms of the three cell means.

So the regression reports τ, which is the effect of X among the units with Z=0, and equals the average effect only under the assumption ψ=0 — an assumption the data cannot examine, because the stratum in which X=1 and Z=1 is empty. The number comes from the functional form, by extrapolation, and not from the observations. A linear model is at its most dangerous where the design is thin, since it never declines to answer.

Exercise (C2)

When every adjustment set contains an unrecorded variable, an observed mediator can still identify the effect. Let U be unrecorded and put X=aU+UX,M=δX+UM,Y=γM+bU+UY, with U,UX,UM,UY independent of mean zero and variances σU2,σX2,σM2,σY2, all positive, and a≠0, b≠0.

  • (a) Show that neither ∅ nor {M} satisfies the back-door criterion relative to (X,Y), so Theorem 25.4.1 identifies nothing from the recorded variables (X,M,Y).

  • (b) Show that the slope of L(M∣X) is exactly δ.

  • (c) Show that the coefficient of M in L(Y∣X,M) is exactly γ, while the coefficient of X there is bκ with κ=aσU2/(a2σU2+σX2), and so is not the direct effect 0.

  • (d) Conclude that the product of the two estimable slopes is the total effect τ=γδ. This is the linear case of Pearl’s front-door criterion.

  • (e) Show that the construction is fragile: if M=δX+gU+UM with g≠0, the two slopes become δ+gκ and γ+bλ with λ=gσU2/(g2σU2+σM2), whose product is not γδ.

Solution.

  • (a) The path X←U→Y is a back-door path with U a non-collider, so the empty set fails (ii); and M is a descendant of X, so {M} fails (i). Since U is unrecorded, there is no candidate set left.

  • (b) Cov(M,X)=δVar(X)+Cov(UM,X)=δVar(X), because UM is independent of X, so Theorem 6.11.1 gives the slope Cov(M,X)/Var(X)=δ.

  • (c) Since UY is uncorrelated with (X,M), L(Y∣X,M)=γM+bL(U∣X,M). Now M=δX+UM, so the span of 1,X,M is the span of 1,X,UM, and UM is uncorrelated with X; by the orthogonality of the two pieces, L(U∣X,M)=L(U∣X)+L(U∣UM)=κX+0, with κ=Cov(U,X)/Var(X)=aσU2/(a2σU2+σX2). So L(Y∣X,M)=γM+bκX: the coefficient of M is exactly γ, and the coefficient of X is bκ≠0 although the arrow X→Y is absent. Conditioning on the mediator has left the confounding in the coefficient of X and removed it from the coefficient of M — because every back-door path from M to Y runs through X, which is held fixed.

  • (d) The total effect of X on Y is the product over the one directed path, τ=γδ, and by (b) and (c) both factors are coefficients of regressions among recorded variables, so each is estimated consistently by least squares (Proposition 6.11.3).

  • (e) Replace UM by E=gU+UM, which is still uncorrelated with X, and repeat the two computations. For (b), Cov(M,X)=δVar(X)+gaσU2, so the slope is δ+gκ. For (c), L(U∣X,M)=L(U∣X)+L(U∣E)=κX+λE with λ=gσU2/(g2σU2+σM2), and E=M−δX, so the coefficient of M is γ+bλ. The product (δ+gκ)(γ+bλ) differs from γδ by terms of first order in g. The front-door criterion buys identification from an assumption — that nothing unrecorded acts on the mediator — that is no more checkable than the one it replaces.

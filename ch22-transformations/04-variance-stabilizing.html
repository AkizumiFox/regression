22.4  Variance-stabilizing transformations

For many responses the variance is tied to the mean by the nature of the measurement: counts, proportions, amounts measured with a fixed relative precision. Then Var(Y)=ϕv(μ) for a known or plausible variance function v, where μ=E(Y) and ϕ is a constant. The transformation that makes the variance constant turns out to be determined by v up to an affine change.

22.4.1. The idea

If g is smooth and Y is concentrated near μ, then g(Y)≈g(μ)+g′(μ)(Y−μ), so Varg(Y)≈g′(μ)2ϕv(μ). This is constant in μ exactly when g′(μ) is proportional to v(μ)−1/2, that is, when

g(μ)=∫μdtv(t).
(22.4.1)

Part (a) of the theorem below makes this precise, with m measuring the information; it is Exercise (B1), where Fisher’s z arose this way. Part (b) adds uniqueness.

Theorem 22.4.1 (Variance-stabilizing transformations)

Let I be an open interval and v:I→(0,∞) continuous. Suppose that for every θ∈I the random variables Tm satisfy m(Tm−θ)→N(0,v(θ)) in distribution as m→∞. Fix t0∈I and let g(t)=∫t0tv(s)−1/2ds for t∈I, extended in any way outside I.

  • (a) For every θ∈I, m{g(Tm)−g(θ)}→N(0,1) in distribution.

  • (b) If h:I→R is continuously differentiable and m{h(Tm)−h(θ)}→N(0,1) for every θ∈I, then h=g+c or h=−g+c on I for a constant c.

Proof.

  • (a) By the fundamental theorem of calculus g′=v−1/2 on I, so this is Exercise (B1) with s=v1/2; the values of g outside I do not matter because Tm→θ in probability.
  • (b) By the delta method (stated in Section 14.4), m{h(Tm)−h(θ)}→N(0,h′(θ)2v(θ)). Limits in distribution are unique, so h′(θ)2v(θ)=1 and |h′(θ)|=v(θ)−1/2 for every θ∈I. The continuous function h′ never vanishes on the interval I, so by the intermediate value theorem it has one sign throughout. Hence h′=g′ or h′=−g′ on I, and integrating gives the result.

Convergence in distribution does not by itself imply that the variance of g(Tm) is close to 1/m; that needs uniform integrability, which the theorem does not assert (Exercise (C2) supplies it for Poisson counts).

Corollary 22.4.2 (The classical transformations)

  • (a) Counts. If Y∼Poisson(μ), then 2(Y−μ)→N(0,1) as μ→∞.

  • (b) Proportions. If S∼Binomial(m,p) with 0<p<1 fixed and p^=S/m, then 2m{arcsin⁡p^−arcsin⁡p}→N(0,1) as m→∞.

  • (c) Constant coefficient of variation. If Y=μU for a positive random variable U whose distribution does not depend on μ, then Var(log⁡Y)=Var(log⁡U) exactly, for every μ, whenever it is finite.

  • (d) Power variance functions. If v(μ)=μ2a on (0,∞), then Eq. 22.4.1 gives μ1−a/(1−a) for a≠1 and log⁡μ for a=1. Up to an affine change this is the Box–Cox transformation with λ=1−a.

Proof.

  • (a) The characteristic function of (Y−μ)/μ is exp⁡{μ(eit/μ−1−it/μ)}, which tends to e−t2/2 because eiu−1−iu=−u2/2+O(u3). By the continuity theorem of Lévy (van der Vaart 1998, chapter 2), (Y−μ)/μ→N(0,1). Now write 2(Y−μ)=Y−μμ⋅2μY+μ. Since Var(Y/μ)=1/μ, Chebyshev’s inequality gives Y/μ→1 in probability, so the second factor tends to 1 in probability, and Slutsky’s lemma gives the limit.
  • (b) By the central limit theorem m(p^−p)→N(0,p(1−p)). With v(p)=p(1−p) on (0,1), the derivative of arcsin⁡p is 12{p(1−p)}−1/2, so Eq. 22.4.1 gives g(p)=2arcsin⁡p, and Theorem 22.4.1(a) applies.
  • (c) log⁡Y=log⁡μ+log⁡U, and adding a constant does not change a variance.
  • (d) Integrate t−a.

Part (c) is exact: for gamma data with shape k, Var(log⁡Y) is the trigamma function at k whatever the mean, 0.2213 for k=5 against the first-order value 1/k=0.2. Part (d) is the rule quoted in Section 22.1. A known variance function determines the power; otherwise the Box–Cox likelihood estimates one, chosen partly to stabilize the variance.

22.4.2. How well they work

The limits say nothing about moderate means, but the variances can be computed exactly by summing probability mass functions. Figure 22.4.1 and the table show them, scaled so that the target is 1.

Poisson mean μ 1 2 5 10 20
4VarY 1.609 1.560 1.144 1.045 1.020
4VarY+3/8 0.717 0.924 1.002 1.001 1.000

Without transformation the variance of a Poisson count grows in proportion to the mean, a factor of 20 between μ=1 and μ=20. After the square root the scaled variance stays between 1 and 1.7 over the same range, and from μ=5 on it is within 15 percent of the target. Anscombe’s (1948) modification Y+3/8 does better still for every mean above about 0.67. The reason is a second-order effect. A formal expansion gives VarY+c=14+(3−8c)/(32μ)+O(μ−2) (Exercise (C1)), and c=3/8 removes the 1/μ term. For binomial proportions from m=20 trials, 4mVar(arcsin⁡p^) is 1.056 at p=0.5, 1.118 at p=0.25, 1.546 at p=0.1 and 1.632 at p=0.05. Anscombe’s version arcsin⁡(S+3/8)/(m+3/4) gives 0.977, 0.979, 0.919 and 0.720. Both transformations fail near the boundary, where most of the probability sits on a few values and no smooth function can equalize anything.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats


def poisson_var(h, mu):
    """Var h(Y) for Y ~ Poisson(mu), by summing the probability mass function."""
    k = np.arange(0, int(mu + 40 * np.sqrt(mu) + 60))
    p = stats.poisson.pmf(k, mu)
    m1 = p @ h(k)
    return p @ (h(k) - m1) ** 2


def binomial_var(h, m, prob):
    """Var h(S) for S ~ Binomial(m, prob)."""
    s = np.arange(m + 1)
    p = stats.binom.pmf(s, m, prob)
    m1 = p @ h(s)
    return p @ (h(s) - m1) ** 2


for mu in (1, 2, 5, 10, 20):
    print(f"Poisson mean {mu:2d}:  4 Var sqrt(Y) = {4 * poisson_var(np.sqrt, mu):.3f}"
          f"   4 Var sqrt(Y + 3/8) = {4 * poisson_var(lambda k: np.sqrt(k + 3 / 8), mu):.3f}")
m = 20
for prob in (0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5):
    plain = 4 * m * binomial_var(lambda s: np.arcsin(np.sqrt(s / m)), m, prob)
    anscombe = 4 * m * binomial_var(lambda s: np.arcsin(np.sqrt((s + 3 / 8) / (m + 3 / 4))), m, prob)
    print(f"binomial m = {m}, p = {prob:.2f}:  4m Var = {plain:.3f}   with 3/8: {anscombe:.3f}")

A stabilized scale also gives a check: for Poisson counts the residual variance on the square-root scale should be near 14. A much larger value says the counts are overdispersed, and then the square root no longer stabilizes (Exercise (B1)).

22.4.3. Estimating the variance function

With replicates at several settings, plot the log standard deviation of each group against the log of its mean. If s∝μa the points lie near a line of slope a, and the power 1−a stabilizes the variance (Exercise (B3)), an idea that goes back to Bartlett (1947). Without replicates, residuals can be grouped by fitted value, but misfit of the mean then inflates some spreads, so the plot should come after the mean function is roughly right. Whether the variance depends on the regressors at all is tested formally by Theorem 21.2.2.

22.4.4. Transform, weight, or model the variance

Transformation is one of three remedies for a variance that depends on the mean.

  1. Transform. Fit the linear model to g(y). The variance becomes nearly constant, but the mean is now modelled on the new scale, and statements about y need the retransformation of Section 22.5.

  2. Weight. Keep the scale and fit by weighted least squares with weights 1/v(μ^i) (Theorem 21.3.1 and Definition 6.9.1), or keep ordinary least squares with heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors (Theorem 21.4.2).

  3. Model. Specify the mean through a link function and the variance through v: a generalized linear model, such as Poisson or logistic regression, whose coefficients refer to the mean of y itself. Chapter 34 develops this modern default.

The first two can be combined. The logit of a binomial proportion is not variance-stabilizing, but its variance is about 1/{mp(1−p)}, so weighted least squares on empirical logits with weights mp^(1−p^) gives a linear model for the log odds (Exercise (B2)), the approach of Grizzle, Starmer and Koch (1969) for categorical data.

22.4.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Find the variance-stabilizing transformation for v(μ)=μ3, the variance function of the inverse Gaussian distribution. Which Box–Cox power is it?

Exercise (A2)

Let Tm be the mean of m independent exponential waiting times with mean θ>0. Find the variance-stabilizing transformation. Use Theorem 22.4.1(b) to show that the square root, although it stabilizes Poisson counts, does not stabilize Tm, and find the limiting variance of m(Tm−θ).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Negative binomial counts have v(μ)=μ+μ2/κ for a shape parameter κ>0. Show that Eq. 22.4.1 gives g(μ)=2κsinh−1⁡μ/κ. Show that g behaves like 2μ for μ≪κ and like κlog⁡μ plus a constant for μ≫κ, and interpret.

Solution. Substitute t=κu2: dt=2κudu and t+t2/κ=κu2(1+u2), so the integrand becomes 2κudu/{κu1+u2}=2κdu/1+u2, whose integral is 2κsinh−1⁡u with u=μ/κ. For small u, sinh−1⁡u≈u, giving 2μ. For large u, sinh−1⁡u=log⁡(2u)+o(1), giving κlog⁡μ plus a constant. Small counts behave like Poisson counts and need a square root; large counts have a nearly constant coefficient of variation and need a logarithm.

Exercise (B2)

Let S∼Binomial(m,p) with 0<p<1. Show by the delta method that m{logit(S/m)−logitp}→N(0,1/{p(1−p)}), where logitp=log⁡{p/(1−p)}. For independent groups i=1,…,k with Si∼Binomial(mi,pi) and 𝛃logitpi=x(i)⊤β, write down the weighted least squares estimate of 𝛃β from the empirical logits, and its approximate covariance matrix.

Solution. The derivative of logitp is 1/{p(1−p)}, and m(S/m−p)→N(0,p(1−p)), so the limiting variance is p(1−p)/{p(1−p)}2. With ℓi=logit(Si/mi) and W=diag{mip^i(1−p^i)}, the estimate is 𝛃β^=(X⊤WX)−1X⊤Wℓ (Definition 6.9.1), with approximate covariance (X⊤WX)−1, since the weights are the approximate inverse variances and no σ2 needs estimating. The empirical logit is infinite when Si=0 or mi, which is why 12 is often added to Si and to mi−Si.

Exercise (B3)

Replicate observations at four settings have means 10, 40, 160, 640 and standard deviations 2, 5.7, 16, 45. Fit the spread–level line log⁡s=c+alog⁡μ described above by eye or by least squares, and use Corollary 22.4.2(d) to choose the power.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Let Y∼Poisson(μ) and c≥0. Expand Y+c=μ(1+u)1/2 with u=(Y−μ+c)/μ to third order in u, use the Poisson central moments E(Y−μ)2=μ, E(Y−μ)3=μ, E(Y−μ)4=3μ2+μ, and show formally that VarY+c=14+(3−8c)/(32μ)+O(μ−2). (The expansion is formal: making the remainder rigorous needs a bound on the tail of Y.)

Exercise (C2)

Corollary 22.4.2(a) is a statement about distributions. Show that the variance converges too: 4VarY→1 as μ→∞ for Y∼Poisson(μ). Use the bound (Y−μ)2≤(Y−μ)2/μ, and the fact that a family of nonnegative random variables that converges in distribution, and whose means converge to the mean of the limit, is uniformly integrable.

Solution. The bound holds because Y−μ=(Y−μ)/(Y+μ) and Y+μ≥μ. The variables Vμ=(Y−μ)2/μ converge in distribution to χ2(1) (by the proof of Corollary 22.4.2(a)) and have mean exactly 1, the mean of the limit, so they are uniformly integrable, and so is the dominated family Wμ=4(Y−μ)2. Since Wμ→χ2(1) in distribution, uniform integrability gives EWμ→1. The same argument applied to 2(Y−μ), which is dominated by 1+Wμ, gives E{2(Y−μ)}→0. Hence 4VarY=EWμ−[E{2(Y−μ)}]2→1.

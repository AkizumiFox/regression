22.3  Transforming regressors

Transforming a regressor changes only the mean function. The errors are untouched, and there is no Jacobian, because the data being explained stay the same. What is at stake is bias. If 𝛃E(Y)=X2β2+β1g(x1) and the analyst fits x1 itself, the missing part β1{g(x1)−x1} acts as an omitted regressor (Proposition 5.5.7(a) and Theorem 19.1.2). It shows up as curvature in the residuals, and tests against a larger model detect it (Proposition 14.7.1 and Theorem 14.7.2). This section finds the right g, first by looking, then by estimating a power.

22.3.1. Component-plus-residual plots

The component-plus-residual plot of Section 20.1 plots the partial residuals 𝛆ε^+β^1x1 against x1. Its least squares line has slope β^1 and residuals 𝛆ε^, as in the added-variable plot, and that section warns that it can mislead when x1 is related nonlinearly to the other regressors. The next result measures how far.

Proposition 22.3.1 (What the plot shows)

Let X=[x1,X2] have full column rank, with 1∈C(X2), and let 𝛃β^ and 𝛆ε^ be the coefficients and residuals of the least squares fit of y on X. Let M and M2 project onto C(X) and C(X2), and x~1=(I−M2)x1.

  • (a) If 𝛃E(Y)=g+X2β2, where g has entries g(xi1) for some function g, then

    𝛆E(ε^+β^1x1)=g−M2(g−bx1),b=E(β^1)=x~1⊤g‖x~1‖2.
    (22.3.1)

  • (b) In particular, if C(X2) is spanned by 1 alone, the expected partial residuals are g shifted by a constant, so the plot shows g exactly apart from its level.

Proof.

  • (a) Since 𝛃X2β2∈C(X), 𝛆𝛃E(ε^)=(I−M)(g+X2β2)=(I−M)g. By Theorem 6.6.2(a), β^1=x~1⊤Y/‖x~1‖2, whose mean is x~1⊤g/‖x~1‖2=b because x~1⊥C(X2). By Lemma 6.6.1(c), M=M2+x~1x~1⊤/‖x~1‖2, so (I−M)g=g−M2g−bx~1. Adding bx1=bx~1+bM2x1 gives g−M2g+bM2x1, which is Eq. 22.3.1.
  • (b) If C(X2)=span{1}, then M2v=v¯1 for every v.

The distortion M2(g−bx1) is the part of the nonlinearity g−bx1 that the other regressors reproduce linearly. Correlation with x1 is not in itself harmful; a regressor that resembles the curvature is. Cook (1993) showed for random regressors that the plot recovers g, up to a linear term, when the conditional mean of the other regressors given x1 is linear in x1, and proposed CERES plots for other cases. The augmented partial residual plot of Mallows (1986) adds x12 to the fit and plots 𝛆ε^+β^1x1+β^11x12. Its expectation is g minus the part of g−b1x1−b11x12 that the other regressors reproduce, small when g is nearly quadratic (Exercise (B3)).

Example (When the plot misleads)

The script simulates 120 cases with E(Y)=3log⁡x1+x2, x1 uniform on [1,10], and measures how much of the curvature of g the expected plot keeps (the coefficient of the nonlinear part of g in that of Eq. 22.3.1; 1 is faithful). In panel (a) of Figure 22.3.1, x2 is linear in x1 plus noise, with correlation 0.95, and the plot keeps 0.99. In panel (b), x2 is a noisy square root of x1, with correlation 0.99; it resembles the logarithm and takes over much of the curvature, and the plot keeps only 0.45, its slope even changing sign. The augmented plot, panel (c), restores 0.88.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2204)
n = 120
x1 = rng.uniform(1, 10, n)
g = 3 * np.log(x1)                                        # the true shape in x1
standardize = lambda v: (v - v.mean()) / v.std()
x2_lin = standardize(x1) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n)          # linear in x1, plus noise
x2_sqrt = standardize(np.sqrt(x1)) + rng.normal(0, 0.1, n)  # nonlinear in x1, plus noise


def lstsq(X, v):
    coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, v, rcond=None)
    return coef


def partial_residuals(y, x2, augmented=False):
    """e + b1 x1 (+ b11 x1^2 if augmented), from the fit of y on 1, x1 (, x1^2), x2."""
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), x1] + ([x1**2] if augmented else []) + [x2])
    coef = lstsq(X, y)
    e = y - X @ coef
    return e + coef[1] * x1 + (coef[2] * x1**2 if augmented else 0)


def curvature_retained(v):
    """Coefficient of the nonlinear part of g in the nonlinear part of v (1 = shape kept)."""
    Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1])
    nonlin = lambda u: u - Z @ lstsq(Z, u)
    return nonlin(v) @ nonlin(g) / (nonlin(g) @ nonlin(g))


noise = rng.normal(0, 0.6, n)
results = {}
for name, x2, aug in [("linear", x2_lin, False), ("square root", x2_sqrt, False),
                      ("square root, augmented", x2_sqrt, True)]:
    mean = g + x2                                         # E(Y); partial residuals are linear in y
    results[name] = (partial_residuals(mean + noise, x2, aug), partial_residuals(mean, x2, aug))
    print(f"{name:23s} corr(x1, x2) {np.corrcoef(x1, x2)[0, 1]:.2f}   curvature retained "
          f"{curvature_retained(results[name][1]):.2f}")

22.3.2. Power transformations: Box and Tidwell

When the plot suggests a power, it can be estimated. Box and Tidwell (1962) considered the mean function

𝛃E(Y)=X2β2+β1x(α),x(α)=(x1(α),…,xn(α))⊤,
(22.3.2)

for a positive regressor x, with 1∈C(X2) and Eq. 22.2.1 applied to the regressor. For fixed α this is a linear model, and α^ minimizes the residual sum of squares SSE(α) of the fit of y on [X2,x(α)]. Under normal errors it is the maximum likelihood estimate, and {α:nlog⁡{SSE(α)/SSE(α^)}≤χ1,0.052} is an asymptotic 95% interval under the conditions of Wilks’s theorem, which include β1≠0 (Exercise (C1)). The derivative of x(α) with respect to α at α0≠0 is xα0log⁡x/α0−x(α0)/α0, and at α0=0 it is 12(log⁡x)2 (Exercise (B1)). Since x(α0) and 1 are already in the model, the part that matters is the constructed variable

w={(xiα0log⁡xi/α0)i=1n,α0≠0,(12(log⁡xi)2)i=1n,α0=0.
(22.3.3)

For α0=1 it is xlog⁡x.

Theorem 22.3.2 (Box–Tidwell)

Let X0=[X2,x(α0)] have full column rank p, with 1∈C(X2), let w be the constructed variable Eq. 22.3.3, and suppose [X0,w] has full column rank p+1<n. Let β^1 be the coefficient of x(α0) and 𝛆ε^0 the residual vector in the least squares fit of y on X0, and let γ^ be the coefficient of w in the fit of y on [X0,w].

  • (a) Exact test. If 𝛃Y∼Nn(X0β,σ2I), which is Eq. 22.3.2 with α=α0, then the t statistic of γ^ has the t(n−p−1) distribution.

  • (b) The step. If β^1≠0, one Gauss–Newton step for minimizing 𝛃‖y−X2β2−β1x(α)‖2, started from the least squares coefficients at α0, moves α to

    α1=α0+γ^/β^1.
    (22.3.4)

  • (c) Stationarity. SSE(α) is differentiable at α0 with 𝛆SSE′(α0)=−2β^1ε^0⊤w=−2β^1γ^‖(I−M0)w‖2, where M0 projects onto C(X0). So if β^1≠0, then γ^=0 iff α0 is a stationary point of SSE, and every fixed point of the iteration Eq. 22.3.4 is a stationary point.

Proof. Write d=∂x(α)/∂α at α0. By the derivative computed above, d−w is a multiple of x(α0), so d−w∈C(X0) and (I−M0)d=(I−M0)w=:w~, which is nonzero because [X0,w] has full rank. By Theorem 6.6.2(a), γ^=w~⊤y/‖w~‖2, and 𝛆w~⊤y=w⊤(I−M0)y=w⊤ε^0.

  • (a) The column w depends on x alone, not on Y. The t test for γ^ is therefore the F test of C(X0) against the fixed larger space C([X0,w]), with t2=F and a symmetric null distribution. By Proposition 14.7.1(a), F∼F(1,n−p−1) when E(Y)∈C(X0), so t∼t(n−p−1).

  • (b) The mean function 𝛍𝛃𝛃μ(β2,β1,α)=X2β2+β1x(α) has derivative matrix J=[X2,x(α0),β^1d] at the starting point, where the residual is 𝛆ε^0. The Gauss–Newton step is the least squares coefficient vector of 𝛆ε^0 on J, and its last entry is the change in α. By Theorem 6.6.2(a) that entry is 𝛆𝛆{(I−M0)β^1d}⊤ε^0‖(I−M0)β^1d‖2=1β^1w~⊤ε^0‖w~‖2=γ^β^1, using 𝛆w~⊤ε^0=w~⊤y, which holds because w~⊥C(X0).

  • (c) Let X(α)=[X2,x(α)], which has full column rank near α0, and let 𝛃β^(α) be its least squares coefficients, a differentiable function of α. Then 𝛃SSE(α)=‖y−X(α)β^(α)‖2, and 𝛆𝛃𝛆𝛆SSE′(α0)=−2ε^0⊤{β^1d+X0β^′(α0)}=−2β^1ε^0⊤d=−2β^1ε^0⊤w, because 𝛆ε^0⊥C(X0) and d−w∈C(X0). Finally 𝛆ε^0⊤w=γ^‖w~‖2 from the first paragraph.

Part (a) is exact because this constructed variable is built from the fixed regressor, not from the response as in Proposition 22.2.4. Part (b) identifies the Box–Tidwell iteration with Gauss–Newton, fast near a well-determined minimum and liable to oscillate otherwise, so in one dimension it is safer to minimize SSE(α) directly; by part (c) the two agree at convergence. Several powers can be stepped at once (Exercise (C2)).

Example (Transforming income)

With log⁡(food) as the response and income(α) as the regressor, starting from α0=1, the Box–Tidwell iteration gives −0.053 after one step and −0.182 after two, where it stays. The direct minimization agrees: α^=−0.182, with 95% likelihood ratio interval [−0.334, −0.036]. The constructed-variable t statistic is −16.58 at α0=1, so untransformed income is decisively rejected, and −2.47 at α0=0, beyond the critical value 1.97, so the logarithm is rejected at the 5% level as well. As a check on part (a), 4000 data sets simulated from the log–log fit give a rejection rate of 0.047 for this test.

The rejection rests largely on one household. The richest, with income 4958 and food expenditure 1827, sits far to the right in panel (c) of Figure 22.1.1, below the line. Without it, α^=−0.119, and the likelihood ratio statistic for α=0 falls from 5.98 to 1.77. A negative power says food expenditure flattens among the richest households, where the data are few; Cook’s distance (Definition 20.4.1) pursues this.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

df = sm.datasets.engel.load_pandas().data
income, food = df["income"].to_numpy(), df["foodexp"].to_numpy()
n = len(food)
y = np.log(food)


def bc(x, a):
    """Box-Cox transform (x^a - 1)/a, with log x at a = 0."""
    return np.log(x) if abs(a) < 1e-12 else np.expm1(a * np.log(x)) / a


def fit(y, x, a):
    return sm.OLS(y, np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), bc(x, a)])).fit()


def box_tidwell_step(y, x, a0):
    """One Box-Tidwell (Gauss-Newton) step: a1 = a0 + gamma/beta1."""
    beta1 = fit(y, x, a0).params[1]                    # slope of x^(a0) in the null fit
    w = x**a0 * np.log(x) / a0 if a0 != 0 else np.log(x) ** 2 / 2   # d bc(x, a)/da, up to C(X0)
    aug = sm.OLS(y, np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), bc(x, a0), w])).fit()
    return a0 + aug.params[2] / beta1, aug.tvalues[2]


a, path = 1.0, [1.0]
for _ in range(8):
    a, t = box_tidwell_step(y, income, a)
    path.append(a)
print("Box-Tidwell iterates from alpha = 1:", " ".join(f"{v:.4f}" for v in path))

22.3.3. Choosing both scales together

The best power for the response depends on the scale of the regressor, and conversely, so the two can be estimated together. With response z(λ) and model matrix [1,x(α)] the proof of Theorem 22.2.2 goes through unchanged, since the Jacobian involves only y: the joint profile log-likelihood is −n2log⁡SSEz(λ,α) plus a constant.

Example (Two powers for Engel’s data)

Figure 22.3.2 shows the joint profile. The maximum is at (λ^,α^)=(−0.25, −0.39). The raw scale (1,1) has likelihood ratio statistic 186.5, and the best pair with untransformed income, (0.63,1) from Example (Box–Cox for Engel’s data), has 167.8: whatever is done to the response, income needs transforming. The log–log pair (0,0) has statistic 11.2, outside the 95% region, whose cut-off is 5.99 for two parameters. Without the richest household the statistic falls to 6.55, still outside.

The region is a long tilted ellipse, since a lower response power can be partly compensated by a lower income power (Exercise (B2)). The log–log model lies outside the region (11.2 against 5.99), largely because of the richest household. It is still the natural working choice for interpretation, since its slope is an elasticity, with the caveat that the data reject a constant elasticity and that it may fall among the richest households (Proposition 22.5.3).

22.3.4. Polynomials and other alternatives

Powers suit positive regressors with monotone, smoothly bending effects. Otherwise the alternative is to add columns rather than change one: a polynomial or a spline. These buy flexibility with interpretability, since no single coefficient measures the effect of x (Definition 5.7.1, third qualification), and high-degree polynomials behave badly at the edges of the data. Royston and Altman’s (1994) fractional polynomials use one or two terms from a small set of powers including the logarithm. Chapter 42 treats polynomial and spline regression.

22.3.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

At α0=1 a fit gives β^1=2.5, and adding xlog⁡x gives it the coefficient γ^=−1.5. What is the next Box–Tidwell value of α? What does the sign of γ^ say about the curvature of the relation?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that ∂x(α)/∂α=xαlog⁡x/α−x(α)/α for α≠0, and that its limit as α→0 is 12(log⁡x)2. Conclude that in a model containing 1 and x(α0) the constructed variable may be taken to be Eq. 22.3.3.

Solution. Differentiate (xα−1)/α: the quotient rule gives {αxαlog⁡x−(xα−1)}/α2=xαlog⁡x/α−x(α)/α. As α→0, expand xα=1+αlog⁡x+12α2(log⁡x)2+O(α3): the numerator is αlog⁡x+α2(log⁡x)2−αlog⁡x−12α2(log⁡x)2+O(α3)=12α2(log⁡x)2+O(α3), and dividing by α2 gives the limit. The term −x(α0)/α0 lies in the model space, and removing it changes neither the residualized column w~ nor γ^.

Exercise (B2)

Suppose y=cxβ exactly, with c,β>0. Show that for λ≠0, y(λ) is an affine function of x(α) iff α=βλ, and that for λ=0 it is affine in x(α) iff α=0. Explain why data close to a power law give a joint likelihood for (λ,α) with a ridge, and why the slope of the ridge need not be exactly β when the variance also depends on λ.

Solution. For λ≠0, yλ=cλxβλ, so y(λ)=(cλxβλ−1)/λ, an affine function of xβλ. An affine function of xβλ is affine in xα (equivalently in x(α)) for all x>0 iff α=βλ, since distinct powers, and a power and the logarithm, are affinely independent functions on (0,∞). For λ=0, log⁡y=log⁡c+βlog⁡x is affine in x(α) iff α=0, which is again α=βλ. So every pair on the line α=βλ makes the mean exactly linear, and nearby data make the residual sum of squares small along it. The likelihood also rewards a constant variance and normal errors, which depend on λ alone, so the maximum lies somewhere along the tilted valley, and its exact direction mixes the two requirements.

Exercise (B3)

Let X=[x1,x12,X2], with 1∈C(X2) and full column rank, and let 𝛃E(Y)=g+X2β2. Show that the augmented partial residuals 𝛆ε^+β^1x1+β^11x12 have expectation g−M2(g−b1x1−b11x12), where (b1,b11) are the expectations of (β^1,β^11). Conclude that the plot is exact, up to a vertical shift, when g is a quadratic.

Solution. Let Q=[x1,x12] and Q~=(I−M2)Q. As in the proof of Proposition 22.3.1, M=M2+Q~(Q~⊤Q~)−1Q~⊤ and the coefficient vector b of Q has expectation (Q~⊤Q~)−1Q~⊤g. Then 𝛆E(ε^)=(I−M)g=g−M2g−Q~b, and adding Qb=Q~b+M2Qb gives g−M2(g−Qb). If g=c01+c1x1+c2x12, then g lies in C(X), b=(c1,c2)⊤, and g−Qb=c01, whose projection is c01.

Exercise (B4)

In the setting of Proposition 22.3.1, let X2=[1,q] with qi=xi12, so the second regressor is an exact quadratic in the first, and write 𝛃β2=(β20,δ)⊤.

  • (a) If g(x)=cx2, show that b=0 and that the expected partial residuals are 0: the plot is flat, although E(Y) curves in x1 when δ≠−c.

  • (b) If g(x)=cx, show that the plot is exact.

  • (c) Explain why no plot against x1 can tell which of the two regressors carries the curvature in (a).

Solution.

  • (a) Here g=cq∈C(X2), so x~1⊤g=0 and b=0; then M2(g−bx1)=M2g=g and Eq. 22.3.1 is 0. The whole of g is the nonlinearity g−bx1, and q reproduces all of it. The mean E(Y)=β20+(c+δ)x12 curves unless c+δ=0.
  • (b) Now b=cx~1⊤x1/‖x~1‖2=c, because x~1⊤x1=‖x~1‖2, so g−bx1=0 and the expected partial residuals are g.
  • (c) The models E(Y)=β20+cx12+δx12 with the same c+δ have the same mean for every case: the split between g and 𝛃X2β2 is not identified from the data, so no display of them can recover it.

22.2  The Box–Cox family

Choosing a rung of the ladder by eye works when the evidence is strong, but it gives no measure of uncertainty and no way to compare rungs that fit almost equally well. Box and Cox (1964) turned the choice into a parameter: embed the powers in a family indexed by a real number λ, assume that for some λ the normal linear model holds on the transformed scale, and estimate λ by maximum likelihood along with everything else.

22.2.1. The family

Definition 22.2.1 (The Box–Cox family)

For y>0 and λ∈R, the Box–Cox transformation of y is

y(λ)={yλ−1λ,λ≠0,log⁡y,λ=0.
(22.2.1)

For a sample y1,…,yn>0 with geometric mean y˙=exp⁡(n−1∑ilog⁡yi), the normalized transformation is zi(λ)=yi(λ)/y˙λ−1, and z(λ)=(z1(λ),…,zn(λ))⊤.

In a model with an intercept y(λ) and yλ give the same fit. Subtracting 1 and dividing by λ make the family continuous in λ, with the logarithm in its place, and increasing for every λ.

Proposition 22.2.1 (Properties of the family)

Let y>0.

  • (a) y(λ)→log⁡y as λ→0, so λ↦y(λ) is continuous.

  • (b) For each λ, y↦y(λ) is strictly increasing on (0,∞), with derivative yλ−1. The normalized transformation has derivative (y/y˙)λ−1, which equals 1 at y=y˙ for every λ.

  • (c) If p<q, then y(q)=φ(y(p)) for a strictly increasing and strictly convex function φ. Equivalently, moving down the ladder from q to p applies a strictly concave increasing function.

  • (d) As y ranges over (0,∞), y(λ) ranges over (−1/λ,∞) if λ>0, over (−∞,−1/λ) if λ<0, and over R if λ=0.

Proof.

  • (a) yλ=exp⁡(λlog⁡y)=1+λlog⁡y+O(λ2), so (yλ−1)/λ=log⁡y+O(λ).
  • (b) For λ≠0 the derivative of (yλ−1)/λ is yλ−1>0, and for λ=0 the derivative of log⁡y is y−1. Dividing by the constant y˙λ−1 gives the second statement.
  • (c) Let u=y(p). By (b) the map y↦u has an increasing inverse y=ψ(u) with ψ′(u)=y1−p. Then φ=(⋅)(q)∘ψ has derivative φ′(u)=yq−1y1−p=ψ(u)q−p. Since q−p>0 and ψ is strictly increasing, φ′ is positive and strictly increasing, so φ is strictly increasing and strictly convex. The inverse of a strictly increasing convex function is strictly increasing and concave.
  • (d) yλ takes every value in (0,∞). For λ>0 subtracting 1 and dividing by λ gives (−1/λ,∞), and for λ<0 the division reverses the interval to (−∞,−1/λ).

Part (c) is the ladder’s advice made precise: a plot of y(q) that bends upwards is a convex function of y(p), and a lower power removes some of the bend. Part (d) is a warning. For λ≠0 the transformed response is bounded on one side, so it cannot be exactly normal. The Box–Cox model is always an approximation, harmless when the bound is many standard deviations from the data.

22.2.2. The likelihood

The working model is that for some λ the Yi(λ) are independent with 𝛃Yi(λ)∼N(x(i)⊤β,σ2), ignoring the small normal probability beyond the bound of Proposition 22.2.1(d). The likelihood must be written for the observed yi, since otherwise changing λ changes the data being explained. That is where the Jacobian enters, and the geometric mean with it.

Theorem 22.2.2 (Profile likelihood of the Box–Cox parameter)

Let X have rank r<n, let y1,…,yn>0, and for each λ let SSEz(λ)=‖(I−M)z(λ)‖2, assumed positive. Under the model above:

  • (a) the log-likelihood of 𝛃(β,σ2,λ) is

    𝛃𝛃ℓ(β,σ2,λ)=−n2log⁡(2πσ2)−‖y(λ)−Xβ‖22σ2+(λ−1)∑i=1nlog⁡yi;
    (22.2.2)

  • (b) for fixed λ it is maximized by the least squares fit of y(λ) on X, and the profile log-likelihood of λ is

    ℓp(λ)=−n2{log⁡(2πSSEz(λ)n)+1},
    (22.2.3)

    so the maximum likelihood estimate λ^ is the value that minimizes SSEz(λ);

  • (c) the likelihood ratio statistic for H:λ=λ0 is 2{ℓp(λ^)−ℓp(λ0)}=nlog⁡{SSEz(λ0)/SSEz(λ^)}, and the likelihood ratio confidence set of level 1−α is

    {λ0: nlog⁡SSEz(λ0)SSEz(λ^)≤χ1,α2},
    (22.2.4)

    where χ1,α2 is the upper α point of χ2(1).

Proof.

  • (a) The map y↦y(λ) is strictly increasing and differentiable with derivative yλ−1 (Proposition 22.2.1(b)). By the change-of-variables formula the density of Yi at yi is the 𝛃N(x(i)⊤β,σ2) density at yi(λ) times yiλ−1. The Yi are independent, so the log-likelihood is the sum of the logs, which is Eq. 22.2.2.
  • (b) The Jacobian term does not involve 𝛃(β,σ2), and the other two terms are the log-likelihood of the normal linear model with response y(λ). By Theorem 7.3.1 its maximum over 𝛃(β,σ2) is attained at any least squares fit and equals −n2{log⁡(2πSSE(y(λ))/n)+1}. Now y(λ)=y˙λ−1z(λ), so SSE(y(λ))=y˙2(λ−1)SSEz(λ), and −n2log⁡y˙2(λ−1)=−n(λ−1)log⁡y˙=−(λ−1)∑i=1nlog⁡yi. This cancels the Jacobian term exactly and leaves Eq. 22.2.3, which is a decreasing function of SSEz(λ).
  • (c) Subtract two values of Eq. 22.2.3.

The theorem is algebraic. The coverage of Eq. 22.2.4 is a separate, asymptotic matter: if the model holds at λ0 and the regularity conditions of Wilks’s theorem are satisfied, the probability that the set contains λ0 tends to 1−α (van der Vaart 1998, chapter 16). That result is not proved here, and by Proposition 22.2.1(d) its hypothesis can hold exactly only at λ0=0. Otherwise, as Hernandez and Johnson (1980) showed, λ^ converges to the value whose normal model is closest in Kullback–Leibler divergence to the true distribution, and the interval describes the precision of that best approximation.

Remark (Why normalize). Residual sums of squares of y(λ) are in the units of yλ and cannot be compared across λ. The geometric mean builds the Jacobian into the response: every z(λ) is in the units of y, with unit slope at y˙ (Proposition 22.2.1(b)), so sz(λ)={SSEz(λ)/(n−r)}1/2 can be compared across λ, and minimizing it is maximizing the likelihood.

The normalization also makes the analysis independent of the units in which y is recorded.

Proposition 22.2.3 (Units of the response)

Suppose 1∈C(X). If every yi is multiplied by the same c>0, then SSEz(λ) is multiplied by c2 for every λ. Consequently ℓp changes by a constant, and λ^, the likelihood ratio statistics and the set Eq. 22.2.4 are unchanged.

Proof. The geometric mean of cy1,…,cyn is cy˙. For λ≠0, (cyi)λ−1λ(cy˙)λ−1=cyiλ−1λy˙λ−1+c1−c−λλy˙λ−1, and for λ=0, cy˙log⁡(cyi)=cy˙log⁡yi+cy˙log⁡c. In both cases the new normalized vector is cz(λ)+k1 for a constant k. Since (I−M)1=0, its residual vector is c(I−M)z(λ), and SSEz is multiplied by c2. By Eq. 22.2.3, ℓp changes by −nlog⁡c, a constant.

Example (Box–Cox for Engel’s data)

First let transformed food expenditure be linear in income. The profile log-likelihood, panel (a) of Figure 22.2.1, peaks at λ^=0.63, and the 95% interval given by Eq. 22.2.4 is [0.45, 0.80]. Both the raw scale and the logarithm are rejected: the likelihood ratio statistics are 18.8 for λ=1 and 37.2 for λ=0, against the critical value 3.84.

Now let food expenditure be linear in log income. The profile, panel (b), peaks at λ^=0.025, with interval [−0.117, 0.162]. The logarithm is well inside, with statistic 0.12, while the square root (46.6) and the raw scale (181.4) are firmly rejected. With log income as the regressor, the normalized residual standard deviation is 78.6 on the log scale and 115.6 on the raw scale, both in the currency of the survey (the geometric mean of food expenditure is 574.4).

The answers differ because a response transformation is chosen to make a particular mean function linear. Section 22.3 estimates the two powers together.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import optimize, stats

df = sm.datasets.engel.load_pandas().data
income, food = df["income"].to_numpy(), df["foodexp"].to_numpy()
n = len(food)


def box_cox_z(y, lam):
    """Box-Cox transform normalized by the geometric mean, so every lambda is in the units of y."""
    gm = np.exp(np.mean(np.log(y)))
    if abs(lam) < 1e-12:
        return gm * np.log(y)
    return np.expm1(lam * np.log(y)) / (lam * gm ** (lam - 1))   # (y^lam - 1)/(lam gm^(lam-1))


def sse(v, X):
    coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, v, rcond=None)
    r = v - X @ coef
    return r @ r


def profile(lam, y, X):
    """Profile log-likelihood of lambda, up to an additive constant."""
    return -0.5 * len(y) * np.log(sse(box_cox_z(y, lam), X))


def box_cox_fit(y, X, level=0.95):
    """MLE of lambda and the likelihood-ratio interval {lambda: 2(l_max - l_p) <= chi2_1}."""
    lam_hat = optimize.minimize_scalar(lambda l: -profile(l, y, X), bounds=(-3, 3), method="bounded",
                                       options={"xatol": 1e-10}).x
    l_max = profile(lam_hat, y, X)
    cut = stats.chi2.ppf(level, 1) / 2
    g = lambda l: l_max - profile(l, y, X) - cut
    lo = optimize.brentq(g, lam_hat - 3, lam_hat)
    hi = optimize.brentq(g, lam_hat, lam_hat + 3)
    return lam_hat, (lo, hi), l_max


X_lin = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), income])            # food linear in income
X_log = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), np.log(income)])    # food linear in log income
for name, X in [("income", X_lin), ("log income", X_log)]:
    lam_hat, (lo, hi), l_max = box_cox_fit(food, X)
    lr = {lam: 2 * (l_max - profile(lam, food, X)) for lam in (0, 0.5, 1)}
    print(f"{name:10s} lambda_hat {lam_hat:.3f}  95% interval [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]  LR at 0, 1/2, 1:",
          " ".join(f"{v:.1f}" for v in lr.values()))

22.2.3. Choosing a convenient value

Nobody thinks in units of food expenditure raised to the power 0.025. The usual practice is to pick a simple value inside the interval, here λ=0, and to analyse on that scale as if it had been chosen in advance. Simple values have interpretations: the logarithm gives percentages and elasticities, the reciprocal turns a time into a rate.

Treating the chosen scale as known ignores that the data chose it. Bickel and Doksum (1981) showed that when λ is estimated, the unconditional variance of 𝛃β^ can be many times its fixed-λ value. Box and Cox (1982) replied that the unconditional variance of a coefficient whose units change with λ has no scientific meaning, and Hinkley and Runger (1984) argued for inference conditional on the selected scale. Quantities whose meaning does not depend on λ, such as a prediction of y or the hypothesis that a regressor has no effect at all (Exercise (C1)), avoid the difficulty, and resampling (Chapter 23) can include the choice of scale in their uncertainty.

22.2.4. The constructed variable

A single extra regression gives a quick check of a proposed λ0 and shows which observations drive the evidence. Let

w(λ0)=∂z(λ)∂λ|λ=λ0,
(22.2.5)

computed entry by entry. Differentiating (yλ−1)y˙1−λ/λ at λ0=1 gives wi=yi{log⁡(yi/y˙)−1}+1+log⁡y˙, and at λ0=0 it gives wi=12y˙{log⁡(yi/y˙)}2−12y˙(log⁡y˙)2 (Exercise (B1)). In a model with an intercept the additive constants do not matter, so the constructed variables are yi{log⁡(yi/y˙)−1} for checking the raw scale and 12y˙{log⁡(yi/y˙)}2 for checking the logarithm.

Proposition 22.2.4 (A constructed variable for the response power)

Let 𝛆ε^0=(I−M)z(λ0) and w=w(λ0), with w∉C(X).

  • (a) 𝛆ddλSSEz(λ)|λ0=2ε^0⊤w, and so 𝛆ℓp′(λ0)=−nε^0⊤w/SSEz(λ0).

  • (b) In the least squares fit of z(λ0) on [X,w], the coefficient of w is 𝛆γ^=ε^0⊤w/‖(I−M)w‖2. Hence γ^ and ℓp′(λ0) have opposite signs, and γ^=0 iff λ0 is a stationary point of the profile log-likelihood. In particular γ^=0 at an interior maximum λ0=λ^.

Proof.

  • (a) SSEz(λ)=z(λ)⊤(I−M)z(λ). Its derivative is 2z(λ)⊤(I−M)∂z(λ)/∂λ, because I−M is symmetric. At λ0 this is 𝛆2ε^0⊤w. The chain rule applied to Eq. 22.2.3 gives the derivative of ℓp.
  • (b) By Theorem 6.6.2 the coefficient of w is w~⊤z(λ0)/‖w~‖2 with w~=(I−M)w≠0, and 𝛆w~⊤z(λ0)=w⊤(I−M)z(λ0)=w⊤ε^0. Comparing with (a) gives the rest.

To first order z(λ)≈z(λ0)+(λ−λ0)w, so if the model holds at λ, then 𝛃𝛆z(λ0)≈Xβ−(λ−λ0)w+ε and λ0−γ^ is a rough one-step estimate of λ. The added-variable plot of w (Theorem 6.6.2) shows which observations carry the evidence, the use Atkinson (1985) made of it. The t statistic of γ^ tests λ=λ0, but not exactly: w is a function of y itself, not only of the fitted values, so Theorem 14.7.2 does not apply.

Example (The constructed variable for Engel’s data)

In the model with log income, the constructed variable for λ0=1 has coefficient γ^=0.831 and t=17.96: overwhelming evidence against the raw scale. The one-step estimate 1−0.831=0.169 is far from λ^=0.025, as expected from so far away. For λ0=0 the coefficient is −0.031 with t=−0.38.

In 4000 data sets simulated from the fitted log–log model, with normal errors and Engel’s incomes (so λ=0 is true), the 95% likelihood ratio interval covers 0 in a fraction 0.945, close to nominal, while the nominal 5% constructed-variable test rejects in a fraction 0.075. The constructed variable is a diagnostic; the likelihood interval is for inference.

Deleting one household at a time, the largest change moves λ^ from 0.025 to 0.081, when the household with income 2823 and food expenditure 2033, the largest in the survey, is removed. It lies well above the line in panels (a) and (c) of Figure 22.1.1, at a point of high leverage (the leverage of Engel’s households is examined in Section 20.2). The logarithm stays inside the interval whichever household is removed. For a fixed scale Chapter 20 gives deletion formulas (Theorem 20.3.1, and Proposition 10.6.2 for their computation); for λ^ the refits are cheap enough to do directly.

def constructed(y, lam0, h=1e-5):
    """w = d z(lambda)/d lambda at lam0 (numerical derivative of the normalized transform)."""
    return (box_cox_z(y, lam0 + h) - box_cox_z(y, lam0 - h)) / (2 * h)


def constructed_test(y, X, lam0):
    """Regress z(lam0) on [X, w]: the coefficient of w and its t statistic."""
    w = constructed(y, lam0)
    fit = sm.OLS(box_cox_z(y, lam0), np.column_stack([X, w])).fit()
    return fit.params[-1], fit.tvalues[-1]


for lam0 in (1, 0):
    g, t = constructed_test(food, X_log, lam0)
    print(f"lambda0 = {lam0}: coefficient of w {g:.3f}, t = {t:.2f}, one-step estimate {lam0 - g:.3f}")

In the browser the simulation uses 200 replications, so its fractions are rough.

def simulate(reps, seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    fit = sm.OLS(np.log(food), X_log).fit()           # the log-log fit: data generated with lambda = 0
    mu, sigma = fit.fittedvalues, np.sqrt(fit.scale)
    cover = reject = 0
    for _ in range(reps):
        y_sim = np.exp(mu + sigma * rng.standard_normal(n))
        lam_s, (lo_s, hi_s), _ = box_cox_fit(y_sim, X_log)
        cover += lo_s <= 0 <= hi_s
        reject += abs(constructed_test(y_sim, X_log, 0)[1]) > stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 3)
    return cover / reps, reject / reps


coverage, size = simulate(reps=200, seed=2201)
print(f"coverage of the 95% LR interval {coverage:.3f}, size of the 5% constructed-variable test {size:.3f}")

Warning. λ^ responds to anything that spoils the normal linear model: curvature, a variance that changes with the level, skewness, single outlying cases. A value that makes the residuals look better has not necessarily corrected the defect that mattered. After transforming, look at the residual plots of Chapter 20 and Chapter 21 again, on the new scale.

22.2.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Compute 4(λ) for λ=−1,0,12,1,2. Check that the values increase with λ, and explain, using the definition, why y(λ) is increasing in λ for every fixed y>0.

Exercise (A2)

Show that if every yi is multiplied by c>0, the residual sum of squares of the unnormalized y(λ) in a model with an intercept is multiplied by c2λ. Conclude that choosing λ by minimizing the unnormalized residual sum of squares would give an answer that depends on the units of y.

Solution. For λ≠0, ((cy)λ−1)/λ=cλ(yλ−1)/λ+(cλ−1)/λ, a multiple cλ of y(λ) plus a constant, which the intercept absorbs. So the residual sum of squares is multiplied by c2λ, a factor that depends on λ. Changing the units reweights the comparison between different values of λ: as c grows the comparison is pushed towards ever more negative λ, and as c shrinks towards ever larger λ. By Proposition 22.2.3 the normalized version has the common factor c2 and no such problem.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Derive the constructed variable at λ0=0. Write z(λ)=f(λ)y˙1−λ with f(λ)=(yλ−1)/λ, expand f(λ)=log⁡y+12λ(log⁡y)2+O(λ2), and show that ∂z(λ)/∂λ at 0 is 12y˙{log⁡(y/y˙)}2−12y˙(log⁡y˙)2.

Solution. The expansion follows from yλ=1+λlog⁡y+12λ2(log⁡y)2+O(λ3), so f(0)=log⁡y and f′(0)=12(log⁡y)2. Also y˙1−λ=y˙exp⁡(−λlog⁡y˙) has value y˙ and derivative −y˙log⁡y˙ at 0. By the product rule the derivative of z(λ) at 0 is y˙{12(log⁡y)2−log⁡ylog⁡y˙}=12y˙{(log⁡y−log⁡y˙)2−(log⁡y˙)2}.

Exercise (B2)

In Example (Box–Cox for Engel’s data), with log income as regressor, the normalized residual standard deviations are 78.6 at λ=0 and 115.6 at λ=1. Show from Eq. 22.2.3 that the difference between the likelihood ratio statistics at λ=1 and λ=0 is 2nlog⁡{sz(1)/sz(0)}, and check it against the values 181.4 and 0.12 of the example.

Exercise (B3)

For data that may include zeros, consider the shifted family (y+c)(λ) with an unknown c such that every yi+c>0. Write the log-likelihood of 𝛃(β,σ2,λ,c), show that the profile log-likelihood of (λ,c) is Eq. 22.2.3 with z(λ) computed from yi+c and its geometric mean, and state the likelihood ratio confidence region for (λ,c).

Solution. The Jacobian of y↦(y+c)(λ) is (y+c)λ−1, so the log-likelihood is Eq. 22.2.2 with yi replaced by yi+c throughout. The proof of Theorem 22.2.2 goes through word for word for each fixed c, with y˙ the geometric mean of the yi+c. The region is {(λ,c):nlog⁡{SSEz(λ,c)/SSEz(λ^,c^)}≤χ2,α2}, two degrees of freedom because two parameters are profiled. The maximization needs care near the boundary c→−miniyi: there log⁡(miniyi+c)→−∞, so for λ<1 the Jacobian term (λ−1)∑ilog⁡(yi+c) grows without bound, and the likelihood can be unbounded, as it is for the three-parameter lognormal distribution.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Suppose that the conditional distribution of Y given (x1,…,xk) does not depend on x1. Show that the same holds for g(Y) for every strictly increasing g, so the hypothesis that x1 has no effect is the same hypothesis on every scale. Why does the converse direction, from a zero coefficient on one scale to a zero coefficient on another, fail when the conditional distribution does depend on x1? Give an example with two groups whose medians are equal but whose means are not.

Exercise (C2)

Let λ^ be an interior maximizer of ℓp, and write 𝛆s(λ)=ε^(λ)⊤w(λ), which is zero at λ^ by Proposition 22.2.4. Suppose case i is deleted. Show, by one Newton step for the equation s=0, that the change in λ^ is approximately proportional to the contribution ε^(i)w~(i) of that case, where both factors are computed with the case deleted. Which cases does this predict to be influential for λ^, and how does leverage in the regression of w on X enter?

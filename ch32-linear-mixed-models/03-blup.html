32.3  Prediction: BLUP and Henderson’s equations

The twelve laboratories of Example (A laboratory proficiency study) each have a realized effect ak. It is not a parameter but the value taken by a random variable, so asking for it is a prediction problem. The answer is worth the trouble of setting the problem up properly: it is not the laboratory’s own average, and the difference is shrinkage of exactly the kind Chapter 27 studied, arriving here without anybody having decided to be biased.

32.3.1. The criterion

Definition 32.3.1 (Mixed functions and linear predictors)

In Definition 32.2.1, a mixed function is a random variable 𝛌𝛃τ=λ⊤β+c⊤u with known 𝛌λ∈Rp and c∈Rq. A linear predictor of τ is a statistic t=a⊤Y+b with known a and b; it is unbiased if E(t)=E(τ) for every 𝛃β∈Rp. The best linear unbiased predictor (BLUP) of τ is the unbiased linear predictor minimizing the prediction mean squared error E(t−τ)2.

Since 𝛃E(t)=a⊤Xβ+b and 𝛌𝛃E(τ)=λ⊤β, unbiasedness for every 𝛃β forces b=0 and 𝛌X⊤a=λ. So an unbiased linear predictor exists exactly when 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤), that is, when 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β is estimable in the sense of Definition 8.2.1; the random part c⊤u places no restriction at all, because its mean is zero whatever c is. Note also that the criterion averages over u as well as over 𝛆ε. This is the decision that makes the problem solvable, and the warning in the next subsection says what it costs.

The problem has already been solved. Theorem 14.2.8 of Section 14.2 predicted a new response 𝛃y0=x0⊤β+e0 whose error was correlated with the training errors, and c⊤u is exactly such an e0. What follows is that theorem in mixed-model notation; its proof is the substitution, plus the uniqueness and the decomposition (c) that Chapter 14 did not need.

Theorem 32.3.1 (Best linear unbiased prediction)

In Definition 32.2.1 let V=ZGZ⊤+R be positive definite and let 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤). Put

𝛃𝛃β^=(X⊤V−1X)−X⊤V−1Y,u^=GZ⊤V−1(Y−Xβ^).
(32.3.1)

  • (a) The BLUP of 𝛌𝛃τ=λ⊤β+c⊤u is 𝛌𝛃τ^=λ⊤β^+c⊤u^. It does not depend on which generalized inverse is used, and it is the unique minimizer.

  • (b) Its prediction error variance is

    E(τ^−τ)2=c⊤(G−GZ⊤V−1ZG)c+d⊤(X⊤V−1X)−d,
    (32.3.2)

    where 𝛌d=λ−X⊤V−1ZGc.

  • (c) If 𝛃β were known, the best linear predictor of τ would be 𝛌𝛃𝛃λ⊤β+c⊤GZ⊤V−1(Y−Xβ), with prediction error variance the first term of Eq. 32.3.2. The second term is the price of estimating 𝛃β.

Proof. Apply Theorem 14.2.8 to 𝛃𝛆Y=Xβ+(Zu+ε), whose error vector has covariance V, with σ2=1, y0=τ, 𝛌x0=λ and e0=c⊤u. Since 𝛆Cov(Zu+ε,u)=ZG, the two covariance inputs of that theorem are 𝛆v0=Cov(Zu+ε,c⊤u)=ZGc=:s,v00=Var(c⊤u)=c⊤Gc, and its hypothesis x0∈C(X⊤) is our 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤). So Eq. 14.2.3 reads 𝛌𝛃𝛃𝛌𝛃τ^=λ⊤β^+c⊤GZ⊤V−1(Y−Xβ^)=λ⊤β^+c⊤u^, and its mean squared error is Eq. 32.3.2, because the vector called c there is 𝛌x0−X⊤V−1v0=λ−X⊤V−1ZGc=d here. That is (a) and (b) apart from the two uniqueness claims.

For those, recall that an unbiased linear predictor is t=a⊤Y with 𝛌X⊤a=λ, and that t−τ then has mean zero, so E(t−τ)2=Var(a⊤Y−c⊤u)=a⊤Va−2a⊤s+c⊤Gc. The last term does not involve a and V is positive definite, so this is a strictly convex function of a on the affine set 𝛌{a:X⊤a=λ}, non-empty because 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤), and a strictly convex function has at most one minimizer on a convex set. So a, and with it τ^, is unique, by an argument that nowhere asks X⊤ to have full row rank. The generalized inverse then cancels, as one can also see directly: 𝛃Xβ^=X(X⊤V−1X)−X⊤V−1Y is the projection of Y onto C(X) in the V−1 inner product (Definition 6.9.1), and both 𝛌𝛃𝛃λ⊤β^=r⊤Xβ^, for r with 𝛌λ=X⊤r, and u^ involve 𝛃β^ only through 𝛃Xβ^.

For (c), with 𝛃β known the problem is unconstrained prediction of c⊤u from 𝛃Y−Xβ, whose solution is the best linear predictor of Theorem 2.5.2: 𝛃Cov(c⊤u,Y)V−1(Y−Xβ), with error variance c⊤Gc−s⊤V−1s, which is the first term of Eq. 32.3.2 because s=ZGc.

Two readings of Eq. 32.3.1 are worth having. First, u^ is the best linear predictor of u with 𝛃β replaced by its generalized least squares estimate; the whole content of “BLUP” is that this substitution is optimal, not merely convenient. Second, if the model is normal then 𝛃E(u∣Y)=GZ⊤V−1(Y−Xβ) by Theorem 3.5.2, so the BLUP is the conditional mean with 𝛃β estimated. That is the door into Section 32.6.

32.3.2. Shrinkage

Specialize to the balanced one-way model of Definition 32.1.1, with X=1, Z the group indicators, G=σa2Ig and R=σ2I. By Eq. 32.1.2, V−1=λ1−1M+σ−2(I−M) with λ1=σ2+mσa2, and since MZ=Z we have Z⊤V−1=λ1−1Z⊤. Also 1∈C(Z), so the generalized least squares estimate of μ is the ordinary mean y¯ (Example (Cluster-constant regressors)). Hence

a^k=mσa2σ2+mσa2(y¯k−y¯)=B(y¯k−y¯),B=mγ1+mγ,
(32.3.3)

with γ=σa2/σ2. The predicted group mean is y¯+B(y¯k−y¯): the group’s own average pulled towards the overall average by B∈(0,1). The pull is strong when groups are small or when groups genuinely differ little, and it vanishes as either m or γ grows. Formula Eq. 32.3.3 is the empirical Bayes shrinkage of Proposition 27.5.4 with the prior variance supplied by the model, and it is a ridge estimate of the group effects with penalty σ2/σa2 (Theorem 27.2.2); the next subsection makes that identification exact.

Warning. Unbiasedness in Definition 32.3.1 averages over u. Conditionally on the realized effects it fails, and deliberately so: from Eq. 32.3.3, E(a^k∣a1,…,ag)=B(ak−a¯), which is nearer zero than ak. Predicted effects are shrunk, so their spread understates the spread of the true effects, and ranking groups by a^k is not the same as ranking them by ak. A league table of hospitals or schools built from BLUPs will move small units towards the middle, which is right for predicting each unit’s next observation and wrong if the question is “which unit is genuinely worst”.

The shrinkage is paid for in mean squared error. Continuing the balanced one-way case, Eq. 32.3.2 with 𝛌λ=0 and c=ek gives, after substituting G−GZ⊤V−1ZG=σa2σ2λ1−1I and X⊤V−1X=n/λ1,

E(a^k−ak)2=σa2λ1(σ2+mσa2g),
(32.3.4)

whereas the obvious predictor y¯k−y¯ has mean squared error σ2(1−1/g)/m+σa2/g (Exercise (B1)).

Example (Shrinking twelve laboratories)

In Example (A laboratory proficiency study), σ^2=0.0620 and σ^a2=0.0386 give a shrinkage factor B=0.714. The laboratory deviations y¯k−y¯ run from −0.450 to 0.250; the predicted effects run from −0.321 to 0.178. Each has been pulled about 29 per cent of the way towards zero.

The gain is real. By Eq. 32.3.4 the prediction error variance is 0.01336, a standard error of 0.1156, against 0.01743 for the raw deviation, a ratio of 1.30 in mean squared error. Panel (b) of Figure 32.3.1 shows the comparison over the whole range of intraclass correlations: shrinkage helps most when the correlation is small, which is exactly when the raw deviations are mostly noise.

32.3.3. Henderson’s mixed model equations

Formula Eq. 32.3.1 asks for V−1, an n×n inverse. Henderson (1950) found a system of size p+q that delivers the generalized least squares estimate and the BLUP together, without ever forming V.

Theorem 32.3.2 (Henderson’s mixed model equations)

Let G and R be positive definite and rank(X)=p. Define

C=(X⊤R−1XX⊤R−1ZZ⊤R−1XZ⊤R−1Z+G−1),h=(X⊤R−1yZ⊤R−1y).
(32.3.5)

  • (a) C is positive definite, and the unique solution of C(b⊤,v⊤)⊤=h is 𝛃b=β^, the generalized least squares estimate, and v=u^, the BLUP of Eq. 32.3.1.

  • (b) The same pair is the unique minimizer of the penalized least squares criterion

    𝛃𝛃𝛃Q(β,u)=(y−Xβ−Zu)⊤R−1(y−Xβ−Zu)+u⊤G−1u.
    (32.3.6)

  • (c) With W=X⊤V−1X, the blocks of C−1 are (C−1)11=W−1,(C−1)12=−W−1X⊤V−1ZG, (C−1)22=G−GZ⊤V−1ZG+GZ⊤V−1XW−1X⊤V−1ZG. Its leading block is 𝛃Cov(β^) and its trailing block is Cov(u^−u), the matrix whose quadratic forms are Eq. 32.3.2.

Proof. We use repeatedly the push-through identity

(G−1+Z⊤R−1Z)−1Z⊤R−1=GZ⊤V−1,
(32.3.7)

proved by multiplying on the right by V=R+ZGZ⊤: (G−1+Z⊤R−1Z)−1(Z⊤+Z⊤R−1ZGZ⊤)=(G−1+Z⊤R−1Z)−1(G−1+Z⊤R−1Z)GZ⊤=GZ⊤.

  • (a) For (b⊤,v⊤)≠0, (b⊤,v⊤)C(b⊤,v⊤)⊤=‖R−1/2(Xb+Zv)‖2+v⊤G−1v, which vanishes only if v=0 and Xb=0, hence only if b=0 by full column rank. So C is positive definite and the system has a unique solution. The second block row gives v=(Z⊤R−1Z+G−1)−1Z⊤R−1(y−Xb)=GZ⊤V−1(y−Xb) by Eq. 32.3.7. Substituting into the first block row, and using the residual identity y−Xb−Zv=(I−ZGZ⊤V−1)(y−Xb)=(V−ZGZ⊤)V−1(y−Xb)=RV−1(y−Xb), the first block row X⊤R−1(y−Xb−Zv)=0 becomes X⊤V−1(y−Xb)=0, the generalized normal equations of Definition 6.9.1. Hence 𝛃b=β^ and then v=u^.

  • (b) Q is a quadratic in 𝛃(β,u) with matrix C and linear term −2h, and the display in (a) shows it is strictly convex. By Proposition 1.11.2 its unique minimizer solves C(b⊤,v⊤)⊤=h, which is Eq. 32.3.5, whose solution is 𝛃(β^,u^) by part (a).

  • (c) Write C11=X⊤R−1X, C12=X⊤R−1Z and C22=Z⊤R−1Z+G−1, so that by Eq. 32.2.3 the Schur complement of C22 is C11−C12C22−1C12⊤=X⊤(R−1−R−1Z(G−1+Z⊤R−1Z)−1Z⊤R−1)X=X⊤V−1X=W. Theorem 1.6.4 now gives (C−1)11=W−1 and (C−1)12=−W−1C12C22−1=−W−1X⊤V−1ZG, the last step being the transpose of Eq. 32.3.7. For the trailing block, (C−1)22=C22−1+C22−1C12⊤W−1C12C22−1=C22−1+GZ⊤V−1XW−1X⊤V−1ZG, and C22−1=G−GZ⊤V−1ZG, because (G−GZ⊤V−1ZG)(G−1+Z⊤R−1Z)=I+GZ⊤R−1Z−GZ⊤V−1(ZGZ⊤+R)R−1Z=I, using Eq. 32.3.7 once more in the form GZ⊤V−1V=GZ⊤. Finally, 𝛃Cov(β^)=W−1 is Corollary 7.2.3, and the trailing block is Eq. 32.3.2 with 𝛌λ=0 read off entry by entry.

Idea. Henderson’s system says that a mixed model is a penalized least squares problem (Eq. 32.3.6): fit the random effects as though they were free parameters, but charge u⊤G−1u for using them. With R=σ2I and G=σu2I the criterion is 𝛃‖y−Xβ−Zu‖2+λ‖u‖2 with λ=σ2/σu2: ridge regression on the random-effect columns only (Theorem 27.2.2). The difference from Chapter 27 is that the penalty is not chosen by cross-validation; it is estimated, because the model says what σ2/σu2 means. Chapter 43 exploits the same equivalence in the other direction, writing a penalized spline as a mixed model so that its smoothing parameter can be estimated by REML (Theorem 43.6.1).

import numpy as np

g, m = 12, 4
n = g * m
mu, sigma_a, sigma = 2.50, 0.20, 0.25           # the values used to simulate

rng = np.random.default_rng(20320048)
lab_effect = sigma_a * rng.standard_normal(g)
y = mu + np.repeat(lab_effect, m) + sigma * rng.standard_normal(n)

X = np.ones((n, 1))                             # one fixed effect, the grand mean
Z = np.repeat(np.eye(g), m, axis=0)             # laboratory indicators

Y = y.reshape(g, m)                             # moment estimates, Section 32.1
lab_mean = Y.mean(axis=1)
ms_between = m * np.sum((lab_mean - Y.mean()) ** 2) / (g - 1)
ms_within = np.sum((Y - lab_mean[:, None]) ** 2) / (n - g)
s2, s2a = ms_within, (ms_between - ms_within) / m

G = s2a * np.eye(g)
R = s2 * np.eye(n)
V = Z @ G @ Z.T + R
Vinv = np.linalg.inv(V)
beta_gls = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ Vinv @ X, X.T @ Vinv @ y)
u_blup = G @ Z.T @ Vinv @ (y - X @ beta_gls)

# Henderson's mixed model equations: one (p + q) x (p + q) system, no n x n inverse
Rinv = np.linalg.inv(R)
C = np.block([[X.T @ Rinv @ X, X.T @ Rinv @ Z],
              [Z.T @ Rinv @ X, Z.T @ Rinv @ Z + np.linalg.inv(G)]])
rhs = np.concatenate([X.T @ Rinv @ y, Z.T @ Rinv @ y])
solution = np.linalg.solve(C, rhs)

print("GLS estimate   ", beta_gls, solution[:1])
print("first two BLUPs", u_blup[:2], solution[1:3])
shrinkage = m * s2a / (s2 + m * s2a)            # the shrinkage factor B
print(f"B = {shrinkage:.4f}")
print("u_hat  ", np.round(u_blup[:4], 4))
print("B(ybar_k - ybar)", np.round(shrinkage * (lab_mean - y.mean())[:4], 4))

Two practical points close the section. First, C is sparse whenever Z is, which is always: each row of Z has one non-zero entry per random factor, so Eq. 32.3.5 can be solved by sparse Cholesky factorization, and a model with hundreds of thousands of random effects is routine. Second, G and R are unknown in practice and estimates are substituted; the result is called the empirical BLUP, and Eq. 32.3.2 is then too small, since it ignores the variability of the estimated covariance. Kackar and Harville (1984) identified the extra term, which returns in Section 32.5 as the Kenward–Roger correction and in Section 32.6 as the gap between posterior and plug-in standard errors.

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In Eq. 32.3.3, what happens to B as (a) m→∞, (b) σa2→0, (c) σ2→0? Describe in words the predictor in each limit, and say which of the three limits makes the mixed model agree with a fixed-effects analysis.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Show that the predictor Y¯k−Y¯ of ak in the balanced one-way model has E(Y¯k−Y¯−ak)2=σ2(1−1/g)/m+σa2/g, and verify that Eq. 32.3.4 is smaller for every γ>0. Where does the difference come from when γ is very small?

Solution. Y¯k−Y¯−ak=ε¯k−ε¯−a¯ with a¯=g−1∑jaj. The three terms have variances σ2/m, σ2/(gm) and σa2/g, and Cov(ε¯k,ε¯)=σ2/(gm), giving σ2/m−σ2/(gm)+σa2/g as stated. In terms of γ=σa2/σ2, Eq. 32.3.4 equals σ2γ(1+mγ/g)/(1+mγ) while the raw predictor’s error is σ2{(1−1/g)/m+γ/g}. Putting the two over the denominator 1+mγ and cancelling, their difference is exactly σ2m(1−1g)11+mγ=σ2m(1−1g)(1−B), which is strictly positive for every γ. As γ→0 the BLUP’s error tends to σa2 while the raw predictor’s tends to σ2/m, which is much larger: the raw predictor pays the full measurement error even when there is nothing to predict, whereas the BLUP predicts 0 and pays only σa2.

Exercise (B2)

Take τ=μ+ak, the mean of group k in the balanced one-way model, so that 𝛌λ=1 and c=ek. Use Eq. 32.3.1 and Eq. 32.3.2 to show that the BLUP is y¯+B(y¯k−y¯) with prediction error variance σ2B/m+(1−B)2λ1/n. Show that this equals λ1/n at B=0 and σ2/m at B=1; that it is strictly below σ2/m, the prediction error of the group’s own mean; and that it is below λ1/n as well exactly when mσa2>σ2(g−2), so that it is no interpolation between the two — with g=3, m=4 and σ2=σa2=1 it beats both.

Solution. The predictor is immediate from Eq. 32.3.1. For the variance, d=1−1⊤V−1ZGek=1−mσa2/λ1=1−B, and W−=λ1/n, so the second term of Eq. 32.3.2 is (1−B)2λ1/n; the first term is σa2σ2/λ1=σ2B/m since B=mσa2/λ1. Setting B=0 and B=1 gives the two stated endpoints. For the comparisons, use 1−B=σ2/λ1 and n=gm to collapse the whole expression to σ2λ1(σa2+σ2gm), and note that σ2/m=(σ2/λ1)(σa2+σ2/m), so the first comparison holds whenever g>1. The second asks σ2σa2gm+σ4<λ12=(σ2+mσa2)2, which after cancelling σ4 and dividing by mσa2 is σ2g<2σ2+mσa2. In the numerical case the expression is 13/60, below both 5/12 and 1/4: the quadratic in B is not an interpolation between two strategies but can beat both, and that gain is what the shrinkage buys.

Exercise (B3)

Take X empty (no fixed effects), R=σ2I and G=σu2I in Theorem 32.3.2. Show that u^ is exactly the ridge estimate (Z⊤Z+λI)−1Z⊤y with λ=σ2/σu2, and that Eq. 32.3.2 becomes σu2c⊤(I+λ−1Z⊤Z)−1c. Compare with the mean squared error of ridge in Theorem 27.2.2: which is the same quantity and which is not?

Solution. With no X, Eq. 32.3.5 reduces to (Z⊤Z/σ2+I/σu2)v=Z⊤y/σ2, that is v=(Z⊤Z+λI)−1Z⊤y. By Theorem 32.3.2(c) the prediction error covariance is C−1=σ2(Z⊤Z+λI)−1=σu2(I+λ−1Z⊤Z)−1. The quantity computed here averages over u; the mean squared error in Theorem 27.2.2 is computed for a fixed 𝛃β and is therefore a function of that fixed value. Averaging the ridge mean squared error over 𝛃β∼N(0,σu2I) reproduces the BLUP formula, which is the empirical Bayes reading of Proposition 27.5.4.

32.5  Inference when the covariance is estimated

Every formula in the last three sections assumed V known. In practice 𝛉V(θ^) is substituted, and the substitution is not free: it perturbs the estimator, it perturbs the estimator’s covariance, and it destroys the exactness of the F and t distributions Part III relied on. Testing a variance component raises a separate difficulty, since the null value sits on the boundary of the parameter space and the usual chi-square calibration is simply wrong; Theorem 32.5.2 computes the right one exactly in the balanced layout.

32.5.1. Feasible generalized least squares is still unbiased

Proposition 32.5.1 (Fixed-effect inference with an estimated covariance)

Consider the normal linear mixed model with rank(X)=p, and write 𝛃𝛉β^(θ)=(X⊤V−1X)−1X⊤V−1Y for the generalized least squares estimator.

  • (a) (Unbiasedness.) Let 𝛉𝛉θ^=θ^(Y) be translation invariant, meaning 𝛉𝛉θ^(y+Xb)=θ^(y) for all b, and even, meaning 𝛉𝛉θ^(−y)=θ^(y). If 𝛃𝛉E‖β^(θ^)‖<∞, then 𝛃𝛉𝛃Eβ^(θ^)=β. Both REML and maximum likelihood estimators of 𝛉θ have these two properties.

  • (b) (A design where everything is exact.) Consider a balanced cluster-randomized trial: g clusters, g/2 assigned to each arm, m units per cluster, with a random cluster effect of variance σa2 and independent errors of variance σ2. Let β^ be the difference of the two arms’ means of cluster means, and let MSC be the mean square between clusters within arms, on g−2 degrees of freedom, and MSE the mean square within clusters. Then, with λ1=σ2+mσa2, Var(β^)=4λ1/(gm) and

    β^−β4MSC/(gm)∼t(g−2)
    (32.5.1)

    exactly. If MSC≥MSE then MSC is also the REML estimate of λ1; otherwise the REML estimate lies on the boundary σ^a2=0, as in Proposition 32.4.3(c).

  • (c) (Satterthwaite’s approximation.) Let U=∑iciMSi>0, where the MSi are independent and νiMSi/EMSi∼χ2(νi). The two-moment match of Section 4.3, fitting νU/EU∼χ2(ν) to the mean and variance of U, has

    ν=(EU)2∑ici2(EMSi)2/νi,
    (32.5.2)

    estimated in practice by replacing EMSi by MSi.

The companion statement for the variance components themselves, where the null value sits on the boundary of the parameter space, is Theorem 32.5.2 below.

Proof.

  • (a) Write 𝛆𝛃ε=Y−Xβ and 𝛉A(θ)=(X⊤V−1X)−1X⊤V−1, so that 𝛉A(θ)X=I and 𝛃𝛉𝛃𝛉𝛆β^(θ^)−β=A(θ^(Y))ε. Translation invariance gives 𝛉𝛉𝛃𝛆𝛉𝛆θ^(Y)=θ^(Xβ+ε)=θ^(ε), so 𝛃𝛉𝛃𝛆β^(θ^)−β=h(ε) with 𝛉h(e)=A(θ^(e))e. Evenness gives h(−e)=−h(e), and 𝛆ε has the same distribution as 𝛆−ε, so 𝛆𝛆Eh(ε)=−Eh(ε) and the expectation is 0. This is the argument of Theorem 21.3.1(c) with the weights replaced by a covariance. REML estimators are functions of K⊤y alone, hence translation invariant, and Eq. 32.4.4 is unchanged by y↦−y, hence even; the same holds for maximum likelihood, whose profile Eq. 32.4.3 depends on y only through y⊤Py and is translation invariant because PX=0.

  • (b) Both columns of X=[1,T] are constant within clusters, so by Example (Cluster-constant regressors) ordinary and generalized least squares agree and β^ is the difference of arm means of cluster means. The g cluster means Y¯k are independent N(β0+βTk,λ1/m), because Var(Y¯k)=σa2+σ2/m=λ1/m. Applying the exact normal theory of Chapter 11 to these g independent normal observations gives β^∼N(β,4λ1/(gm)), since the two arm means are independent with variance λ1/(m⋅g/2) each. The quadratic form m∑k(Y¯k−Y¯arm(k))2/λ1=(g−2)MSC/λ1 is the residual sum of squares of a two-group model fitted to the g cluster means, so it is χ2(g−2) and is independent of the fitted arm means by Theorem 4.4.1. Dividing gives Eq. 32.5.1.

    For the REML claim, the error contrast space C(X)⊥ decomposes orthogonally into C(Z)∩C(X)⊥, of dimension g−2, on which V acts as λ1I, and C(Z)⊥, of dimension n−g, on which V acts as σ2I. Choosing K adapted to this decomposition, Eq. 32.4.4 becomes −2ℓR=(g−2)log⁡λ1+(n−g)log⁡σ2+SSC/λ1+SSE/σ2 up to constants, to be minimized over λ1≥σ2>0, exactly as in Proposition 32.4.3. Its minimizers are λ^1=MSC and σ^2=MSE when MSC≥MSE; otherwise the constraint binds, by the argument of Proposition 32.4.3(c).

  • (c) The two-moment match of Section 4.3 gives ν=2(EU)2/Var(U), and here Var(U)=∑ici2Var(MSi)=2∑ici2(EMSi)2/νi, which is Eq. 32.5.2 — the calculation already made for a linear combination of independent variance estimates in Section 15.5.

Part (b) isolates the problem. The standard error a mixed-model program reports for this design is exactly right: it is 4MSC/(gm) whenever the REML estimate of λ1 is interior, that is, unless MSC<MSE — probability 0.157 in the design simulated below, and 0.558 when σa2 is in fact zero. What is wrong is the reference distribution. Treating β^/se as standard normal, which is what a Wald test does, ignores that MSC came from g−2 degrees of freedom. With g=8 clusters that is six, and the t critical value 2.447 exceeds 1.96 by 25 per cent.

Example (Eight clusters)

Simulate the trial with g=8 clusters of m=5, an intraclass correlation of 0.2, and no treatment effect. Over 200000 trials, the nominal 95% normal Wald interval covers 0.901 of the time, while the t(g−2) interval covers 0.949. Figure 32.5.1 traces the coverage against the number of clusters: 0.812 with four clusters, still 0.943 with forty. The normal interval’s coverage is exactly 2Ft(g−2)(1.96)−1, which is why it approaches but never reaches 0.95.

The coverage study is the null one; take now a single data set simulated from the same design but with a true effect of 0.8. On it the estimated effect is 1.154 with standard error 0.479. The normal interval is 1.154±0.940, which excludes zero; the exact interval is 1.154±1.173, which does not. The whole difference between “significant” and “not” is the reference distribution.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def simulate(g, m, sigma_a, sigma, effect, B, seed):
    """B cluster-randomized trials; returns the estimate and its estimated variance."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    treat = np.tile([0.0, 1.0], g // 2)                 # cluster k is treated if treat[k]=1
    Y = (effect * treat[:, None] + sigma_a * rng.standard_normal((B, g, 1))
         + sigma * rng.standard_normal((B, g, m)))
    cluster_mean = Y.mean(axis=2)
    diff = (cluster_mean[:, treat == 1].mean(axis=1)
            - cluster_mean[:, treat == 0].mean(axis=1))
    # mean square between clusters within arms, on g - 2 degrees of freedom
    centred = cluster_mean - np.where(treat == 1,
                                      cluster_mean[:, treat == 1].mean(axis=1, keepdims=True),
                                      cluster_mean[:, treat == 0].mean(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    lam1 = m * np.sum(centred**2, axis=1) / (g - 2)     # estimates sigma^2 + m sigma_a^2
    return diff, 4 * lam1 / (g * m)

g, m = 8, 5
# a shorter run than the 200 000 trials quoted in the text, so the cell finishes quickly
diff, var_hat = simulate(g, m, 0.5, 1.0, 0.0, 20000, seed=5150)
t = diff / np.sqrt(var_hat)
print(f"normal interval covers {np.mean(np.abs(t) <= stats.norm.ppf(0.975)):.3f}")
print(f"t(g-2) interval covers {np.mean(np.abs(t) <= stats.t.ppf(0.975, g - 2)):.3f}")

32.5.2. Degrees of freedom in general

Outside designs as tidy as Proposition 32.5.1(b) there is no exact answer, and two approximations are in common use. Neither is proved here; both rest on simulation evidence rather than exact distribution theory.

Satterthwaite. The estimated variance 𝛌𝛉𝛌λ⊤(X⊤V(θ^)−1X)−1λ is a smooth function of 𝛉θ^. Approximating its distribution by a scaled chi-square and matching two moments, with the variance from the delta method and the asymptotic covariance of 𝛉θ^, gives a denominator degrees of freedom that is in general not an integer. Giesbrecht and Burns (1985) developed this for mixed models; in the design of Proposition 32.5.1(b) it returns the exact g−2 (Exercise (B1)).

Kenward and Roger. Satterthwaite corrects the reference distribution but not the variance estimate, and the variance estimate is biased downwards: the plug-in covariance 𝛉(X⊤V(θ^)−1X)−1 is what 𝛃β^ would have if 𝛉θ^ were the truth, and the estimator actually used varies for a second reason, because 𝛉θ^ itself varies, adding a nonnegative definite term. Kackar and Harville (1984) approximated it; Kenward and Roger (1997) add an estimate of it, then scale the F statistic and match two moments for its degrees of freedom. The correction is the default in several packages and performs well in small samples; Example (The proficiency study, four ways) exhibits the inflation.

Warning. Unlike Proposition 32.5.1(a), these are approximations with no finite-sample guarantee. When the design is small, unbalanced and the covariance structure rich, the safest calibration is a parametric bootstrap: simulate from the fitted model, refit, and use the simulated distribution of the statistic (Proposition 23.3.3 and Section 23.3). It costs a few hundred fits and needs no asymptotics.

32.5.3. Comparing models

Two rules govern likelihood comparisons in mixed models, and both follow from Section 32.4.

Different fixed effects: use maximum likelihood. Since K is determined by C(X), two fixed-effects structures give restricted likelihoods of different random vectors, and their difference means nothing. Likelihood ratio tests and information criteria across fixed-effects structures must use the ordinary likelihood Eq. 32.4.1.

Same fixed effects, different covariance: REML is preferred. Here K is the same, the restricted likelihoods are likelihoods of the same data, and the additive constants of Theorem 32.4.2(c) cancel. Restricted likelihood ratio tests, and the AIC and BIC of Definition 29.2.2 computed from ℓR with dk the number of covariance parameters, are legitimate. For BIC the sample size to use is n−p, the dimension of the data actually modelled, not n, following Verbeke and Molenberghs (2000).

Example (Do the firms’ slopes differ?)

Continue Example (Grunfeld’s panel with a random firm effect). Adding a firm-specific slope on market value, with variance σs2 independent of the firm intercept, raises the restricted log-likelihood from −1176.53 to −1159.01. The two models have the same fixed effects, so the comparison is legitimate: the statistic is 35.05 on one boundary parameter, far beyond any reasonable critical value, and AIC falls from 2357.1 to 2324.0, BIC from 2363.8 to 2334.2. The firms’ responses to market value genuinely differ: the estimated standard deviation of the firm-specific slopes is 0.1492.

The consequence for inference is dramatic. In the random-intercept model the coefficient of market value is 0.1094 with standard error 0.0100; in the random-slope model it is 0.0127 with standard error 0.0562, five and a half times larger. Nothing about the data changed. Two things did. The standard error grew because the effective sample size for a population-average slope is 11 firms, not 220 firm-years. The estimate moved because the two models estimate different functionals. Firm-specific intercepts and slopes with a common capital coefficient give eleven slopes of mean −0.0009 and standard deviation 0.198: their spread dwarfs their average. The random-slope model estimates that average; the random-intercept model, forced to give every firm the same slope, reports a precision-weighted blend instead. Panel (a) of Figure 32.5.2 shows the four analyses side by side; panel (b) shows that with 20 years per firm the predicted firm effects are indistinguishable from the firm-indicator estimates.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import optimize

df = sm.datasets.grunfeld.load_pandas().data.sort_values(["firm", "year"])
firms = df["firm"].unique()
y = df["invest"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), df["value"], df["capital"]])
blocks = [np.where(df["firm"].to_numpy() == f)[0] for f in firms]
n, p, g = len(y), X.shape[1], len(firms)

def restricted_loglik(theta, Zcols):
    """Restricted log-likelihood; theta holds log sigma^2 and the log variances of Zcols."""
    s2, dvar = np.exp(theta[0]), np.exp(theta[1:])
    logdet, W, XtVy, yVy = 0.0, np.zeros((p, p)), np.zeros(p), 0.0
    for ix in blocks:
        Zi = Zcols[ix]
        Vi = (Zi * dvar) @ Zi.T + s2 * np.eye(len(ix))
        Vinv = np.linalg.inv(Vi)
        logdet += np.linalg.slogdet(Vi)[1]
        W += X[ix].T @ Vinv @ X[ix]
        XtVy += X[ix].T @ Vinv @ y[ix]
        yVy += y[ix] @ Vinv @ y[ix]
    beta = np.linalg.solve(W, XtVy)
    return -0.5 * (logdet + yVy - XtVy @ beta + np.linalg.slogdet(W)[1]
                   - np.linalg.slogdet(X.T @ X)[1] + (n - p) * np.log(2 * np.pi)), beta, W

Z_int = np.ones((n, 1))                                   # random intercept only
fit = optimize.minimize(lambda t: -restricted_loglik(t, Z_int)[0],
                        np.log([2000.0, 5000.0]), method="Nelder-Mead",
                        options={"xatol": 1e-9, "fatol": 1e-10, "maxiter": 5000})
ll_int, beta_int, W_int = restricted_loglik(fit.x, Z_int)
s2_hat, s2_firm = np.exp(fit.x)
se_int = np.sqrt(np.diag(np.linalg.inv(W_int)))

print(f"sigma^2 {s2_hat:.1f}   firm variance {s2_firm:.1f}")
print(f"value   {beta_int[1]:.4f} ({se_int[1]:.4f})")
print(f"capital {beta_int[2]:.4f} ({se_int[2]:.4f})")

32.5.4. Testing a variance component

The hypothesis σa2=0 sits on the edge of the parameter space, so the usual chi-square calibration of a likelihood ratio statistic does not apply: the standard argument expands the log-likelihood about an interior maximum, and here the unconstrained maximum falls outside the parameter space about half the time. In the balanced one-way model the correct answer can be computed exactly.

Theorem 32.5.2 (Exact null distribution of the restricted likelihood ratio)

In the balanced normal one-way random effects model with n=gm, let L=2{supσa2≥0ℓR−supσa2=0ℓR} be the restricted likelihood ratio statistic for H0:σa2=0, and write F=MSB/MSE, a=(g−1)/(n−1), b=(n−g)/(n−1). Then

L={0,F≤1,(n−1)log⁡(aF+b)−(g−1)log⁡F,F>1.
(32.5.3)

Under H0, F∼F(g−1,n−g), so the null distribution of L is free of σ2 and exactly computable. In particular L is a nondecreasing function of F and Pr(L=0)=Pr{F(g−1,n−g)≤1}, a probability that tends to 12 as g and n−g grow.

Proof. By Eq. 32.4.8, −2ℓR=(g−1)log⁡λ1+(n−g)log⁡λ2+SSB/λ1+SSE/λ2 up to a constant, over the region λ1≥λ2>0. Under H0 the region collapses to λ1=λ2=λ, where the objective is (n−1)log⁡λ+(SSB+SSE)/λ, minimized at λ^=(SSB+SSE)/(n−1)=MST, with minimum (n−1)log⁡MST+(n−1).

If MSB≥MSE the unrestricted maximizers of Proposition 32.4.3(a) are feasible, and the minimum of −2ℓR is (g−1)log⁡MSB+(n−g)log⁡MSE+(n−1). Subtracting, L=(n−1)log⁡MST−(g−1)log⁡MSB−(n−g)log⁡MSE. Dividing inside every logarithm by MSE, which is legitimate because the coefficients (n−1), −(g−1) and −(n−g) sum to zero, and using MST/MSE=aF+b, gives Eq. 32.5.3. If MSB<MSE then by Proposition 32.4.3(c) the constrained maximizer is on the boundary, which is the null model, so L=0.

Under H0 the distribution of F is F(g−1,n−g) by Proposition 32.4.3 and Proposition 32.1.1(d) with γ=0, and L is a function of F alone. It is nondecreasing in F because, using (n−1)a=g−1 and then 1−a=b, dLdF=(n−1)aaF+b−g−1F=(g−1){F−(aF+b)}F(aF+b)=(g−1)b(F−1)F(aF+b) ≥ 0 for F≥1. Hence {L=0}={F≤1}. Finally, as r=g−1 and s=n−g grow, log⁡F is asymptotically normal with variance 2/r+2/s→0 and mean of order 1/r+1/s, which is small compared with the standard deviation; so Pr(log⁡F≤0)→1/2.

Remark (Sketch of the chi-bar-squared limit). The exact result explains the shape of the general answer. Put δ=F−1 and expand Eq. 32.5.3 for small δ>0: log⁡(1+aδ)=aδ−a2δ2/2+O(δ3) and log⁡(1+δ)=δ−δ2/2+O(δ3). Using (n−1)a=g−1 and (n−1)a2=(g−1)a, the first-order terms cancel and L=12(g−1)(1−a)δ2+O((n−1)δ3). Under H0, δ=F−1 has, to first order, the variance of log⁡F, namely τ2=2/(g−1)+2/(n−g)=2(n−1)/{(g−1)(n−g)}, and δ/τ is asymptotically standard normal. Since (g−1)(1−a)=(g−1)(n−g)/(n−1), the product (g−1)(1−a)τ2 equals 2 exactly, so L≈Z2 on {δ>0} for a standard normal Z, while L=0 on {δ≤0}, an event of limiting probability 1/2. So L converges in distribution to 12χ2(0)+12χ2(1), the chi-bar-squared mixture: a point mass at zero of weight one half, plus one half of a chi-square on one degree of freedom. This is a sketch, not a proof; the expansions above are heuristic. The general theorem, covering several variance components on the boundary simultaneously with weights given by the geometry of the constraint cone, is due to Self and Liang (1987), with the mixed-model case worked out by Stram and Lee (1994).

Example (What the calibration costs)

Take g=6 groups of m=4. Exactly 0.554 of the null distribution of L sits at zero, and a simulation of 200000 data sets puts 0.554 there. Referring L to the chi-bar-squared mixture, whose 5 per cent point is the 90 per cent point 2.706 of χ2(1), gives an actual size of 0.0411: slightly conservative. Referring it to χ2(1), with critical value 3.841, gives 0.0204, badly conservative — the test throws away about half its power. With g=40 groups the mixture is nearly exact, at 0.0462. Figure 32.5.3 compares the three tail functions.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def lrt_from_f(f, g, m):
    """Restricted likelihood ratio statistic for sigma_a^2 = 0, from the F ratio."""
    n = g * m
    a, b = (g - 1) / (n - 1), (n - g) / (n - 1)
    return np.where(f <= 1, 0.0,
                    (n - 1) * np.log(a * np.maximum(f, 1) + b) - (g - 1) * np.log(np.maximum(f, 1)))

g, m = 6, 4
n = g * m
p_zero = stats.f.cdf(1.0, g - 1, n - g)             # P(L = 0) = P(F <= 1)
crit_mix = stats.chi2.ppf(0.90, 1)                  # 5% point of the 50:50 mixture
print(f"g = {g}, m = {m}:  P(L = 0) = {p_zero:.3f},  5% point of the mixture {crit_mix:.3f}")

Crainiceanu and Ruppert (2004) showed that for models with a single variance component the exact null distribution can be computed in general, not only in the balanced case, and that the chi-bar-squared approximation can be poor when the design is unbalanced. Where their results do not apply, the parametric bootstrap is again the practical answer.

32.5.5. Intervals for variance components

Three routes, in decreasing order of reliability. In balanced designs, Corollary 32.1.2 gives exact intervals for ratios of variance components and for the intraclass correlation. In general, the profile restricted likelihood of Example (REML and ML for the twelve laboratories) gives an interval by cutting 1.92 units below the maximum; it respects the boundary and is asymmetric, as the likelihood is. Last and least, the Wald interval from the inverse information matrix is symmetric, frequently includes negative values, and is badly calibrated whenever σ^a2 is near zero. Burdick and Graybill (1992) is a book-length treatment of what can and cannot be done exactly.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Consider testing σa2=0 when a second variance component σb2>0 is present and is not on the boundary. Argue informally that the limiting null distribution is still 12χ2(0)+12χ2(1), and explain why testing both components simultaneously gives a mixture over χ2(0),χ2(1),χ2(2) with weights determined by the probability that the unconstrained maximizer lands in each orthant. Which weights arise if the two estimates are asymptotically independent?

Solution. Locally the log-likelihood is approximately quadratic in the parameter, so the likelihood ratio behaves like the squared distance from the unconstrained maximizer to the constrained set, measured in the information metric. With one parameter on the boundary, the constraint acts only in that coordinate: half the time the unconstrained maximizer already satisfies it and the ratio is zero, half the time the constrained maximum is on the face and the ratio is a squared standard normal. With two parameters constrained, the ratio is zero when the maximizer lies in the nonnegative orthant, a χ2(1) when it lies in one of the two adjacent orthants, and a χ2(2) when it lies in the fully negative orthant. If the two coordinates are asymptotically independent, those four orthants have probabilities 1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4, giving weights 1/4 for χ2(0), 1/2 for χ2(1) and 1/4 for χ2(2).

Exercise (C2)

Proposition 32.5.1(a) needs the error distribution to be symmetric about zero. Give an example of a translation-invariant, even 𝛉θ^ and a skewed error distribution for which 𝛃𝛉β^(θ^) is biased, and explain why symmetry cannot simply be dropped. (Compare the discussion after Theorem 21.3.1.)

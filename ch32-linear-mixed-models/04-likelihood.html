32.4  Maximum likelihood and REML

Everything so far has treated the variance components as known. They never are. The moment estimates of Section 32.1 work in balanced layouts and lose their footing in unbalanced ones, where no canonical set of quadratic forms presents itself. Under normality the likelihood has no such difficulty, but it has one defect, which this section diagnoses and repairs: maximum likelihood underestimates variance components, because it does not pay for the fixed effects it has fitted.

32.4.1. The likelihood and its profile

Under the normal linear mixed model, 𝛃Y∼Nn(Xβ,V) with 𝛉V=V(θ), so by Theorem 3.2.2

𝛃𝛉𝛃𝛃ℓ(β,θ)=−12{nlog⁡2π+log⁡detV+(y−Xβ)⊤V−1(y−Xβ)}.
(32.4.1)

For fixed 𝛉θ only the last term involves 𝛃β, and it is the generalized least squares criterion of Definition 6.9.1, minimized by 𝛃𝛉β^(θ). Writing

P=V−1−V−1X(X⊤V−1X)−X⊤V−1,
(32.4.2)

the profile log-likelihood of Definition 7.3.1 is

𝛉ℓP(θ)=−12{nlog⁡2π+log⁡detV+y⊤Py}.
(32.4.3)
Lemma 32.4.1 (The residual operator P)

Let V be positive definite and r=rank(X). The matrix P of Eq. 32.4.2 does not depend on the choice of generalized inverse, and

  • (a) PX=0, PVP=P, P is symmetric and rank(P)=n−r;

  • (b) 𝛃Py=V−1(y−Xβ^) and y⊤Py=minb(y−Xb)⊤V−1(y−Xb);

  • (c) if V depends smoothly on 𝛉θ and Vj=∂V/∂θj, then ∂P/∂θj=−PVjP.

Proof. Write X∗=V−1/2X (Theorem 1.7.6). Then P=V−1/2(I−M∗)V−1/2, where M∗=X∗(X∗⊤X∗)−X∗⊤ is the orthogonal projection onto C(X∗), which by Theorem 6.3.6 is the same for every generalized inverse and has rank r. Symmetry, PX=V−1/2(I−M∗)X∗=0, PVP=V−1/2(I−M∗)2V−1/2=P and the rank statement follow at once, which is (a). For (b), 𝛃Py=V−1y−V−1Xβ^ by the definition of 𝛃β^, and y⊤Py=‖(I−M∗)V−1/2y‖2, the squared distance from V−1/2y to C(X∗), which is the stated minimum. Part (c) is proved after Theorem 32.4.2, which supplies a shorter route.

32.4.2. Restricted maximum likelihood

The trouble with Eq. 32.4.3 is visible in the simplest case. With V=σ2I it reduces to the profile of Theorem 7.3.1, whose maximizer is σ^2=SSE/n, biased downwards by the factor (n−p)/n. The likelihood has spent p dimensions fitting 𝛃β and then measured the spread of the data as if it had not. The repair is to compute the likelihood of a part of the data that never saw 𝛃β.

Definition 32.4.1 (Error contrasts and restricted maximum likelihood)

Let r=rank(X). An error contrast is a random variable k⊤Y with X⊤k=0; its distribution does not involve 𝛃β. A maximal set of error contrasts is K⊤Y for an n×(n−r) matrix K of full column rank with C(K)=C(X)⊥. The restricted (or residual) log-likelihood is the log-likelihood of K⊤Y,

𝛉ℓR(θ)=−12{(n−r)log⁡2π+log⁡det(K⊤VK)+y⊤K(K⊤VK)−1K⊤y},
(32.4.4)

and the REML estimate of 𝛉θ maximizes it over the parameter space.

Theorem 32.4.2 (The restricted likelihood)

In Definition 32.4.1, with V positive definite:

  • (a) K⊤Y∼Nn−r(0,K⊤VK) with K⊤VK positive definite, so Eq. 32.4.4 is its log-likelihood.

  • (b) K(K⊤VK)−1K⊤=P for every admissible K. In particular the quadratic form in Eq. 32.4.4 equals y⊤Py and does not depend on K.

  • (c) Two admissible K1,K2 give restricted log-likelihoods differing by a constant free of 𝛉θ. Hence the REML estimate does not depend on which maximal set of error contrasts is used.

  • (d) If rank(X)=p, then for any admissible K with K⊤K=I,

    det(K⊤VK)=detVdet(X⊤V−1X)det(X⊤X),
    (32.4.5)

    so that

    𝛉𝛉ℓR(θ)=ℓP(θ)+p2log⁡2π−12log⁡det(X⊤V−1X)+12log⁡det(X⊤X).
    (32.4.6)

Proof.

  • (a) By Theorem 3.3.1, K⊤Y is normal with mean 𝛃K⊤Xβ=0 and covariance K⊤VK, which is positive definite because K has full column rank and V is positive definite. Its density is Theorem 3.2.2.

  • (b) Fix y and consider min{z⊤V−1z: K⊤z=K⊤y}. The feasible set is y+C(X): indeed K⊤(z−y)=0 says z−y∈C(K)⊥=C(X). So the minimum equals minb(y−Xb)⊤V−1(y−Xb)=y⊤Py by Lemma 32.4.1(b). On the other hand the constrained problem can be solved directly. The criterion is strictly convex, so by Proposition 1.11.3 the minimizer satisfies 𝛉V−1z=Kθ, that is 𝛉z=VKθ; the constraint gives 𝛉K⊤VKθ=K⊤y, and the minimum value is 𝛉𝛉θ⊤K⊤VKθ=y⊤K(K⊤VK)−1K⊤y. The two expressions agree for every y, and both are quadratic forms with symmetric matrices, so the matrices coincide.

  • (c) Since C(K1)=C(K2) and both have n−r columns of full rank, K2=K1A for a nonsingular (n−r)×(n−r) matrix A. Then det(K2⊤VK2)=(detA)2det(K1⊤VK1), while the quadratic forms are equal by (b). So the two log-likelihoods differ by −log⁡|detA|, which does not involve 𝛉θ.

  • (d) Let T=[X,K], which is nonsingular. Because X⊤K=0 and K⊤K=I, det(T⊤T)=det(X⊤X00I)=det(X⊤X), so (detT)2=det(X⊤X) and det(T⊤VT)=det(X⊤X)detV. Next, T−1 has rows (X⊤X)−1X⊤ stacked on K⊤, as one checks by multiplying. Hence the leading p×p block of (T⊤VT)−1=T−1V−1T−⊤ is (X⊤X)−1X⊤V−1X(X⊤X)−1. By Theorem 1.6.4 that block is the inverse of the Schur complement of the trailing block K⊤VK, so by Theorem 1.6.3(b), det(T⊤VT)=det(K⊤VK)⋅det(X⊤X)2det(X⊤V−1X). Equating the two expressions for det(T⊤VT) gives Eq. 32.4.5, and substituting it together with (b) into Eq. 32.4.4 gives Eq. 32.4.6.

The proof of Lemma 32.4.1(c) is now immediate: differentiating P=K(K⊤VK)−1K⊤ with K fixed gives ∂P/∂θj=−K(K⊤VK)−1K⊤VjK(K⊤VK)−1K⊤=−PVjP.

Idea. REML fits 𝛉θ to the residual directions only. It is the general form of the familiar divisor n−p: with V=σ2I, rank(X)=p and an orthonormal K (that is K⊤K=I, as in Theorem 32.4.2(d)), Eq. 32.4.4 is the likelihood of K⊤Y∼Nn−p(0,σ2I), maximized at σ^2=‖K⊤y‖2/(n−p)=SSE/(n−p)=s2; by Theorem 32.4.2(c) any other admissible K gives the same answer. Ordinary maximum likelihood gives SSE/n. So s2, the estimator every earlier chapter used, was the REML estimator all along. Its bias in a genuine mixed model is computed exactly in Proposition 32.4.3.

Warning. Since K is determined by C(X), two models with different fixed effects have restricted likelihoods of different data, and comparing them is meaningless; Section 32.5 makes the rule explicit. Worse, Eq. 32.4.6 shows that the constant 12log⁡det(X⊤X) is part of the definition, and software often omits it; then the reported restricted log-likelihood changes if a column of X is measured in thousands rather than units, by log⁡|detS| for the rescaling matrix S. Comparisons of covariance structures with the same X are unaffected, since the constant cancels.

32.4.3. Why maximum likelihood is biased, exactly

In the balanced one-way model both likelihoods can be maximized in closed form, and the bias can be computed rather than asserted.

Proposition 32.4.3 (Balanced one-way: ML and REML)

In the balanced normal one-way random effects model, write λ1=σ2+mσa2 and λ2=σ2, and treat (λ1,λ2) with λ1≥λ2>0 as the parameters. Then, up to constants free of the parameters,

−2ℓP=glog⁡λ1+(n−g)log⁡λ2+SSBλ1+SSEλ2,
(32.4.7)
−2ℓR=(g−1)log⁡λ1+(n−g)log⁡λ2+SSBλ1+SSEλ2.
(32.4.8)

The two criteria differ in one coefficient, but leave the interior of the parameter space at different moments.

  • (a) When MSB≥MSE, REML gives λ^1=MSB, λ^2=MSE, so σ^2=MSE and σ^a2=(MSB−MSE)/m: the moment estimates of Proposition 32.1.1(c), which are unbiased.

  • (b) When MSB≥gMSE/(g−1), maximum likelihood gives λ^1=SSB/g, λ^2=MSE, so σ^ML2=MSE and σ^a,ML2=1m(g−1gMSB−MSE).

    The right-hand side is the unconstrained maximizer whatever the data, it is nonnegative exactly under the stated condition, and

    E{1m(g−1gMSB−MSE)}−σa2=−λ1gm.
    (32.4.9)

  • (c) Otherwise the maximizer lies on the boundary λ1=λ2 and the method returns σ^a2=0 with σ^2 the pooled mean square: for REML when MSB<MSE, for maximum likelihood on the wider range MSB<gMSE/(g−1). Between the two thresholds the REML estimate of σa2 is positive and the ML estimate is zero.

Proof. By Eq. 32.1.2, V=λ1M+λ2(I−M), so detV=λ1gλ2n−g and V−1=λ1−1M+λ2−1(I−M). Since 1∈C(Z), V−11=λ1−11, so the generalized least squares estimate of μ is y¯ and the residual is y−y¯1. Splitting it by Theorem 6.5.1 into its parts in C(Z)∩1⊥ and C(Z)⊥, y⊤Py=(y−y¯1)⊤V−1(y−y¯1)=SSBλ1+SSEλ2. Substituting into Eq. 32.4.3 gives Eq. 32.4.7. For the restricted version, X⊤V−1X=n/λ1 and X⊤X=n, so the correction in Eq. 32.4.6 is −12log⁡(n/λ1)+12log⁡n=12log⁡λ1, which converts the coefficient g of log⁡λ1 into g−1 after multiplying by −2. That is Eq. 32.4.8.

Both right-hand sides have the form c1log⁡λ1+S1/λ1 plus c2log⁡λ2+S2/λ2, a sum of two functions of separate arguments, each strictly convex in 1/λ and minimized at λ=S/c. So without the constraint λ1≥λ2, REML gives λ^1=SSB/(g−1)=MSB and λ^2=SSE/(n−g)=MSE, while maximum likelihood gives λ^1=SSB/g and the same λ^2. These are feasible exactly when λ^1≥λ^2, that is when MSB≥MSE for REML and when SSB/g=(g−1)MSB/g≥MSE, i.e. MSB≥gMSE/(g−1), for maximum likelihood. Translating back through σ2=λ2 and σa2=(λ1−λ2)/m gives (a) and (b), except for Eq. 32.4.9. For that, use EMSB=λ1 and EMSE=σ2: E{1m(g−1gMSB−MSE)}=1m(g−1gλ1−σ2)=σa2−λ1gm. For (c), suppose the unconstrained minimizer of one of the two objectives violates λ1≥λ2, which by the previous paragraph happens exactly on the stated range. Each objective is a sum of two terms, one in each argument, each strictly unimodal on (0,∞) and tending to +∞ at both ends, so a constrained minimum exists; were it interior to {λ1>λ2} the gradient would vanish there, making it the infeasible unconstrained minimizer. Hence it lies on the boundary λ1=λ2=λ, where the objective becomes clog⁡λ+(SSB+SSE)/λ with c=n for ML and c=n−1 for REML, minimized at the corresponding pooled mean square.

The bias −λ1/(gm) is λ1 divided by the total sample size: the price of the single degree of freedom used up by the grand mean, charged entirely to the between-group component. In the proficiency study, λ1=σ2+mσa2=0.252+4(0.20)2=0.2225 and gm=48, so the bias is −0.0046, which is 11.6 per cent of σa2. A simulation of 40000 studies reproduces it to the last recorded digit. Both the formula Eq. 32.4.9 and that simulation describe the interior expression of Proposition 32.4.3(b); truncating it at zero, as the estimator itself does, can only raise the estimate, so the estimator’s own bias is slightly smaller in modulus. Here the expression is negative in 2.0 per cent of the simulated studies and the truncated bias is −0.0045. With p fixed effects instead of one the same calculation gives a bias of order p/(gm), which is why REML matters most when the fixed-effects model is large relative to the data.

32.4.4. Score equations and algorithms

Proposition 32.4.4 (Score equations)

Let Vj=∂V/∂θj and P be as in Eq. 32.4.2. Then

∂ℓP∂θj=−12{tr(V−1Vj)−y⊤PVjPy},∂ℓR∂θj=−12{tr(PVj)−y⊤PVjPy},
(32.4.10)

The restricted score exceeds the profile score by 12tr{(X⊤V−1X)−X⊤V−1VjV−1X}, which is nonnegative whenever Vj is nonnegative definite, as it is for a variance-component parameterization V=∑jθjZjZj⊤.

Proof. For the profile, ∂log⁡detV/∂θj=tr(V−1Vj) and ∂(y⊤Py)/∂θj=−y⊤PVjPy by Lemma 32.4.1(c). For the restricted version, write ℓR through Eq. 32.4.4 with a fixed K: then ∂log⁡det(K⊤VK)/∂θj=tr{(K⊤VK)−1K⊤VjK}=tr(PVj) by Theorem 32.4.2(b) and the cyclic property of the trace (Theorem 1.6.1), and the quadratic term differentiates as before. Subtracting, and using Eq. 32.4.2 for P, gives the stated difference. Its value is the same for every generalized inverse, because C(X⊤V−1VjV−1X)⊂C(X⊤)=C(X⊤V−1X), so take the Moore–Penrose inverse, which is nonnegative definite; when Vj is nonnegative definite too, the difference is a trace of a product of two nonnegative definite matrices, hence nonnegative.

Setting Eq. 32.4.10 to zero gives equations that are not solvable in closed form outside balanced designs, and three families of algorithms are used.

Fisher scoring. The expected information for the restricted likelihood has entries 12tr(PVjPVk), so a Newton-type step costs one trace per pair of components. Convergence is fast near the maximum and unreliable far from it, and nothing keeps the iterates inside the parameter space.

Average information REML. Gilmour, Thompson and Cullis (1995) noticed that the average of the observed and expected information has entries 12y⊤PVjPVkPy, which need no traces at all. Average information REML is the workhorse of large animal-breeding and genomic analyses.

EM. Treat u as missing data (Dempster, Laird and Rubin, 1977). With R=σ2I and G=σu2Iq, the complete-data maximum likelihood estimates are averages of squares, so the E-step and M-step give the maximum likelihood update σu2,new=1q{‖u^‖2+trS}, 𝛃σ2,new=1n{‖y−Xβ^−Zu^‖2+tr(ZSZ⊤)}, because E(u⊤u∣y)=‖u^‖2+trS and 𝛆𝛆𝛃E(ε⊤ε∣y)=‖y−Xβ^−Zu^‖2+tr(ZSZ⊤), where S=Cov(u∣y)=(G−1+Z⊤R−1Z)−1=G−GZ⊤V−1ZG is the inverse of the trailing block of C in Eq. 32.3.5, the matrix the push-through identity Eq. 32.3.7 was applied to. It is not the trailing block of C−1 in Theorem 32.3.2(c), which carries a third term because 𝛃β has been estimated; that matrix gives an iteration maximizing neither likelihood. Each step is cheap, increases the likelihood and never leaves the parameter space; the price is slow convergence. A restricted version runs the same argument on the error contrasts. Laird and Ware (1982) introduced this algorithm to longitudinal data analysis, and it remains the most reliable starting procedure.

Warning. One starting value is not enough. Fit the Grunfeld panel of Example (Grunfeld’s panel with a random firm effect) with a random slope as well as a random intercept, on the logarithmic scale for the variances that keeps the iterates positive: a search begun with a negligible slope variance crawls along the boundary and stops at the random-intercept fit, with restricted log-likelihood −1176.53 against the maximum −1159.01, a gap of 17.52 reported as successful convergence. Here thirty random starts all reach the same maximum, which is some evidence that it is the only one; but such likelihoods can also be genuinely multimodal, increasingly so as the covariance structure grows (Searle, Casella and McCulloch, 1992).

32.4.5. The proficiency study, computed

Example (REML and ML for the twelve laboratories)

For the data of Example (A laboratory proficiency study), Proposition 32.4.3 gives σ^2=0.0620 by both methods, and σ^a,REML2=0.0386,σ^a,ML2=0.0341, the maximum likelihood estimate being 0.883 of the restricted one, because it replaces MSB by (g−1)MSB/g before subtracting MSE. The restricted log-likelihood at its maximum is −8.228 and the ordinary log-likelihood is −8.360; the two are not comparable, being likelihoods of 47 and 48 numbers respectively. Both agree with the general matrix formulas coded from Eq. 32.4.3 and Eq. 32.4.4, and with statsmodels’ MixedLM.

Figure 32.4.1 plots the two profiles against σa2, each with σ2 maximized out: the same shape in different places, the restricted one flatter on the right, which is why REML intervals are wider. Cutting the restricted profile 1.92 units below its maximum gives σa2∈(0.0086,0.1275), an interval comparable to the exact one of Corollary 32.1.2 and available in models where no exact interval exists.

import numpy as np

g, m = 12, 4
n = g * m
mu, sigma_a, sigma = 2.50, 0.20, 0.25           # the values used to simulate

rng = np.random.default_rng(20320048)
lab_effect = sigma_a * rng.standard_normal(g)
y = mu + np.repeat(lab_effect, m) + sigma * rng.standard_normal(n)

X = np.ones((n, 1))
Z = np.repeat(np.eye(g), m, axis=0)

def loglik(s2, s2a, reml=True):
    """Log-likelihood (reml=False) or restricted log-likelihood of the variances."""
    V = s2a * (Z @ Z.T) + s2 * np.eye(n)
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(V)
    Vi_X = np.linalg.solve(V, X)
    W = X.T @ Vi_X                              # X' V^{-1} X
    r = y - X @ np.linalg.solve(W, Vi_X.T @ y)  # GLS residual
    quad = r @ np.linalg.solve(V, r)            # = y' P y
    logdetV = 2 * np.sum(np.log(np.diag(L)))
    out = -0.5 * (n * np.log(2 * np.pi) + logdetV + quad)
    if reml:
        out += -0.5 * (np.linalg.slogdet(W)[1] - np.linalg.slogdet(X.T @ X)[1]
                       - X.shape[1] * np.log(2 * np.pi))
    return out
Y = y.reshape(g, m)
lab_mean = Y.mean(axis=1)
ms_between = m * np.sum((lab_mean - Y.mean()) ** 2) / (g - 1)
ms_within = np.sum((Y - lab_mean[:, None]) ** 2) / (n - g)

reml_s2, reml_s2a = ms_within, (ms_between - ms_within) / m
ml_s2 = ms_within
ml_s2a = ((g - 1) / g * ms_between - ms_within) / m

print(f"REML  sigma^2 {reml_s2:.4f}  sigma_a^2 {reml_s2a:.4f}")
print(f"ML    sigma^2 {ml_s2:.4f}  sigma_a^2 {ml_s2a:.4f}")
print("restricted log-likelihood", round(loglik(reml_s2, reml_s2a), 4))
print("log-likelihood           ", round(loglik(ml_s2, ml_s2a, reml=False), 4))

Two hundred EM steps from a deliberately poor start reach those maximum likelihood values to nine decimal places, which is what a correct S buys; the trailing block of C−1 in its place converges instead to (0.0644,0.0380), maximizing neither likelihood.

ZZt = Z @ Z.T
s2, s2a = 0.1, 0.1                              # a deliberately poor starting value
for _ in range(200):
    V = s2a * ZZt + s2 * np.eye(n)
    Vi = np.linalg.inv(V)
    W = X.T @ Vi @ X
    beta_hat = np.linalg.solve(W, X.T @ Vi @ y)
    u_hat = s2a * Z.T @ Vi @ (y - X @ beta_hat)
    S = np.linalg.inv(np.eye(g) / s2a + Z.T @ Z / s2)   # Cov(u | y), not (C^{-1})_22
    resid = y - X @ beta_hat - Z @ u_hat
    s2a = (u_hat @ u_hat + np.trace(S)) / g
    s2 = (resid @ resid + np.trace(Z @ S @ Z.T)) / n

print(f"EM    sigma^2 {s2:.4f}  sigma_a^2 {s2a:.4f}")

32.4.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Take V=σ2I and rank(X)=p. Show from Eq. 32.4.6 that −2ℓR=(n−p)log⁡σ2+SSE/σ2 up to constants, and deduce that the REML estimate of σ2 is s2. Then use Proposition 7.3.2 to find which multiple of SSE has the smallest mean squared error, and show that the maximum likelihood estimator beats s2 for some (n,p) and loses to it for others.

Solution. With V=σ2I: log⁡detV=nlog⁡σ2, y⊤Py=SSE/σ2 and det(X⊤V−1X)=σ−2pdet(X⊤X), so log⁡det(X⊤V−1X)−log⁡det(X⊤X)=−plog⁡σ2; adding this to −2ℓP=nlog⁡σ2+SSE/σ2 leaves (n−p)log⁡σ2+SSE/σ2. Its minimizer is SSE/(n−p)=s2. By Proposition 7.3.2 the best multiple of SSE is SSE/(n−p+2), which neither estimator in the question attains; writing k=n−p, the same proposition gives relative mean squared errors 2/k for s2 and (2k+p2)/n2 for SSE/n. Multiplying out n2=(k+p)2, the inequality (2k+p2)/n2≤2/k reduces to k(p−4)≤2p. So maximum likelihood wins for every p≤4, whatever n, and loses once p is moderately large: with n=100 and p=10 it gives (180+100)/1002=0.0280 against 2/90=0.0222. Fitting many fixed effects and then dividing by n is the bad case, which is the case REML exists for.

Exercise (B3)

Specialize the score equations Eq. 32.4.10 of Proposition 32.4.4 to the balanced one-way model with 𝛉θ=(λ1,λ2), for which V=λ1M+λ2(I−M), V1=M and V2=I−M. Show that the restricted score equations are λ^1=MSB and λ^2=MSE, recovering Proposition 32.4.3(a) without any optimization.

Solution. Here P=λ1−1(M−P1)+λ2−1(I−M), so PV1=λ1−1(M−P1) with trace (g−1)/λ1, and y⊤PV1Py=SSB/λ12. The first equation in Eq. 32.4.10 therefore reads (g−1)/λ1=SSB/λ12, that is λ1=SSB/(g−1). Likewise tr(PV2)=(n−g)/λ2 and y⊤PV2Py=SSE/λ22, giving λ2=SSE/(n−g). Using V−1 instead of P replaces g−1 by tr(V−1V1)λ1=g, which is the ML answer.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that 𝛃𝛉𝛃𝛉∫exp⁡{ℓ(β,θ)}dβ=exp⁡{ℓR(θ)} when rank(X)=p and the constant 12log⁡det(X⊤X) is dropped from Eq. 32.4.4. (Hint: complete the square in 𝛃β and use the normal integral.) This identity is the subject of Section 32.6; state in words what it says about REML.

Solution. Write 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃(y−Xβ)⊤V−1(y−Xβ)=y⊤Py+(β−β^)⊤X⊤V−1X(β−β^), which is the decomposition behind Lemma 32.4.1(b). Integrating 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃exp⁡{−12(β−β^)⊤X⊤V−1X(β−β^)} over Rp gives (2π)p/2det(X⊤V−1X)−1/2. Hence 𝛃∫eℓdβ=e−12{nlog⁡2π+log⁡detV+y⊤Py}×(2π)p/2det(X⊤V−1X)−1/2, whose logarithm is Eq. 32.4.6 without the det(X⊤X) term. In words: the restricted likelihood is the marginal likelihood of 𝛉θ after integrating the fixed effects out with a flat prior.

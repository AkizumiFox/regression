32.2  The linear mixed model

The one-way random effects model of Section 32.1 has one random factor and no regressors. Real studies have both: a trial has fixed treatment effects and random patient effects, a panel has fixed economic regressors and random firm effects, a field trial has fixed varieties and random blocks. The model that holds them all keeps the usual fixed part and adds a second design matrix whose coefficients are random.

32.2.1. Definition and the marginal model

Definition 32.2.1 (Linear mixed model)

Let X be a known n×p matrix and Z a known n×q matrix. The linear mixed model is

𝛃𝛆Y=Xβ+Zu+ε,
(32.2.1)

where 𝛃β∈Rp is a vector of unknown constants, the fixed effects; u is a q×1 random vector, the random effects, with E(u)=0 and Cov(u)=G; 𝛆ε is an n×1 random vector with 𝛆E(ε)=0 and 𝛆Cov(ε)=R; and 𝛆Cov(u,ε)=0. The matrices G and R are nonnegative definite and are known functions 𝛉G(θ), 𝛉R(θ) of an unknown parameter 𝛉θ, the variance components. The normal linear mixed model adds u∼Nq(0,G) independent of 𝛆ε∼Nn(0,R).

Taking expectations in Eq. 32.2.1 and using Theorem 2.2.1 gives the marginal model

𝛃𝛉E(Y)=Xβ,Cov(Y)=V(θ)=ZGZ⊤+R,
(32.2.2)

and under normality 𝛃Y∼Nn(Xβ,V). Everything Part VII knows about the general Gauss–Markov model applies to Eq. 32.2.2 at once. What distinguishes a mixed model from the general model of Chapter 31 is that V is not an arbitrary unknown matrix but is built from a handful of parameters by a structure the experiment dictates. An n×n covariance has n(n+1)/2 free entries; a random intercept model has two.

Remark (Conditional and marginal). Two means live in Eq. 32.2.1. Conditionally on the random effects, 𝛃E(Y∣u)=Xβ+Zu: this is what the subjects in this sample did. Marginally, 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ: this is what the population does on average. In the linear model with normal errors the two lead to the same 𝛃β, and the distinction looks pedantic. It is not pedantic at all once the link is nonlinear. For a generalized linear mixed model (Chapter 40) the marginal mean of 𝛃g−1(Xβ+Zu) is not 𝛃g−1(Xβ), and the subject-specific and population-averaged coefficients genuinely differ (Proposition 40.1.1).

Warning. The pair (G,R) is not identified by the marginal distribution; only V=ZGZ⊤+R is. In the one-way layout with Z the group indicators, the model with G=σa2Ig, R=σ2In has exactly the same marginal distribution as the model with no random effects at all and R=σ2I+σa2ZZ⊤, that is, with equicorrelated errors within groups. Every likelihood-based procedure in this chapter estimates V, so it cannot tell the two apart. What the random-effects reading adds is a target for prediction: the vector u, whose realized value Section 32.3 predicts. It also adds a constraint, since σa2≥0 forces a nonnegative intraclass correlation, whereas the equicorrelated model allows ρ down to −1/(m−1).

32.2.2. Inverting the covariance matrix

Formulas in this chapter are written with V−1, which is n×n. When q is much smaller than n, or when the data fall into independent clusters, V−1 never has to be formed.

Proposition 32.2.1 (The inverse and determinant of V)

Let R and G be positive definite in Definition 32.2.1. Then V is positive definite, and

V−1=R−1−R−1Z(G−1+Z⊤R−1Z)−1Z⊤R−1,
(32.2.3)
detV=detRdetGdet(G−1+Z⊤R−1Z).
(32.2.4)

If in addition the observations fall into g clusters with R=⨁kRk, Z=⨁kZk and G=⨁kGk block diagonal, the last saying that different clusters’ random effects are independent, then V=⨁k(ZkGkZk⊤+Rk) is block diagonal too, and everything decomposes cluster by cluster.

Proof. V=R+ZGZ⊤ is a sum of a positive definite and a nonnegative definite matrix, hence positive definite. Now use Theorem 1.6.5 with the roles B↦R, U↦Z, C↦G and (in the notation of that theorem) the second outer factor also Z. The matrix G−1+Z⊤R−1Z is positive definite, hence nonsingular, so the hypothesis holds and Eq. 1.6.6 is Eq. 32.2.3. For the determinant, Theorem 1.6.3(c) with B=R, U=ZG and the second factor Z⊤ gives detV=detRdet(Iq+Z⊤R−1ZG)=detRdetGdet(G−1+Z⊤R−1Z), the last step by det(Iq+AG)=det(G−1+A)detG. The block diagonal statement is immediate from the definitions.

A study of n=105 pupils in q=500 schools therefore needs a 500×500 inverse, not a 105×105 one, and if R is diagonal the rest of Eq. 32.2.3 costs nothing. Section 32.3 improves on this again.

32.2.3. Four examples

Example (Random intercepts)

Observations are grouped, group k contributing nk of them. Take Z to be the n×g matrix of group indicators, G=σa2Ig and R=σ2In, so that V=σ2I+σa2ZZ⊤ as in Proposition 32.1.1(a). The fixed part 𝛃Xβ may hold anything: an intercept alone, recovering Definition 32.1.1; a treatment indicator constant within group, as in a cluster-randomized trial; or regressors that vary within a group, as in a longitudinal study. Each group’s block of V is σ2Ink+σa211⊤, the compound symmetric or equicorrelated form.

Example (Random intercepts and slopes)

Subject i is measured at times ti1,…,tini, and each subject has its own intercept and its own slope in time: 𝛃Yij=x(ij)⊤β+u0i+u1itij+εij,(u0iu1i)∼N2(0,D), independently across subjects, with 𝛆εi∼Nni(0,σ2I). Here Zi=[1,ti], G=IN⊗D for N subjects, and subject i’s covariance block is Vi=ZiDZi⊤+σ2I, with entries Cov(Yij,Yij′)=d11+d12(tij+tij′)+d22tijtij′+σ21{j=j′}. Two features deserve notice. The variance of a response is quadratic in time, so a random slope model necessarily says that subjects spread apart, or, if d12<0, first converge and then spread. And the correlation between two occasions depends on both times, not only on their separation: it is not stationary. Whether this is the right shape is a modelling question that Chapter 33 takes up.

Example (Nested and crossed random factors)

With pupils inside classrooms inside schools, write Z=[Zsch,Zcls] and G=(σS2I00σC2I). Because every classroom belongs to exactly one school, V is block diagonal by school, and the analysis splits school by school; this is the covariance behind Exercise (C1). With subjects responding to items, each subject seeing every item, the two factors are crossed: Zsub and Zitem generate overlapping patterns and V has no block diagonal form at all. Nothing in the theory changes, but the computation does, and crossed random effects are the reason modern software stores Z sparsely.

Example (Grunfeld’s panel with a random firm effect)

Section 6.6 fitted gross investment on market value and capital stock for 11 US firms over 20 years, once with a separate intercept per firm (the within estimator) and once with a single intercept. The mixed model puts the firm effect in between: X holds the intercept, value and capital, Z holds the firm indicators, G=σf2I11 and R=σ2I. Fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (Section 32.4), it gives σ^2=2527.6 and σ^f2=6740.9, a firm standard deviation of 82.1 and an intraclass correlation of 0.727: most of the variation in investment is between firms. The coefficients are 0.1094 for value and 0.3082 for capital, against 0.1101 and 0.3100 for the within estimator and 0.1145 and 0.2275 with a single intercept. The mixed estimate has landed essentially on the within estimate, and Section 32.3 explains why: with 20 years per firm, the weight the model gives to between-firm comparisons is 1−0.9816, less than two per cent.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm

df = sm.datasets.grunfeld.load_pandas().data.sort_values(["firm", "year"])
firms = df["firm"].unique()
y = df["invest"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), df["value"], df["capital"]])
blocks = [np.where(df["firm"].to_numpy() == f)[0] for f in firms]
n, p, g = len(y), X.shape[1], len(firms)
print(f"{n} observations, {g} firms, {n // g} years each")

32.2.4. When ordinary least squares is already best

The marginal model Eq. 32.2.2 is a general Gauss–Markov model, so Corollary 7.2.3 says the best linear unbiased estimator of an estimable 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β is the generalized least squares estimate built from V−1, and Theorem 31.2.2 says that ordinary least squares achieves this for every y exactly when C(VX)⊆C(X). Mixed models supply the standard examples on both sides of that line.

Example (Cluster-constant regressors)

Take Example (Random intercepts) with a balanced layout, nk=m, and suppose every column of X is constant within groups, so that C(X)⊆C(Z). By Eq. 32.1.2, V=λ1M+σ2(I−M) with M the projection onto C(Z), so VX=λ1X and C(VX)=C(X). Ordinary least squares is therefore the best linear unbiased estimator, whatever the variance components are, and the analysis of a balanced cluster-randomized trial can be carried out on the cluster means without loss. The covariance of the estimate is not σ2(X⊤X)−1, however: it is λ1(X⊤X)−1, larger by the factor 1+mγ. Getting the point estimate right and the standard error wrong is the classic error in clustered data.

Example (Regressors that vary within a cluster)

Keep the same V, but let X=[1,x] with x varying within groups. Decomposing x into its between and within parts, Vx=λ1Mx+σ2(I−M)x, which lies in C(X) only if one of the two parts vanishes or λ1=σ2. Ordinary least squares is then not efficient, and the reason is visible: generalized least squares downweights the between-group part of x by σ2/λ1=1/(1+mγ) relative to the within-group part, because between-group comparisons carry the extra variance σa2. The mixed estimate is a compromise between the within estimator of Section 6.6, which uses only (I−M)x, and the between estimator, which uses only Mx; as mγ→∞ the weight on the between part goes to zero. Example (Grunfeld’s panel with a random firm effect) is that limit in practice.

Idea. A random effect is a statement about where the information is. Declaring the grouping factor random says that comparisons between groups carry an extra variance σa2 and should be downweighted accordingly, but not discarded. Fixed effects for the groups discard them entirely; ignoring the grouping keeps them at full weight. The mixed model interpolates, and the interpolation weight is estimated from the data.

The remaining difficulty is that V depends on the unknown 𝛉θ. The estimator actually used is the feasible generalized least squares estimator of Definition 31.4.1,

𝛃𝛉𝛉𝛉β^(θ^)=(X⊤V(θ^)−1X)−1X⊤V(θ^)−1Y,
(32.2.5)

with 𝛉θ^ from Section 32.4. Theorem 31.4.1 gives the large-sample justification; Section 32.5 asks what it costs in small samples, and the answer is: more than people usually allow for.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Specialize Eq. 32.2.3 to Example (Random intercepts) with group sizes nk, and show that the kth block of V−1 is 1σ2(Ink−σa2σ2+nkσa211⊤). Deduce that the generalized least squares criterion is σ−2∑k{∑jrkj2−wknkr¯k2} with wk=nkσa2/(σ2+nkσa2), and interpret wk.

Solution. Each block of V is σ2I+σa211⊤; apply Eq. 1.6.7 with B=σ2I, u=v=σa1: the correction is σa2σ−411⊤/(1+nkσa2/σ2), which rearranges to the stated form. The criterion r⊤V−1r then equals σ−2∑k{∑jrkj2−nkr¯k2wk} after writing 1⊤rk=nkr¯k. The weight wk∈(0,1) increases with nk: the model removes a fraction wk of the group’s mean residual from the criterion, because that part of the residual is explained by the group’s random effect rather than counted against the fit.

Exercise (B2)

Let V=λ1M+σ2(I−M) as in Eq. 32.1.2. Show that C(VX)⊆C(X) holds if and only if C(X) decomposes as (C(X)∩C(Z))⊕(C(X)∩C(Z)⊥), that is, iff M maps C(X) into itself. Give an X with both between and within columns for which ordinary least squares is best.

Solution. VX=σ2X+(λ1−σ2)MX, so (assuming λ1≠σ2) C(VX)⊆C(X) iff C(MX)⊆C(X), that is, M maps C(X) into itself. A subspace invariant under an orthogonal projection splits into its parts inside C(Z) and inside C(Z)⊥. Example: X=[1,x] with 1 constant within groups and x having zero mean within every group, so Mx=0; then MX=[1,0] lies in C(X). A balanced split-plot design has exactly this structure, which is why Chapter 33 can analyse it stratum by stratum.

31.1  Generalized least squares

The model of this chapter keeps the mean structure of every earlier chapter and loosens the second moments:

𝛃E(Y)=Xβ,Cov(Y)=σ2V,
(31.1.1)

where X is n×p of rank r, the matrix V is known and nonnegative definite, and σ2>0 is unknown. This section and the next assume V nonsingular; Section 31.3 removes that assumption.

31.1.1. What is assumed and what is not

The split of the covariance into a known matrix V and an unknown scalar σ2 is a convention, not extra knowledge. Only the product σ2V enters Eq. 31.1.1, so (σ2,V) and (σ2/c,cV) describe the same model for every c>0; it is usual to normalize V so that tr(V)/n=1, making σ2 the variance of a single observation on a reference scale. What the model really assumes is that the shape of the covariance — the ratios of variances and all the correlations — is known.

That is exactly true only when the design forces the shape, as when an observation is the mean of mi replicates and has variance σ2/mi (Section 21.3), or when the construction of the data does, as under the linear transformations of Section 31.3. Otherwise V belongs to a family 𝛉V(θ) estimated first and then treated as known, and this section’s exactness is a fiction whose price is computed in Section 31.4.

31.1.2. The estimator and the whitened model

Generalized least squares was defined in Definition 6.9.1 as projection in the V−1 inner product; we repeat it in the notation of this part.

Definition 31.1.1 (Generalized least squares)

Under Eq. 31.1.1 with V positive definite, a generalized least squares estimate is any 𝛃β^V minimizing QV(b)=(y−Xb)⊤V−1(y−Xb), equivalently any solution of the generalized normal equations X⊤V−1Xb=X⊤V−1y. The fitted vector is 𝛃Xβ^V=AVy, where

AV=X(X⊤V−1X)−X⊤V−1
(31.1.2)

for any generalized inverse, and the generalized residual is 𝛆𝛃ε^V=y−Xβ^V.

Everything algebraic about AV is Theorem 6.9.1 with A=V−1: the choice of generalized inverse is immaterial, it is idempotent with column space C(X), and V−1AV is symmetric although AV is not — in the Euclidean geometry it is an oblique projection (Proposition 6.9.4). Everything statistical comes from one change of variables. Let V−1/2 be the symmetric positive definite square root of V−1 (Theorem 1.7.6) and set

Y∗=V−1/2Y,X∗=V−1/2X.
(31.1.3)

By Theorem 2.2.1, 𝛃E(Y∗)=X∗β and Cov(Y∗)=σ2I: the whitened model satisfies the assumptions of Part II exactly. Since QV(b)=‖Y∗−X∗b‖2, generalized least squares for (Y,X) is ordinary least squares for (Y∗,X∗). Because V−1/2 is nonsingular, rank(X∗)=r and C(X∗⊤)=C(X⊤), so by Theorem 8.3.1 the two models have the same estimable functions: nothing about identifiability changes. Any nonsingular L with V=LL⊤ serves equally well, by Proposition 2.2.4(b); the Cholesky factor is the usual choice in computation.

Whitening is rarely carried out by forming V−1/2; structured V almost always has a shortcut. If the observations fall into G clusters of size m that share a random shock, so that V=(1−ρ)I+ρZZ⊤ with Z the n×G matrix of cluster indicators, then Theorem 1.6.5 gives

V−1=11−ρ(I−ρ1+(m−1)ρZZ⊤),
(31.1.4)

and the whitening is the quasi-demeaning

ykj ⟼ ykj−cy¯k1−ρ,c=1−1−ρ1+(m−1)ρ,
(31.1.5)

where y¯k is the mean of cluster k (Exercise (B1)). At ρ=0, c=0 and nothing happens; at ρ=0.5 with m=12, c=0.723; as ρ→1, c→1 and each cluster is fully centred, throwing away everything the shared shock contaminated. Generalized least squares interpolates, and Chapter 32 will recognize Eq. 31.1.5 as the transformation a random intercept induces.

31.1.3. Exact inference when the shape is known

Theorem 31.1.1 (Inference under a known covariance shape)

Let 𝛃Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2V) with V known and positive definite, r=rank(X)<n. Put

𝛆𝛆SSEV=ε^V⊤V−1ε^V=Y⊤V−1(I−AV)Y,sV2=SSEVn−r.
(31.1.6)

  • (a) For 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤), the statistic 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^V is the same for every solution of the generalized normal equations, and 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌λ⊤β^V∼N(λ⊤β, σ2λ⊤(X⊤V−1X)−λ). It is the best linear unbiased estimator of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β (Corollary 7.2.3).

  • (b) SSEV/σ2∼χ2(n−r), independently of 𝛃Xβ^V.

  • (c) E(sV2)=σ2 and Var(sV2)=2σ4/(n−r).

  • (d) For estimable 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β with 𝛌λ≠0, 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌λ⊤β^V−λ⊤βsVλ⊤(X⊤V−1X)−λ∼t(n−r).

  • (e) Let C(X0)⊆C(X) with rank(X0)=r0<r, and let SSEV,0 be Eq. 31.1.6 computed in the reduced model. Then

    F=(SSEV,0−SSEV)/(r−r0)sV2∼F(r−r0,n−r,γ),
    (31.1.7)

    with 𝛃γ=‖(M∗−M∗0)X∗β‖2/σ2, where M∗ and M∗0 are the orthogonal projections onto C(V−1/2X) and C(V−1/2X0). The noncentrality is zero iff 𝛃Xβ∈C(X0).

Proof. Pass to the whitened model Eq. 31.1.3, which satisfies the normal linear model Eq. 7.3.1 with model matrix X∗ of rank r. As shown above, 𝛃β^V is exactly a least squares estimate for (Y∗,X∗), and the two models have the same estimable functions. Moreover 𝛃SSEV=‖Y∗−X∗β^V‖2=‖(I−M∗)Y∗‖2 is the residual sum of squares of the whitened problem, because 𝛆V−1/2ε^V=(I−M∗)Y∗ by Theorem 6.4.1, and X⊤V−1X=X∗⊤X∗.

Now apply Theorem 7.5.1 to (Y∗,X∗). Part (a) of that theorem gives (a) here, since 𝛌𝛌𝛌𝛌λ⊤(X∗⊤X∗)−λ=λ⊤(X⊤V−1X)−λ and invariance of the estimate follows from Theorem 6.4.4 applied in the whitened model. Parts (b) and (c) of Theorem 7.5.1 give (b) here, noting that 𝛃Xβ^V=V1/2M∗Y∗ is a function of M∗Y∗, and part (d) gives (c). Part (d) follows from (a) and (b) exactly as Theorem 12.1.1 does in the Euclidean case. For (e), C(X0)⊆C(X) implies C(V−1/2X0)⊆C(V−1/2X) with ranks r0<r, so Definition 11.1.1 holds for the whitened pair and Theorem 11.1.1 applies; SSEV,0=‖(I−M∗0)Y∗‖2 by the same computation. The noncentrality Eq. 11.1.2 is 𝛃‖(M∗−M∗0)X∗β‖2/σ2, and it vanishes iff 𝛃X∗β∈C(X∗0), that is, iff 𝛃Xβ∈C(X0) since V−1/2 is nonsingular.

The theorem is not a new theory: it is Parts II and III read in another coordinate system, and the proof is nothing but the dictionary. Under normality 𝛃β^V also maximizes the likelihood (Exercise (B3)), and sV2 has smallest variance among unbiased estimates of σ2, by Section 7.4 applied to the whitened model.

31.1.4. What changes, and what does not

The dictionary is exact, but several habits from Parts II and III do not survive translation.

Warning. Under Eq. 31.1.1 with V≠I:

  • The generalized residual is not orthogonal to C(X); what holds is 𝛆X⊤V−1ε^V=0. The entries of 𝛆ε^V need not sum to zero even when the model has an intercept.
  • SSEV is not ∑i(yi−y^i)2, and the ordinary residual sum of squares of the generalized fit is necessarily larger than that of the ordinary fit, which minimizes it. A model comparison using ∑i(yi−y^i)2 always prefers ordinary least squares and proves nothing.
  • R2 has no agreed meaning: the squared-cosine reading of Theorem 6.7.2 holds in the V−1 geometry, not the Euclidean one.
  • The diagonal entries of AV still sum to r, but they are not leverages in the sense of Proposition 6.8.1 and can fall outside [0,1] (Exercise (B2)).

What survives is everything inference needs: unbiasedness, optimality, the χ2, t and F distributions, the degrees of freedom n−r, and γ as a squared distance from the reduced model space, measured now in the V−1 metric.

Example (A trend in sea surface temperature)

The El Niño data record the monthly averaged sea surface temperature of a region of the eastern Pacific for the 61 complete years 1950 to 2010, 732 observations in 61 blocks of 12. Fit a linear trend in time together with three harmonic pairs at one, two and three cycles per year, so p=8. Warm and cold years are warm and cold in every month, so the natural working model is a random annual shock: equicorrelation ρ inside each year, independence across years. Take ρ=0.5 as known for the moment.

Ordinary least squares gives a trend of 0.1349 degrees per decade with the usual standard error 0.0223; generalized least squares gives 0.1347 with standard error 0.0512, larger by a factor of 2.30. The estimates agree to three decimals, the standard errors not at all. The annual cycle tells the same story in reverse: the coefficient of sin⁡(2πmonth/12) is 2.7629 under both fits, with standard error 0.0554 from ordinary and 0.0354 from generalized least squares — this time the usual standard error is too large. Section 31.2 explains both facts.

The F test of “no trend” is 36.7 if the annual shock is ignored and 6.92 once it is allowed for, on 1 and 724 degrees of freedom (p=0.0087): still clear evidence, but with a tenth of the apparent weight. The test that the second and third harmonics are absent moves the other way, from 10.4 to 25.6, because the annual shock inflates s2 and so deflates every F whose numerator lives inside years. Figure 31.1.1(a) shows the annual mean residuals, far too spread out for independent errors, and panel (b) the two confidence bands.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats

frame = sm.datasets.elnino.load_pandas().data
years = frame["YEAR"].to_numpy()
temps = frame.iloc[:, 1:].to_numpy()                  # 61 years by 12 months
G, m = temps.shape
n = G * m
y = temps.ravel()                                     # the month varies fastest
year = np.repeat(years, m)
month = np.tile(np.arange(1, m + 1), G)
time = (year - 1980.0) + (month - 6.5) / 12.0         # decimal years, centred at 1980

cols = [np.ones(n), time / 10.0]                      # the trend is per decade
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    cols += [np.cos(2 * np.pi * k * month / m), np.sin(2 * np.pi * k * month / m)]
X = np.column_stack(cols)
p = X.shape[1]
rho = 0.5                                             # taken as known for now


def gls_fit(X, y, rho, m):
    """GLS with equicorrelation rho inside blocks of m consecutive observations.

    Whitening is the quasi-demeaning y -> (y - c ybar_g)/sqrt(1 - rho) with
    c = 1 - sqrt((1 - rho)/(1 + (m - 1) rho)); ordinary least squares on the whitened
    data is generalized least squares on the original data.
    """
    c = 1.0 - np.sqrt((1 - rho) / (1 + (m - 1) * rho))

    def whiten(a):
        b = a.reshape(len(a) // m, m, -1) if a.ndim > 1 else a.reshape(-1, m)
        return ((b - c * b.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)) / np.sqrt(1 - rho)).reshape(a.shape)

    Xw, yw = whiten(X), whiten(y)
    b = np.linalg.solve(Xw.T @ Xw, Xw.T @ yw)
    rss = float((yw - Xw @ b) @ (yw - Xw @ b))         # the V^{-1} residual sum of squares
    s2 = rss / (len(y) - X.shape[1])
    return b, s2 * np.linalg.inv(Xw.T @ Xw), rss, c


b_ols = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
rss_ols = float((y - X @ b_ols) @ (y - X @ b_ols))
V_ols = rss_ols / (n - p) * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)     # the standard errors usually reported
b_gls, V_gls, rss_gls, cq = gls_fit(X, y, rho, m)

print(f"trend per decade: OLS {b_ols[1]:+.4f}  GLS {b_gls[1]:+.4f}")
print(f"standard error:   OLS {np.sqrt(V_ols[1, 1]):.4f}  GLS {np.sqrt(V_gls[1, 1]):.4f}")
print(f"quasi-demeaning constant c = {cq:.4f},  s_GLS = {np.sqrt(rss_gls / (n - p)):.4f}")

The F test of Theorem 31.1.1(e) is computed exactly as in Part III: fit the full and the reduced model in the same geometry and compare their V−1 residual sums of squares. The listing does this for both hypotheses of Example (A trend in sea surface temperature), using ρ=0 to recover the ordinary analysis.

def f_test(X, y, drop, rho, m):
    """F test that the coefficients listed in `drop` are zero, in the rho-geometry."""
    keep = [j for j in range(X.shape[1]) if j not in drop]
    full, null = gls_fit(X, y, rho, m), gls_fit(X[:, keep], y, rho, m)
    df1, df2 = len(drop), len(y) - X.shape[1]
    F = (null[2] - full[2]) / df1 / (full[2] / df2)
    return F, stats.f.sf(F, df1, df2)


F_trend_gls, p_trend_gls = f_test(X, y, [1], rho, m)
F_trend_ols, p_trend_ols = f_test(X, y, [1], 0.0, m)   # rho = 0 is ordinary least squares
F_harm_gls, p_harm_gls = f_test(X, y, [4, 5, 6, 7], rho, m)
F_harm_ols, p_harm_ols = f_test(X, y, [4, 5, 6, 7], 0.0, m)
print(f"trend:      F = {F_trend_ols:7.2f} (rho = 0)   F = {F_trend_gls:6.2f} (rho = 0.5)")
print(f"harmonics:  F = {F_harm_ols:7.2f} (rho = 0)   F = {F_harm_gls:6.2f} (rho = 0.5)")

Idea. Generalized least squares is one change of coordinates away from ordinary least squares, and everything in Parts II and III transfers — provided all of it does: the estimate, the residual sum of squares, the degrees of freedom and the sums of squares of every hypothesis. Mixing geometries, as in a generalized estimate with an ordinary standard error, is the one way to get this wrong.

31.1.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that replacing (σ2,V) by (σ2/c,cV) for c>0 leaves 𝛃β^V, AV and every statistic in Theorem 31.1.1(d), (e) unchanged, and multiplies SSEV and sV2 by 1/c. Conclude that no normalization of V affects any inference about 𝛃β.

Solution. The criterion QcV(b)=QV(b)/c is minimized at the same b, and (cV)−1=V−1/c cancels from Eq. 31.1.2. Then SSEcV=SSEV/c and 𝛌𝛌𝛌𝛌λ⊤(X⊤(cV)−1X)−λ=cλ⊤(X⊤V−1X)−λ, so the factors c cancel in the t statistic; in F they cancel between numerator and denominator.

Exercise (A2)

Show that 𝛆1⊤ε^V=0 for every y iff 1∈C(V−1X). Give an example with an intercept in which this fails.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let V0=(1−ρ)Im+ρ11⊤ with −1/(m−1)<ρ<1. Verify Eq. 31.1.4 by Theorem 1.6.5, and show that L0=(Im−c11⊤/m)/1−ρ satisfies L0V0L0⊤=Im for c=1−(1−ρ)/(1+(m−1)ρ). Deduce Eq. 31.1.5.

Solution. Write P=11⊤/m, which is idempotent, so V0=(1−ρ)I+ρmP. For any c, (I−cP)V0(I−cP)=(1−ρ)I+[(1−ρ)(c2−2c)+ρm(1−c)2]P, using P2=P. Setting a=1−ρ and b=ρm, the bracket vanishes iff (a+b)c2−2(a+b)c+b=0, that is c2−2c+b/(a+b)=0, whose root in [0,1) is c=1−a/(a+b). Dividing by 1−ρ gives L0V0L0⊤=I. The Woodbury identity with U=1 gives Eq. 31.1.4 directly. Applying L0 block by block is Eq. 31.1.5, since 11⊤yk/m=y¯k1.

Exercise (B2)

Show that trAV=r for every positive definite V. Then take n=2, p=1, X=(12),V=(10.90.91), and show that the diagonal of AV is (−4/7,11/7). Why does this not contradict Proposition 6.8.1?

Exercise (B3)

Show that 𝛆𝛆𝛆𝛆ε^V⊤ε^V≥ε^⊤ε^, where 𝛆ε^=(I−M)y is the ordinary residual, with equality iff 𝛃Xβ^V=My. Show also that 𝛆𝛆𝛆𝛆ε^⊤V−1ε^≥ε^V⊤V−1ε^V. Explain why neither inequality says anything about which fit is better.

Exercise (B4)

Observations Ykj, j=1,…,mk, k=1,…,G, have mean μ and covariance σ2V with V=(1−ρ)I+ρZZ⊤, Z the cluster indicator matrix. Using Eq. 31.1.4 cluster by cluster, show that

μ^V=∑kwkY¯k∑kwk,wk=mk1−ρ+mkρ,
(31.1.8)

with Var(μ^V)=σ2/∑kwk. Interpret the weights as ρ→0 and ρ→1.

Solution. With V block diagonal, V−1 is block diagonal with blocks (I−ρ1−ρ+mkρ11⊤)/(1−ρ). Hence 1⊤V−11=∑k(mk−ρmk2/(1−ρ+mkρ))/(1−ρ)=∑kwk, and 1⊤V−1y=∑kwky¯k by the same computation. The generalized normal equation is (1⊤V−11)μ=1⊤V−1y. As ρ→0, wk→mk and μ^V→y¯; as ρ→1, wk→1 and every cluster counts once, however large, because the extra observations only repeat the same shock.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Suppose (Y⊤,Y0)⊤ is jointly distributed with 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ, 𝛃E(Y0)=x0⊤β, Cov(Y)=σ2V, Cov(Y,Y0)=σ2c and Var(Y0)=σ2v0, with V positive definite and rank(X)=p. Among predictors a⊤Y with E(a⊤Y−Y0)=0 for all 𝛃β, show that the one minimizing E(a⊤Y−Y0)2 is 𝛃𝛃Y^0=x0⊤β^V+c⊤V−1(Y−Xβ^V), with prediction error variance σ2[v0−c⊤V−1c+d⊤(X⊤V−1X)−1d], d=x0−X⊤V−1c. This is Goldberger’s formula; Chapter 32 recognizes it as the best linear unbiased predictor of a random effect.

Solution. Unbiasedness requires X⊤a=x0, and E(a⊤Y−Y0)2=σ2(a⊤Va−2a⊤c+v0). By Proposition 1.11.3 with the positive definite V, the minimizer is a=V−1c+V−1X(X⊤V−1X)−1d, and collecting terms gives 𝛃𝛃a⊤Y=x0⊤β^V+c⊤V−1(Y−Xβ^V); the minimum value is the stated variance. Setting c=0 recovers the ordinary prediction of Chapter 12. A correlated future observation is predicted by correcting the fitted mean with the part of the current residual that predicts it.

Exercise (C2)

Let 𝛃𝛉Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2V(θ)) with rank(X)=p. Show that for each fixed 𝛉θ the log-likelihood is maximized over 𝛃(β,σ2) at 𝛃𝛉β^V(θ) and 𝛉𝛉σ^2(θ)=SSEV(θ)/n, and that the profile log-likelihood is 𝛉𝛉𝛉ℓp(θ)=−n2log⁡σ^2(θ)−12log⁡detV(θ)+const. Why does the determinant term make the normalization of V matter here, when Exercise (A1) says it does not matter for inference about 𝛃β?

30.4  Componentwise boosting

Boosting is an algorithm, not a criterion: it improves the fit by many small steps, each changing the single coefficient that helps most, and the number of steps plays the part of a penalty. Begun as a way to combine classifiers (Freund and Schapire, 1997) and recast as gradient descent on a loss (Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani, 2000; Friedman, 2001), with squared error and single regressors it is the L2 boosting of Bühlmann and Yu (2003).

30.4.1. The algorithm

Let y and the columns of X be centred, and let no column be zero. For a vector r, the least squares fit of r on the single column xj has coefficient xj⊤r/‖xj‖2. It reduces the sum of squares by (xj⊤r)2/‖xj‖2, by the one-column case of Theorem 6.4.1.

Definition 30.4.1 (Componentwise L2 boosting)

Fix a step size ν∈(0,2). Start from b(0)=0 and r(0)=y. For m=0,1,2,…:

  1. choose jm=argmaxj(xj⊤r(m))2/‖xj‖2, the column whose simple regression fits the current residual best;
  2. set bjm(m+1)=bjm(m)+νxjm⊤r(m)/‖xjm‖2, leave the other coordinates unchanged, and put r(m+1)=y−Xb(m+1).

The estimate after m steps is b(m), and the stopping iteration m is the tuning parameter.

With unit-length columns, step 1 picks the column most correlated with the residual. The residual is the negative gradient of 12‖y−f‖2 in the fitted vector f, so each step fits the negative gradient by the best single column and moves a fraction ν of the way. The theory below allows ν<2; with ν>1 a step overshoots the simple regression fit, and in practice ν≤1, often ν=0.1. With ν=1 this is matching pursuit (Mallat and Zhang, 1993), which is not forward selection, since earlier coefficients are never refitted.

30.4.2. Convergence to least squares

Boosting converges to the least squares fit at a geometric rate, slow when the design is badly conditioned.

Theorem 30.4.1 (Convergence of componentwise L2 boosting)

Let ν∈(0,2), let Q(b)=12‖y−Xb‖2 with minimum Q∗, let s>0 be the smallest nonzero singular value of X, and put κ=ν(2−ν)s2pmaxj‖xj‖2. Then 0<κ≤1, and:

  • (a) each step lowers the residual sum of squares by exactly

    Q(b(m))−Q(b(m+1))=ν(1−ν2)maxj(xj⊤r(m))2‖xj‖2;
    (30.4.1)

  • (b) Q(b(m))−Q∗≤(1−κ)m(Q(0)−Q∗). Hence the fitted values converge geometrically to the least squares fit: ‖Xb(m)−My‖2=2{Q(b(m))−Q∗}→0;

  • (c) the coefficients b(m) converge to a least squares estimate. If X has full column rank, the limit is 𝛃β^.

Proof.

  • (a) Write r=r(m), j=jm and c=xj⊤r/‖xj‖2. Then r(m+1)=r−νcxj, and ‖r−νcxj‖2=‖r‖2−2νcxj⊤r+ν2c2‖xj‖2=‖r‖2−(2ν−ν2)(xj⊤r)2‖xj‖2. Halving gives Eq. 30.4.1, since j maximizes the ratio.

  • (b) Let um=Mr(m)=My−Xb(m), which lies in C(X). The residual splits orthogonally as r(m)=(I−M)y+um, so Q(b(m))−Q∗=12‖um‖2 and X⊤r(m)=X⊤um. By the singular value decomposition (Theorem 1.8.1), ‖X⊤u‖≥s‖u‖ for every u∈C(X). A maximum is at least an average, so maxj(xj⊤r(m))2‖xj‖2≥∑j(xj⊤um)2pmaxj‖xj‖2=‖X⊤um‖2pmaxj‖xj‖2≥s2‖um‖2pmaxj‖xj‖2. With (a), Q(b(m))−Q(b(m+1))≥κ{Q(b(m))−Q∗}, that is, Q(b(m+1))−Q∗≤(1−κ){Q(b(m))−Q∗}. Iterating gives (b). Finally κ>0, and κ≤1 because ν(2−ν)≤1 and s2≤tr(X⊤X)=∑j‖xj‖2≤pmaxj‖xj‖2.

  • (c) The mth step changes one coordinate, by ν|xjm⊤um|/‖xjm‖2≤ν‖xjm‖‖um‖/‖xjm‖2≤ν‖um‖/minj‖xj‖, using Cauchy–Schwarz. By (b), ‖um‖≤(1−κ)m/2‖u0‖, which is summable. So ∑m‖b(m+1)−b(m)‖<∞, the sequence is Cauchy, and it converges to some b∞. By continuity, Xb∞=limXb(m)=My, so b∞ is a least squares estimate (Theorem 6.4.1). With full column rank there is only one.

The bound is crude, but it shows what controls the speed: the step size and the conditioning of X.

Remark (When p>n). If rankX=n, as is typical when p>n, boosting run long enough interpolates the data, so stopping early is a necessity. Its limit depends on the order in which columns were chosen and is in general not the least squares estimate of smallest norm (Proposition 6.10.1), which full gradient descent from zero does reach (Exercise (B2); this is the ridgeless estimator of Theorem 28.1.1).

30.4.3. Early stopping as shrinkage

For a base learner linear in the data, the effect of stopping early is exact. Let an n×n matrix S map a residual vector to its fitted values; boosting updates f(m+1)=f(m)+νS(y−f(m)) from f(0)=0.

Proposition 30.4.2 (The boosting operator)

  • (a) f(m)=Bmy with Bm=I−(I−νS)m.

  • (b) Let S be symmetric with eigenvalues dk≥0 and orthonormal eigenvectors uk, and let νdk≤1 for every k. Then Bm=∑k{1−(1−νdk)m}ukuk⊤. Each factor 1−(1−νdk)m lies in [0,1], is nondecreasing in m, and tends to 1 when dk>0. The trace trBm increases with m towards rankS.

  • (c) (Least squares learner.) If S=M, then Bm={1−(1−ν)m}M. Early stopping shrinks the least squares fit by a single factor.

  • (d) (Gradient descent.) The iteration b(m+1)=b(m)+νX⊤(y−Xb(m)) from b(0)=0 is the case S=XX⊤. If X has singular values σk and νσk2≤1, its fitted values shrink the kth singular component of the least squares fit by 1−(1−νσk2)m. Ridge regression shrinks the same component by σk2/(σk2+λ).

Proof.

  • (a) y−f(m+1)=(I−νS)(y−f(m)), so y−f(m)=(I−νS)my. (b) (I−νS)m=∑k(1−νdk)mukuk⊤ by the spectral theorem (Theorem 1.7.2), and 0≤1−νdk≤1. (c) M has eigenvalues 0 and 1, and it is idempotent.
  • (b) Xb(m+1)=Xb(m)+νXX⊤(y−Xb(m)). The eigenvalues of XX⊤ are the σk2 and zeros, and the eigenvectors for the σk2 are the left singular vectors (Theorem 1.8.1). For ridge, see Theorem 27.2.2.

For small σk2, gradient descent keeps the fraction 1−(1−νσk2)m≈mνσk2 of a component and ridge keeps ≈σk2/λ; for large σk2 both keep nearly everything. So m steps behave like ridge with λ≈1/(mν). Part (c) is the proportional shrinkage of Proposition 7.6.3(c).

Degrees of freedom. For a linear smoother the complexity is trBm, which equals the covariance penalty σ−2∑iCov(y^i,yi) (Theorem 29.1.1). Componentwise boosting is not linear, since the chosen columns depend on y, but for a fixed sequence j0,…,jm−1 its fitted values are Bmy with

Bm=I−(I−νHjm−1)⋯(I−νHj0),Hj=xjxj⊤‖xj‖2,
(30.4.2)

by Proposition 30.4.2(a) applied step by step. Bühlmann (2006) used trBm along the observed sequence as the degrees of freedom in a corrected Akaike criterion for choosing m. That treats the selection as fixed in advance, and the next example shows how much it can understate.

Example (Boosting a sparse regression)

The script draws n=60 observations of p=30 normal regressors with correlations 0.5|j−k|, five nonzero coefficients and σ=1.5, and boosts on the standardized regressors. The expected squared error on a new case (the risk) is computed exactly from the design law; its floor is σ2=2.25.

Convergence. For ν=0.1, κ=3.18×10−4, and 𝛃𝛃‖b(m)−β^‖/‖β^‖ is 0.497, 0.161 and 2.7×10−8 after 100, 1000 and 50000 steps; the script checks Eq. 30.4.1 and the bound (b) at every step.

Early stopping. Least squares has risk 4.046. With ν=0.1 the risk is smallest, 2.981, after 108 steps (with 14 nonzero coefficients), and within 5 per cent of that from m=72 to m=305; five-fold cross-validation chooses m=229, with risk 3.047. With ν=1 the best risk, 2.893, occurs at m=4 and only there is the risk within 5 per cent of it: a needle that a data-based stopping rule is unlikely to hit (Figure 30.4.1).

Degrees of freedom. Over 2000 responses with the same design and mean, the covariance degrees of freedom after m=20 steps are 3.1 against an average trace of 1.5, and after m=120 steps 12.6 against 6.2. The trace does not see the adaptation of the chosen columns to the noise.

import numpy as np

def l2_boost(X, y, nu, steps):
    """Componentwise L2 boosting from b = 0; returns the coefficient path (steps + 1) x p."""
    n, p = X.shape
    norms2 = np.sum(X ** 2, axis=0)
    b = np.zeros(p)
    r = y.astype(float).copy()
    path = [b.copy()]
    for _ in range(steps):
        corr = X.T @ r
        j = np.argmax(corr ** 2 / norms2)               # largest drop in the residual sum of squares
        step = nu * corr[j] / norms2[j]
        b[j] += step
        r -= step * X[:, j]
        path.append(b.copy())
    return np.array(path)

rng = np.random.default_rng(3004)
n, p, sigma = 60, 30, 1.5
C = 0.5 ** np.abs(np.subtract.outer(np.arange(p), np.arange(p)))
L = np.linalg.cholesky(C)
beta = np.zeros(p)
beta[[0, 4, 9, 14, 19]] = [2.0, -1.5, 1.0, 1.0, -0.5]

def draw(m):
    Z = rng.normal(size=(m, p)) @ L.T
    return Z, Z @ beta + sigma * rng.normal(size=m)

X, y = draw(n)
mx, sx, my = X.mean(axis=0), X.std(axis=0), y.mean()
Xs, ys = (X - mx) / sx, y - my                          # centre and scale by the training data

def risk(path):
    """Expected squared error on a new case, for each row b of path (exact, from the design law)."""
    d = beta[None, :] - path / sx                       # coefficient error on the original scale
    offset = path @ (mx / sx) - my                      # error in the fitted intercept
    return np.sum((d @ C) * d, axis=1) + offset ** 2 + sigma ** 2

b_ls = np.linalg.lstsq(Xs, ys, rcond=None)[0]
M = 1500
test_err = {}
for step in [0.1, 1.0]:
    test_err[step] = risk(l2_boost(Xs, ys, step, M))
err_ls = risk(b_ls[None, :])[0]
m_best = int(np.argmin(test_err[0.1]))
print(f"least squares: risk {err_ls:.3f}")
print(f"nu = 0.1: best m = {m_best}, risk {test_err[0.1][m_best]:.3f}")
print(f"nu = 1.0: best m = {np.argmin(test_err[1.0])}, risk {test_err[1.0].min():.3f}")

The next cell repeats the comparison with 300 responses and m=20; the script’s run of it gives 3.2 against 1.5.

mu = Xs @ (beta * sx)                                    # the true mean, in the centred scale
def operator_trace(X, path, nu):
    """Trace of B_m = I - prod_k (I - nu H_{j_k}), with the selected columns held fixed."""
    n = X.shape[0]
    Rm = np.eye(n)
    for k in range(len(path) - 1):
        j = int(np.flatnonzero(path[k + 1] != path[k])[0])
        x = X[:, j]
        Rm -= nu * np.outer(x, x @ Rm) / (x @ x)
    return n - np.trace(Rm)

reps, m_df = 300, 20
rng_df = np.random.default_rng(30041)
Y = mu + sigma * rng_df.normal(size=(reps, n))            # repeated responses, same design and mean
paths = [l2_boost(Xs, Y[r], 0.1, m_df) for r in range(reps)]
F = np.array([Xs @ P[-1] for P in paths])                 # fitted values after m_df steps
cov_df = np.sum(np.mean((Y - Y.mean(axis=0)) * (F - F.mean(axis=0)), axis=0)) / sigma ** 2
trace_df = np.mean([operator_trace(Xs, P, 0.1) for P in paths])
print(f"m = {m_df}: mean trace {trace_df:.1f}, covariance df {cov_df:.1f}")

The gap is the optimism of selection (Proposition 29.5.1). For the lasso, by contrast, the covariance degrees of freedom equal the expected number of nonzero coefficients (Zou, Hastie and Tibshirani, 2007; we do not reproduce the proof): shrinkage offsets the cost of selection (compare Exercise (C1)). In practice m is chosen by cross-validation (Chapter 29), which needs no degrees of freedom.

30.4.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Let X⊤X=I, z=X⊤y=(3,−1,0.5)⊤ and ν=0.1. Carry out the first three boosting steps by hand. Show that under an orthonormal design the current residual correlations are z−b(m), so boosting always moves the coordinate whose remaining correlation is largest in absolute value, and removes one tenth of that correlation.

Solution. X⊤r(m)=X⊤y−X⊤Xb(m)=z−b(m), with unit column lengths. Step 1: coordinate 1, b1=0.3, residual correlation 2.7. Step 2: coordinate 1 again, b1=0.3+0.27=0.57, correlation 2.43. Step 3: coordinate 1, b1=0.57+0.243=0.813. Coordinate 1 keeps being chosen until its remaining correlation 3(0.9)m falls below 1, which first happens at m=11. This is the approach to soft thresholding that Section 30.5 makes precise.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Use Eq. 30.4.1 to show that ∑m≥0maxj(xj⊤r(m))2/‖xj‖2≤‖y‖2/{ν(2−ν)}. Deduce, without the singular value argument, that X⊤r(m)→0 for every ν∈(0,2).

Solution. Summing Eq. 30.4.1 over m=0,…,M−1 gives ν(1−ν/2)∑m<Mmaxj(⋯)=Q(0)−Q(b(M))≤12‖y‖2. Multiply by 2/{ν(2−ν)} and let M→∞. The terms of a convergent series tend to zero. So maxj|xj⊤r(m)|/‖xj‖→0, and hence every coordinate of X⊤r(m) does.

Exercise (B2)

For the gradient descent iteration of Proposition 30.4.2(d) with 0<ν<2/σ12, show that every b(m) lies in C(X⊤), and that b(m)→X+y, the minimum-norm least squares estimate (Proposition 6.10.1). Why does this argument fail for componentwise boosting?

Solution. Each increment νX⊤(y−Xb(m)) lies in C(X⊤), and b(0)=0. In the singular value basis, b(m)=∑k{1−(1−νσk2)m}σk−1(uk⊤y)vk, because the iteration acts on each component separately. With |1−νσk2|<1 this converges to ∑kσk−1(uk⊤y)vk=X+y. Componentwise boosting adds multiples of coordinate vectors ej, which need not lie in C(X⊤) when rankX<p. So its limit can have a component in N(X).

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Under an orthonormal design with 𝛃y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I), the lasso fit with parameter λ has covariance degrees of freedom E#{j:|zj|>λ} (by Stein’s lemma, Lemma 27.5.1, applied coordinatewise). Show that hard thresholding at the same λ has covariance degrees of freedom E#{j:|zj|>λ}+∑j(λ/σ){ϕ((λ−βj)/σ)+ϕ((λ+βj)/σ)}, where ϕ is the standard normal density.

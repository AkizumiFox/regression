11.4  The likelihood ratio test and its relatives

The F test was motivated in Section 11.1 by a heuristic: compare the extra sum of squares with the noise level. General statistical theory offers other routes to a test, chiefly the likelihood ratio, the Wald statistic and the score statistic. In the normal linear model all three routes lead back to F. They differ only when they are calibrated by their common large-sample χ2 approximation instead of the exact distribution, and then they can disagree about the same data. This section derives all three, shows how different the approximate versions can be, and then proves that the F test is the best test among those that respect the symmetries of Section 11.2.

11.4.1. The likelihood ratio

For a hypothesis H0 about the parameter of a family of densities 𝛉L(θ;y), the likelihood ratio is 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉Λ(y)=supθ∈H0L(θ;y)supθL(θ;y), and the likelihood ratio test rejects when Λ is small, that is, when the best explanation the hypothesis can offer is much worse than the best explanation overall. In the linear model both suprema are available in closed form from Theorem 7.3.1, and Exercise (A2) already carried out the computation for reduced models. The theorem records it for testable hypotheses, including the calibration.

Theorem 11.4.1 (The F test is the likelihood ratio test)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with r<n, and let 𝛃H:Λ⊤β=d be testable with q=rank(Λ). Let SSEH=minΛ⊤b=d‖y−Xb‖2 and suppose SSE>0, which holds with probability one. Then:

  • (a) under H the likelihood is maximized at any minimizer 𝛃β^H of ‖y−Xb‖2 subject to Λ⊤b=d (for Λ of full column rank, the estimate of Proposition 11.3.3; compare Theorem 9.2.5), with σ~H2=SSEH/n;

  • (b) the likelihood ratio is

    Λ=(SSESSEH)n/2=(1+qFHn−r)−n/2,
    (11.4.1)

    a strictly decreasing function of the statistic FH of Eq. 11.3.1;

  • (c) the likelihood ratio test of size α rejects exactly when FH>Fα(q,n−r).

The same holds for a reduced model E(Y)∈C(X0), with q=r−r0 and the statistic of Theorem 11.1.1.

Proof.

  • (a) By Theorem 11.3.2(b), H holds iff E(Y)∈Xb0+S0. Maximizing the likelihood over H is therefore maximizing it in the linear model Y−Xb0∼Nn(m,σ2I), m∈S0. By Theorem 7.3.1 applied to that model (with any matrix whose columns span S0), the maximizing mean is the least squares fit and the maximizing variance is its residual sum of squares divided by n. That fit is PS0(y−Xb0), so the maximizing mean of Y is Xb0+PS0(y−Xb0), the point of Xb0+S0 nearest to y. As shown in the proof of Theorem 11.3.2(b), no rank condition on Λ is needed for this. That point is Xb exactly for the minimizers b of ‖y−Xb‖2 subject to Λ⊤b=d, so it is 𝛃Xβ^H, and its residual sum of squares is SSEH. This needs SSEH>0, which follows from SSEH≥SSE>0.

  • (b) By Theorem 7.3.1 the two maxima are (2πeSSE/n)−n/2 and (2πeSSEH/n)−n/2. Their ratio is (SSE/SSEH)n/2, and SSEH=SSE+SSH=SSE{1+qFH/(n−r)} by Theorem 11.3.2(b).

  • (c) By (b), Λ≤k iff FH≥c for a constant c determined by k. By Theorem 11.3.2(c) the null distribution of FH is F(q,n−r) at every point of H, so size α requires c=Fα(q,n−r).

The large-sample theory of likelihood ratios (Wilks’s theorem) would calibrate −2log⁡Λ=nlog⁡{1+qFH/(n−r)} by the χ2(q) distribution. That calibration is only approximate. In the normal linear model the exact distribution is available through FH and should be used.

11.4.2. Wald and score statistics

Two other large-sample recipes are in wide use. The Wald statistic measures the estimated departure 𝛃Λ⊤β^−d against its estimated covariance, both computed at the unrestricted maximum likelihood estimate. The score statistic, also called the Lagrange multiplier statistic, measures the gradient of the log-likelihood at the restricted maximum likelihood estimate against the information there. In the linear model, with Λ of full column rank q and the maximum likelihood variance estimates σ^2=SSE/n and σ~H2=SSEH/n, they are 𝛃𝛃Wald=(Λ⊤β^−d)⊤(Λ⊤GΛ)−1(Λ⊤β^−d)σ^2 and Score=U⊤(X⊤Xσ~H2)−U=σ~H2U⊤(X⊤X)−U, where 𝛃U=X⊤(y−Xβ^H)/σ~H2. Here U is the gradient of the log-likelihood Eq. 7.3.1 with respect to 𝛃β at 𝛃(β^H,σ~H2), and X⊤X/σ~H2 is the corresponding block of the Fisher information. The gradient with respect to σ2 vanishes at the restricted maximum, and the information matrix is block diagonal, because 𝛃E{X⊤(Y−Xβ)}=0. So the σ2 coordinates contribute nothing, and the formula is the full score statistic. The generalized inverse is harmless because U∈C(X⊤)=C(X⊤X).

Proposition 11.4.2 (Wald, likelihood ratio and score)

In the setting above, let u=SSH/SSE=qFH/(n−r). Then Wald=nu,−2log⁡Λ=nlog⁡(1+u),Score=nu1+u. Each is a strictly increasing function of FH, so the three tests, calibrated exactly, coincide with the F test. For every sample with SSH>0, Wald>−2log⁡Λ>Score. If each is compared with the upper α point c of χ2(q), the exact sizes are Pr{F(q,n−r)>n−rquc},uc=cn,ec/n−1,cn−c  (n>c) for the Wald, likelihood ratio and score tests respectively.

Proof. With σ^2=SSE/n, Wald=nSSH/SSE=nu by Eq. 11.3.1. For the score, 𝛃X⊤y=X⊤Xβ^, so 𝛃𝛃𝛃X⊤(y−Xβ^H)=X⊤X(β^−β^H) and 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃U⊤(X⊤X)−U⋅σ~H2=(β^−β^H)⊤X⊤X(X⊤X)−X⊤X(β^−β^H)σ~H2=‖X(β^−β^H)‖2σ~H2. By the proof of Proposition 11.3.3, 𝛃𝛃‖X(β^−β^H)‖2=SSH, so Score=nSSH/SSEH=nu/(1+u). The likelihood ratio is Eq. 11.4.1. All three are increasing in u, which is proportional to FH. For u>0, log⁡(1+u)=∫0u(1+t)−1dt lies strictly between u/(1+u) and u, since the integrand lies strictly between (1+u)−1 and 1 on (0,u). Finally, each statistic exceeds c iff u>uc, with uc as stated (for the score, nu/(1+u)>c iff u(n−c)>c, and it never exceeds c when n≤c), and u>uc iff FH>(n−r)uc/q.

The inequality Wald≥LR≥Score is a general feature of linear restrictions in normal regression, and it has a practical consequence: with χ2 critical values, the Wald test rejects most often and the score test least often, on the same data.

Example (Three verdicts from one data set)

For the region test of Example (Do regions matter?), n=50, q=3 and n−r=43. The three statistics are

Statistic Value p-value from χ2(3)
Wald 8.512 0.0365
Likelihood ratio, −2log⁡Λ 7.861 0.0490
Score 7.274 0.0637
F=2.440, exact 0.0773

With the 5% critical value 7.815 of χ2(3), the Wald test rejects, the likelihood ratio test rejects barely, and the score test does not. The exact test, which all three approximate, does not reject. The disagreement is not a paradox. The approximate tests are different tests, and their true sizes differ from 0.05. With q=3 and r=7, the formulas of Proposition 11.4.2 give

n 15 30 50 100 400
Wald 0.315 0.143 0.097 0.071 0.055
Likelihood ratio 0.221 0.106 0.079 0.063 0.053
Score 0.102 0.069 0.060 0.055 0.051

At the sample size of the example the nominal 5% Wald test has true size close to 10%. All three sizes approach 0.05 as n grows, but slowly when r is not small compared with n.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats

data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data
data.index = data.index.str.strip()
data = data.drop(index="District of Columbia")
codes = "SWWSWWNSSSWWMMMMSSNSNMMSMWMWNNWNSMMSWNNSMSSWNSWSMW"
region = np.array(list(codes))
y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)
covariates = np.column_stack([data["poverty"], data["single"], data["urban"]])
X0 = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), covariates])
D = np.column_stack([(region == g).astype(float) for g in "NSW"])
X = np.column_stack([X0, D])
r, q = 7, 3

def fit(Z):
    b, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Z, y, rcond=None)
    return b, np.sum((y - Z @ b) ** 2)

b, sse = fit(X)
b0, sse0 = fit(X0)
F = (sse0 - sse) / q / (sse / (n - r))

u = (sse0 - sse) / sse                          # the ratio behind every statistic
wald = n * u                                    # n (SSE0 - SSE) / SSE
lr = n * np.log1p(u)                            # n log(SSE0 / SSE)
score = n * u / (1 + u)                         # n (SSE0 - SSE) / SSE0
crit = stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, q)
for name, stat in (("Wald", wald), ("LR", lr), ("score", score)):
    print(f"{name:5s} {stat:.3f}  chi2 p = {stats.chi2.sf(stat, q):.4f}")
print(f"F     {F:.3f}  exact p = {stats.f.sf(F, q, n - r):.4f}")

The lesson for other models is double. In the normal linear model none of the three approximations is needed, because the exact F test is available. In models without an exact theory, generalized linear models among them (Chapter 34 onwards), the three statistics are the standard tools (Proposition 34.5.3). Their disagreement in small samples is a warning that the χ2 approximation is poor. The linear model suggests a remedy: dividing by q and referring to an F distribution with finite denominator degrees of freedom often calibrates better.

11.4.3. Invariance and optimality

Is the F test a good test? For a single constraint and a one-sided alternative, the t test of Section 11.6 has strong optimality properties. For q≥2 no test is uniformly most powerful: the most powerful test against alternatives in one direction of the test space rejects for large values of the estimated departure in that direction, and it is a different test for each direction (Exercise (C1)). Some restriction on the class of tests is needed. The natural one is invariance. By Proposition 11.2.2, the problem does not change if the data are rescaled, shifted within the reduced model, or rotated within the test space and within the residual space. A test whose verdict changes under such operations would be favouring particular directions for no reason given by the problem. We show that among tests that do not, the F test is the most powerful, simultaneously against every alternative.

We need one property of the noncentral F family.

Lemma 11.4.3 (Monotone likelihood ratio of the noncentral F)

For q,ν≥1 let fγ be the density of F(q,ν,γ). For every γ>0, the ratio fγ(x)/f0(x) is a strictly increasing function of x>0.

Proof. Let V∼χ2(ν), and for k=0,1,… let hk be the density of (Uk/q)/(V/ν), where Uk∼χ2(q+2k) is independent of V. By Exercise (B4), B=Uk/(Uk+V)∼Beta(ak,b) with ak=q/2+k and b=ν/2. The map B↦x=νB/{q(1−B)} is increasing, with inverse B=t(x):=qx/(ν+qx), and 1−t(x)=ν/(ν+qx). Changing variables, hk(x)=t(x)ak−1{1−t(x)}b−1B(ak,b)⋅qν(ν+qx)2,sohk(x)h0(x)=B(q/2,ν/2)B(q/2+k,ν/2)t(x)k=ckt(x)k, where B is the beta function and ck>0. By Theorem 4.1.2(d), U∼χ2(q,γ) has the law of UK with K∼Poisson(γ/2) independent of the Uk and V. Conditioning on K, F=(U/q)/(V/ν) has density fγ(x)=∑k≥0pkhk(x) with pk=e−γ/2(γ/2)k/k!, and f0=h0. Hence fγ(x)f0(x)=∑k≥0pkckt(x)k. Every coefficient is positive when γ>0, so the series is strictly increasing in t, and t(x) is strictly increasing in x.

A test is a measurable function ϕ of the data with values in [0,1], the probability of rejecting. It is invariant if ϕ(g(y))=ϕ(y) for all y and all g in the set G of maps g(y)=aHy+v,a>0,v∈C(X0),H orthogonal with HC(X0)=C(X0), HC(X)=C(X). For a testable hypothesis with d≠0, the same applies to the data y−Xb0, with C(X0) replaced by S0.

Theorem 11.4.4 (The F test is uniformly most powerful invariant)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 and the setting of Definition 11.1.1. Let ϕ be an invariant test with Eϕ(Y)≤α at every point of H0. Then at every point 𝛃(β,σ2) of the full model, 𝛃Eβ,σ2ϕ(Y)≤Pr{F(q,n−r,γ)>Fα(q,n−r)}, with γ given by Eq. 11.1.2. The right side is the power of the F test, which is itself invariant and of size α.

Proof. Use the canonical coordinates zk=Qk⊤y of Proposition 11.2.3, and let Y={y:z2≠0}, a set of probability one on which F is defined.

Step 1: an invariant test depends on y only through F. Let y,y′∈Y with F(y)=F(y′). Put a=‖z2′‖/‖z2‖>0. Equality of the F values gives ‖z1′‖=a‖z1‖. Choose orthogonal matrices O1 (q×q) and O2 ((n−r)×(n−r)) with O1(az1)=z1′ and O2(az2)=z2′. This is possible because the vectors in each pair have equal lengths, and if z1=0 then z1′=0 and O1=I will do. Put H=Q0Q0⊤+Q1O1Q1⊤+Q2O2Q2⊤,v=Q0(z0′−az0). H is orthogonal, maps C(X0)=C(Q0) and C(X)=C([Q0,Q1]) onto themselves, and v∈C(X0). The canonical coordinates of aHy+v are az0+z0′−az0=z0′, aO1z1=z1′ and aO2z2=z2′, so aHy+v=y′ and ϕ(y′)=ϕ(y). Now fix, for each f≥0, the point yf=qf/(n−r)Q1e1+Q2e1, where e1 denotes a first coordinate vector, and put ψ(f)=ϕ(yf). Then F(yf)=f, so ϕ(y)=ψ(F(y)) for every y∈Y. The map f↦yf is continuous, so ψ is measurable.

Step 2: reduction to one dimension. By Step 1 and Theorem 11.1.1(b), Eϕ(Y)=Eψ(F)=∫0∞ψ(x)fγ(x)dx, with fγ the density of F(q,n−r,γ). The hypothesis is γ=0 (Theorem 11.1.1(c)), so the size condition says ∫ψf0≤α.

Step 3: Neyman–Pearson. Fix γ>0, let c=Fα(q,n−r), ϕ∗(x)=1{x>c} and k=fγ(c)/f0(c). By Lemma 11.4.3, fγ(x)−kf0(x) is positive for x>c and negative for x<c. Since ϕ∗−ψ≥0 where x>c and ϕ∗−ψ≤0 where x<c, the product (ϕ∗−ψ)(fγ−kf0) is nonnegative everywhere. Integrating, ∫(ϕ∗−ψ)fγ ≥ k∫(ϕ∗−ψ)f0=k(α−∫ψf0) ≥ 0. So Eϕ(Y)=∫ψfγ≤∫ϕ∗fγ, which is the power of the F test. For γ=0 the inequality is the size condition. The F test is invariant by Proposition 11.2.2.

The theorem says more than “the F test is good”. It says that once one agrees that no direction in the test space and no scale deserves special treatment, the choice of test is forced: every invariant test is a function of F (Step 1), and among functions of F the upper tail is best against every alternative at once. The likelihood ratio principle arrives at the same test without any appeal to optimality, which is reassuring but not a proof. In other models the likelihood ratio test need not be optimal in any sense.

Remark (What invariance costs). Invariance is a restriction, and the F test is not the most powerful test against every alternative. If one contrast in the test space is of special interest, the t test for that contrast is more powerful against departures in its direction and less powerful against others (Section 11.6). A Bayesian analysis with a prior concentrated on some directions behaves similarly. Invariance expresses the absence of such preferences. It is appropriate for an omnibus question like “do the regions differ?”, and inappropriate when the scientific question singles out a direction.

11.4.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

From F=2.440, q=3, n=50 and r=7, compute the three statistics of Proposition 11.4.2 and check them against Example (Three verdicts from one data set).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Fix q and r. Show that under H, as n→∞, qFH converges in distribution to χ2(q), and that the differences Wald−qFH, −2log⁡Λ−qFH and Score−qFH converge to zero in probability. Deduce that the sizes in Example (Three verdicts from one data set) tend to 0.05.

Solution. Write qFH=(SSH/σ2)/(s2/σ2). The numerator is χ2(q) for every n, and s2/σ2→1 in probability (Exercise (B1)), so qFH→χ2(q) in distribution. With u=qFH/(n−r), Wald−qFH=qFH{n/(n−r)−1}=qFHr/(n−r)→0 in probability. For the others, nu=Op(1), so u→0, and nlog⁡(1+u)−nu=nu{log⁡(1+u)/u−1} and nu/(1+u)−nu=−nu2/(1+u) both tend to zero. Each statistic therefore converges in distribution to χ2(q), whose upper 5% point is the critical value used, so the sizes converge to 0.05.

Exercise (B2)

Let 𝛆𝛃ε^H=y−Xβ^H be the residual vector of the restricted fit. Show that the score statistic equals 𝛆𝛆n‖Mε^H‖2/‖ε^H‖2, which is n times the uncentred R2 from regressing 𝛆ε^H on X. Explain why this “nR2” recipe needs only the restricted fit.

Solution. 𝛆𝛃𝛃𝛃Mε^H=My−Xβ^H=X(β^−β^H), whose squared length is SSH (proof of Proposition 11.4.2), and 𝛆‖ε^H‖2=SSEH. So 𝛆𝛆n‖Mε^H‖2/‖ε^H‖2=nSSH/SSEH=Score. The uncentred R2 of a regression of 𝛆ε^H on X is 𝛆𝛆‖Mε^H‖2/‖ε^H‖2. Computing it requires the restricted residuals and one auxiliary regression, but not 𝛃β^ or the unrestricted model’s residual sum of squares as such. This is why score tests are popular when the full model is hard to fit, as in tests for omitted nonlinear terms or for heteroscedasticity (Chapter 21).

Exercise (B3)

Suppose σ2=σ02 is known. Show that the likelihood ratio test of a reduced model rejects for large (SSE0−SSE)/σ02, the statistic of Exercise (B1), and that −2log⁡Λ is then exactly χ2(q) under H0.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In the canonical form with σ2 known and q≥2, consider testing 𝛈η1=0 against the single alternative 𝛈η1=a≠0. Show by the Neyman–Pearson lemma that the most powerful level-α test rejects when a⊤z1>σ‖a‖zα, where zα is the upper α point of N(0,1). Show that this test has power below α against −a, and conclude that no uniformly most powerful test of 𝛈η1=0 exists.

Solution. The likelihood ratio of N(a,σ2I) to N(0,σ2I) at z1 is exp⁡{(a⊤z1−‖a‖2/2)/σ2}, increasing in a⊤z1. Under the null, a⊤z1∼N(0,σ2‖a‖2), which gives the critical value, and the Neyman–Pearson argument of Step 3 of the proof of Theorem 11.4.4 shows that this test is most powerful. Against −a, a⊤z1∼N(−‖a‖2,σ2‖a‖2), so the power is Pr{Z>zα+‖a‖/σ}<α. A uniformly most powerful test would have to be most powerful against both a and −a. The most powerful tests against these two alternatives are essentially unique and different, so no single test can be both.

Exercise (C2)

Extend Lemma 11.4.3: show that for 0≤γ1<γ2 the ratio fγ2(x)/fγ1(x) is increasing in x. (Write both densities as power series in t(x) with coefficients ak(γ), and show that ak(γ2)/ak(γ1) increases with k.) Deduce that the F test is also uniformly most powerful invariant for testing γ≤γ1 against γ>γ1, with a noncentral critical value. Such tests appear in equivalence testing.

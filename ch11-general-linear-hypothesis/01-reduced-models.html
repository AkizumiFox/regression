11.1  Testing reduced models

Part II fitted linear models without asking whether a fitted term was needed. Part III adds normal errors, and with them exact answers to that question. Does the murder rate of a state depend on its region once poverty and family structure are accounted for? Is a straight line enough, or is there curvature? Do two groups share a regression line? Is a coefficient equal to the value a theory predicts? Each asks whether a reduced model, a special case of the model already fitted, is adequate. This section sets up that comparison and derives its test. The rest of the chapter looks at the same test from other directions.

11.1.1. Hypotheses are subspaces

Throughout the chapter the data follow the normal linear model 𝛃𝛃Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I),β∈Rp, σ2>0, of Eq. 7.3.1, with r=rank(X)<n. We call it the full model. Its content is that the mean vector 𝛍μ=E(Y) lies in the r-dimensional subspace C(X) (Section 6.1). A reduced model says more: the mean lies in a smaller subspace.

Definition 11.1.1 (Reduced model and linear hypothesis)

Let X0 be an n×p0 matrix with C(X0)⊆C(X) and r0=rank(X0)<r. The reduced model is E(Y)=X0c, c∈Rp0. Inside the full model it corresponds to the linear hypothesis 𝛃𝛃𝛃H0: Xβ∈C(X0)againstH1: Xβ∈C(X), Xβ∉C(X0). The number q=r−r0 is the number of degrees of freedom of the hypothesis, and we write M and M0 for the orthogonal projections onto C(X) and C(X0).

Three features of the definition deserve comment. First, the hypothesis is a statement about the mean vector, not about coefficients. Coefficients enter only through the column spaces, so recoding the regressors of either model changes nothing (Theorem 6.7.1). Statements about coefficients, such as β2=β3, are translated into subspaces in Section 11.3. Second, the models must be nested. Two models neither of which contains the other, say one with poverty and one with urbanization, are not compared by the tests of this chapter. Third, both models share the same σ2 and the same error law. The alternative is not “anything other than the reduced model”. It is “the full model, but not the reduced one”.

The most common reduced models come from three operations on X:

  • Deleting columns. Dropping a block X2 from X=[X1,X2] tests whether those regressors are needed given the others. With a single column this is the familiar question about one coefficient. With all columns except the intercept it is the “overall” test of Section 11.6.

  • Merging columns. Replacing x2 and x3 by their sum x2+x3 forces equal coefficients. Merging the indicator columns of several groups forces those groups to share a mean.

  • Fixing a component. Moving a known multiple of a column into an offset, for example testing that a slope equals 1, gives a reduced model of the form E(Y)=X0c+Xb0 with b0 known. It is handled by subtracting Xb0 from the data (Section 11.3).

11.1.2. The F statistic

If the reduced model is adequate, forcing the fit into C(X0) should cost little: the residual sum of squares SSE0=‖(I−M0)y‖2 should exceed SSE=‖(I−M)y‖2 by no more than noise would produce. The difference is the extra sum of squares of Section 9.2, and by Eq. 6.5.2 it is a squared length, SSE0−SSE=‖(M−M0)y‖2=y⊤(M−M0)y, the squared length of the projection of y onto the orthogonal complement of C(X0) within C(X). How large is “more than noise”? Noise contributes σ2 per dimension (Theorem 9.6.1), and M−M0 has rank q, so we compare the hypothesis mean square (SSE0−SSE)/q with the unbiased estimate s2=SSE/(n−r) of σ2 from the full model. The ratio

F=(SSE0−SSE)/(r−r0)SSE/(n−r)=y⊤(M−M0)y/qy⊤(I−M)y/(n−r)
(11.1.1)

is the F statistic for the reduced model. It is scale-free: multiplying y by a constant, or changing the units of the response, does not change it. Its exact distribution is the content of the first theorem of the chapter.

Theorem 11.1.1 (The F test of a reduced model)

Assume the normal linear model Eq. 7.3.1 and the setting of Definition 11.1.1, so that 0≤r0<r<n. Then:

  • (a) SSE0−SSE and SSE are independent, with SSE0−SSEσ2∼χ2(r−r0,γ),SSEσ2∼χ2(n−r);

  • (b) the statistic Eq. 11.1.1 has the noncentral F distribution

    𝛃F∼F(r−r0, n−r, γ),γ=‖(M−M0)Xβ‖2σ2;
    (11.1.2)

  • (c) 𝛃𝛃γ=‖(I−M0)Xβ‖2/σ2=minc‖Xβ−X0c‖2/σ2, the squared distance from the mean vector to the reduced model space in units of σ2. In particular γ=0 iff H0 holds;

  • (d) under H0, F∼F(r−r0,n−r), the central F distribution, whatever the values of 𝛃β and σ2.

Proof. By Theorem 6.5.1, M−M0 and I−M are symmetric idempotent with ranks r−r0 and n−r, and (M−M0)(I−M)=0. Theorem 4.4.4, applied with 𝛍𝛃μ=Xβ, shows that ‖(M−M0)Y‖2/σ2 and ‖(I−M)Y‖2/σ2 are independent with distributions 𝛃χ2(r−r0,‖(M−M0)Xβ‖2/σ2) and 𝛃χ2(n−r,‖(I−M)Xβ‖2/σ2). The second noncentrality is zero because 𝛃𝛃MXβ=Xβ. With Eq. 6.5.2 this is (a). Part (b) follows from (a) and Definition 4.1.2, since σ2 cancels in the ratio. (It is also Theorem 4.4.6 with P=M and P0=M0.) For (c), 𝛃𝛃MXβ=Xβ gives 𝛃𝛃(M−M0)Xβ=(I−M0)Xβ, which is the difference between 𝛃Xβ and its nearest point in C(X0) (Theorem 6.3.1). Its length is therefore the distance from 𝛃Xβ to C(X0), which is zero iff 𝛃Xβ∈C(X0). Part (d) is (b) with γ=0.

Part (d) is what makes a test possible. The reduced model is a composite hypothesis: it leaves c and σ2 unspecified. Yet the null distribution of F is the same at every point of it. The unknown coefficients cancel because (M−M0)X0c=0 and (I−M)X0c=0, and the unknown scale cancels in the ratio. A statistic with this property is called pivotal under the hypothesis.

Part (c) says what the test detects. The alternative is measured by one number, the squared distance of the true mean from the reduced model, relative to the noise variance. Two alternatives with the same γ are equally hard to detect, however different their coefficients. Section 11.5 is built on this.

11.1.3. The test and its p-value

Large values of F are evidence against H0: under the alternative the numerator mean square estimates σ2+σ2γ/q while the denominator still estimates σ2 (Eq. 9.6.1). The F test at level α rejects H0 when F>Fα(r−r0,n−r), where Fα(a,b) is the upper α point of the central F(a,b) distribution. By Theorem 11.1.1(d) its probability of rejecting is exactly α at every point of the reduced model. It rejects with probability greater than α at every point of the alternative, by (c) and Theorem 4.1.4. Instead of a fixed level, one usually reports the p-value p(y)=Pr{F(r−r0,n−r)≥Fobs}, the probability under the reduced model of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.

Proposition 11.1.2 (The p-value is uniform under the hypothesis)

Under H0, the p-value p(Y) has the uniform distribution on (0,1). For every α∈(0,1), rejecting when p(Y)≤α is the F test at level α.

Proof. Let ΦF be the distribution function of F(q,n−r). It is continuous and strictly increasing on (0,∞), since the distribution has a positive density there, and p(y)=1−ΦF(Fobs). Under H0 the statistic F has distribution function ΦF, so for u∈(0,1), Pr{p(Y)≤u}=Pr{ΦF(F)≥1−u}=Pr{F≥ΦF−1(1−u)}=u. Finally, p(y)≤α iff ΦF(Fobs)≥1−α iff Fobs≥Fα(q,n−r), and the event F=Fα has probability zero.

The p-value is a statement about the data under H0. It is not the probability that H0 is true, and it is not the probability that the result is “due to chance”. A small p-value says that data like these would be unusual if the reduced model held.

Example (Do regions matter?)

In the 2009 data on 50 US states used in Chapter 6 and Chapter 9, regress the murder rate on the poverty rate, the percentage of single-parent households and the percentage living in urbanized areas, with an intercept. This is the reduced model, with r0=4. The full model adds indicators of three of the four Census regions (Northeast, South, West, with the Midwest as reference), so r=7. The question is whether the regions differ in murder rate once the three covariates are accounted for. The listing gives

Source Degrees of freedom Sum of squares Mean square
Regions, given the covariates 3 14.83 4.944
Residual, full model 43 87.13 2.026
Residual, reduced model 46 101.96

so F=4.944/2.026=2.440 on 3 and 43 degrees of freedom. The 5% critical value is 2.822 and the p-value is 0.077. At the conventional level the reduced model is not rejected. The data do not show convincingly that the regions differ, given the covariates.

Yet in the full model the Northeast coefficient is −1.736 murders per 100,000, with a t statistic of −2.519 and a p-value of 0.016. One of the three contrasts looks clearly nonzero while the joint test does not reject. There is no contradiction: Section 11.6 shows how a joint test can dilute one strong effect among weak ones, and Chapter 13 explains why a contrast singled out after looking at the estimates cannot be judged by its own p-value.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats

data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data
data.index = data.index.str.strip()
data = data.drop(index="District of Columbia")
# Census region of each state, in the data's alphabetical order (N, M, S, W)
codes = "SWWSWWNSSSWWMMMMSSNSNMMSMWMWNNWNSMMSWNNSMSSWNSWSMW"
region = np.array(list(codes))
y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)
covariates = np.column_stack([data["poverty"], data["single"], data["urban"]])
X0 = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), covariates])                    # reduced model
D = np.column_stack([(region == g).astype(float) for g in "NSW"])  # Midwest is the reference
X = np.column_stack([X0, D])                                      # full model

def sse(Z):
    """Residual sum of squares of the least squares fit of y on the columns of Z."""
    b, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Z, y, rcond=None)
    return np.sum((y - Z @ b) ** 2)

r, r0 = np.linalg.matrix_rank(X), np.linalg.matrix_rank(X0)
sse_full, sse_reduced = sse(X), sse(X0)
F = ((sse_reduced - sse_full) / (r - r0)) / (sse_full / (n - r))
p_value = stats.f.sf(F, r - r0, n - r)
crit = stats.f.ppf(0.95, r - r0, n - r)
print(f"SSE0 = {sse_reduced:.2f}, SSE = {sse_full:.2f}, df = ({r - r0}, {n - r})")
print(f"F = {F:.3f}, p = {p_value:.4f}, 5% critical value = {crit:.3f}")

11.1.4. What the test assumes

Theorem 11.1.1 rests on three assumptions.

The full model must hold. The denominator s2 estimates σ2 only if E(Y)∈C(X). If the full model omits something, E(s2) exceeds σ2 by 𝛍‖(I−M)μ‖2/(n−r) (Theorem 9.6.1(b)), the denominator is inflated, and F is pushed towards zero. The test then loses power, and a nonsignificant result says even less than usual. The test of a reduced model is a test within the full model. It cannot certify the full model itself. Chapter 14 tests the full model against a nonparametric alternative when there are replicate observations.

The errors must be independent with a common variance. Correlated or heteroscedastic errors change both numerator and denominator, and the null distribution is no longer F. Chapter 21 and Chapter 31 treat these cases.

Normality gives the exact distribution, and it is the least critical of the three. The null distribution is exactly F for every spherically symmetric error law (Section 11.2), and approximately so for independent non-normal errors unless the sample is small or some points have high leverage (Theorem 19.3.2).

Warning (What a nonsignificant F does not show). Failing to reject the reduced model is not evidence that the reduced model is true. The test controls the probability of wrongly rejecting H0. It says nothing on its own about the probability of wrongly accepting it, which depends on the unknown noncentrality γ and may be large. In Example (Do regions matter?), p=0.077 is compatible with no regional differences. It is equally compatible with sizeable regional differences. Section 11.5 computes that the test had only about an even chance of detecting a Northeast shortfall of 1.5 murders per 100,000, about the size estimated in Section 11.3 (1.379 below the other three regions). To support “the reduced model is adequate”, one must show that every alternative of practical importance would have been rejected. That calls for a power calculation made before the data were seen, or better, a confidence region for the omitted effects that excludes all important values (Chapter 12), or a test of equivalence in which the roles of hypothesis and alternative are reversed. Nor does a nonsignificant F license dropping the terms and then reporting the reduced fit as if it had been specified in advance.

Warning (What a significant F does not show). A significant F says that the mean vector is not in C(X0), assuming the full model holds. It does not say which of the q dimensions of the test space carries the departure, nor that every omitted coefficient is nonzero. It does not say that the departure is large enough to matter: with large n, a tiny γ/n is detected with certainty (Section 11.5). And it does not say that the full model is correct. The statistic only compares two models, and a third, better model may exist.

11.1.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that the F statistic Eq. 11.1.1 does not change when y is replaced by ay+X0c for any a≠0 and any vector c. Interpret the result for the region test of Example (Do regions matter?) when murder rates are expressed per million instead of per 100,000.

Exercise (A2)

In the model E(Yi)=β0+β1xi1+β2xi2+β3xi3, write down a matrix X0 (or an offset and an X0) for each reduced model and give q: (i) β2=β3=0; (ii) β1=β2=β3; (iii) β1=2; (iv) β2+β3=0.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Suppose σ2=σ02 is known. Show that X2=(SSE0−SSE)/σ02 has the χ2(q,γ) distribution and that the test rejecting when X2 exceeds the upper α point of χ2(q) has level α. Show that qF/X2→1 in probability as n→∞ with q fixed and the full model true, and explain why the F test and this test then behave alike.

Solution. The distribution is Theorem 11.1.1(a) with σ2=σ02; under H0 it is χ2(q), so the level is exact. Now qF/X2=σ02/s2, and s2→σ2=σ02 in probability, since E(s2)=σ2 and Var(s2)=2σ4/(n−r)→0 (Theorem 7.5.1(d)) and Chebyshev’s inequality applies. So qF and X2 differ by a factor tending to one, and qFα(q,n−r) tends to the upper α point of χ2(q) for the same reason. With few residual degrees of freedom the two tests differ, and only the F test has the right level when σ2 must be estimated.

Exercise (B2)

Suppose the full model is wrong: 𝛍E(Y)=μ with 𝛍𝛅(I−M)μ=δ≠0, but 𝛍(M−M0)μ=0. Show that the numerator and denominator of Eq. 11.1.1 are still independent, that E(numerator)=σ2 and 𝛅E(denominator)=σ2+‖δ‖2/(n−r), and that Pr(F≤c)≥Pr{F(q,n−r)≤c} for every c>0. Explain why an F statistic far below one, with a p-value near one, is a hint that the full model is inadequate.

Solution. Independence and the distributions follow from Theorem 4.4.4 as in the proof of Theorem 11.1.1: the numerator is σ2χ2(q)/q, and 𝛅SSE/σ2∼χ2(n−r,‖δ‖2/σ2), with mean 𝛅n−r+‖δ‖2/σ2 (Theorem 4.1.2(b)). For the probability, condition on the numerator U: F≤c iff SSE/σ2≥(n−r)U/(qcσ2), and a noncentral χ2 exceeds any fixed value with at least the central probability (Proposition 4.1.3). Averaging over U gives the inequality. So omitted structure in the residual space makes small values of F more likely. Under the full model, Pr(F≤c) is small for small c, and an observed F in the far lower tail is surprising.

Exercise (B3)

Let C(X0)⊆C(X1)⊆C(X) with ranks r0<r1<r<n, and let s2=SSE/(n−r) come from the largest model. Show that F1=y⊤(M1−M0)y/(r1−r0)s2 has the F(r1−r0,n−r,γ1) distribution with 𝛃γ1=‖(M1−M0)Xβ‖2/σ2 whenever the largest model holds, and that its numerator is independent of that of the test of X1 within X. Why is this preferable to using the residual mean square of X1?

Exercise (B4)

Two samples of sizes n1 and n2 follow 𝛃E(Yk)=Xkβk, k=1,2, with a common σ2 and Xk of full column rank p. Write the model for both samples together as a full model with block-diagonal model matrix, and the hypothesis 𝛃𝛃β1=β2 as a reduced model. Show that F=(SSEpooled−SSE1−SSE2)/p(SSE1+SSE2)/(n1+n2−2p), where SSEk comes from fitting sample k alone and SSEpooled from one fit to all the data. Apply the test to the question whether two firms of the Grunfeld panel (sm.datasets.grunfeld) share their investment equation, and comment on the assumption of a common σ2.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In Exercise (B4) suppose instead that n2≤p and rank(X2)=n2, so that 𝛃β2 cannot be estimated from the second sample. Show that the full model then fits the second sample exactly, identify the test space, and show that the F statistic of Theorem 11.1.1 becomes F=(SSEpooled−SSE1)/n2SSE1/(n1−p), a test of whether the second sample is predicted by the fit to the first, on n2 and n1−p degrees of freedom.

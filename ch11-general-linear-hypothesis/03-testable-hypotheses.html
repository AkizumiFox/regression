11.3  Testable hypotheses

Scientific questions are usually phrased in terms of coefficients rather than subspaces: “the coefficients of poverty and single parenthood are equal”, “the four regions have the same intercept”, “the elasticity is one”. Such statements have the form 𝛃H: Λ⊤β=d, with a known p×m matrix Λ and a known d∈Rm. This is the general linear hypothesis. This section decides when such a statement is a hypothesis about the distribution of Y at all, gives its F statistic in terms of the unrestricted fit alone, shows that the statistic agrees with the reduced-model statistic of Section 11.1, and describes least squares under the hypothesis. Nothing here assumes that X has full column rank.

11.3.1. Which constraints are hypotheses

In the full model the distribution of Y depends on 𝛃β only through 𝛃Xβ. A statement about 𝛃β that cannot be read off from 𝛃Xβ is therefore not a statement about the data-generating distribution, and no data can test it.

Definition 11.3.1 (Testable hypothesis)

Let Λ be a p×m matrix and d∈Rm. The hypothesis 𝛃H:Λ⊤β=d is testable if

  • (i) 𝛃Λ⊤β is estimable, that is, C(Λ)⊆C(X⊤) (Definition 8.2.1 and Theorem 8.3.1), so that Λ=X⊤T for some n×m matrix T; and

  • (ii) the equations Λ⊤b=d are consistent, that is, d∈C(Λ⊤) (Theorem 1.4.1).

Its number of degrees of freedom is q=rank(Λ). We assume q≥1.

Condition (ii) is automatic when Λ has full column rank, for then C(Λ⊤)=Rm. It matters when the columns of Λ are redundant, as when all six pairwise differences of four group effects are set to zero. Redundant constraints are harmless if they agree with each other. If they do not, as in α1−α2=1, α2−α3=1, α1−α3=0, the “hypothesis” is false for every 𝛃β, and no test is possible.

Condition (i) is the substantive one. Without it the constraint cannot be settled by any amount of data.

Proposition 11.3.1 (Nonestimable constraints cannot be tested)

Let 𝛌λ∉C(X⊤) and c∈R.

  • (a) For every 𝛃β∈Rp there is 𝛃β′ with 𝛃𝛃Xβ′=Xβ and 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β′=c.

  • (b) Let ϕ be any test of 𝛌𝛃H:λ⊤β=c in the model Eq. 7.3.1, with rejection probability 𝛃𝛃π(β,σ2)=Eβ,σ2ϕ(Y). For every point 𝛃(β,σ2) of the alternative there is a point 𝛃(β′,σ2) of the hypothesis with 𝛃𝛃π(β′,σ2)=π(β,σ2). So no test has power exceeding its size anywhere.

Proof.

  • (a) By Theorem 8.3.1(c), 𝛌λ∉C(X⊤) means that 𝛌λ is not orthogonal to N(X): there is v with Xv=0 and 𝛌λ⊤v≠0. Put 𝛃𝛃β′=β+tv with 𝛌𝛃𝛌t=(c−λ⊤β)/λ⊤v. Then 𝛃𝛃Xβ′=Xβ and 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β′=c. (b) The law of Y, and hence π, depends on 𝛃(β,σ2) only through 𝛃(Xβ,σ2). Apply (a).

For a matrix Λ, Proposition 9.2.6 gives the general statement: the constraint Λ⊤b=0 restricts the mean vector only through its estimable part C(Λ)∩C(X⊤), and the extra sum of squares has dim⁡(C(Λ)∩C(X⊤)) degrees of freedom. In the one-way layout E(Yij)=μ+αi, the constraint α1=0 is a side condition (Theorem 8.4.2). It selects one of the equivalent parameterizations and says nothing about the data. Programs that report a “test” of α1=0 are testing something else, usually a contrast such as α1−αg that the program’s coding makes estimable.

11.3.2. The F statistic of a testable hypothesis

The sum of squares of a testable hypothesis was computed in Theorem 9.2.4 for Λ of full column rank. The next theorem removes that restriction, identifies the reduced model, and supplies the distribution.

Theorem 11.3.2 (The F test of a testable hypothesis)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with r<n, and let 𝛃H:Λ⊤β=d be testable with q=rank(Λ). Let G be any generalized inverse of X⊤X, 𝛃β^ any least squares estimate, W=Λ⊤GΛ and W− any generalized inverse of W. Put

𝛃𝛃SSH=(Λ⊤β^−d)⊤W−(Λ⊤β^−d),FH=SSH/qSSE/(n−r).
(11.3.1)

  • (a) SSH does not depend on the choices of 𝛃β^, G and W−.

  • (b) Let b0 be any solution of Λ⊤b0=d and S0={Xb:Λ⊤b=0}. Then S0 is a subspace of C(X) of dimension r−q, the set of mean vectors allowed by H is Xb0+S0, and SSH=minΛ⊤b=d‖y−Xb‖2−SSE=‖(M−PS0)(y−Xb0)‖2.

  • (c) FH∼F(q,n−r,γ) with

    𝛃𝛃γ=(Λ⊤β−d)⊤W−(Λ⊤β−d)σ2,
    (11.3.2)

    and γ=0 iff 𝛃Λ⊤β=d. In particular FH is the F statistic of Theorem 11.1.1 for the data y−Xb0 and the reduced model space S0.

Proof. Reduction to full column rank. Choose q linearly independent columns of Λ, forming a p×q matrix Λ1. Every column of Λ is a combination of them, so Λ=Λ1K for a q×m matrix K, which has rank q because rankΛ=q. Hence KK⊤ is nonsingular. Put d1=Λ1⊤b0, so that d=K⊤d1. Since K⊤ has full column rank it is one-to-one, so d1 is determined by d and does not depend on the choice of b0. Moreover Λ⊤b=d⟺K⊤(Λ1⊤b−d1)=0⟺Λ1⊤b=d1. The same argument with d replaced by 0 shows that S0 is also {Xb:Λ1⊤b=0}. The columns of Λ1 are columns of Λ, so 𝛃Λ1⊤β is estimable.

(a). By Theorem 9.2.4(a), 𝛃u1=Λ1⊤β^−d1 and the positive definite matrix W1=Λ1⊤GΛ1 do not depend on 𝛃β^ and G. Now 𝛃u=Λ⊤β^−d=K⊤u1 and W=K⊤W1K. Let v=K⊤(KK⊤)−1W1−1u1, so that Kv=W1−1u1. Then Wv=K⊤W1Kv=K⊤u1=u, and for every generalized inverse, u⊤W−u=v⊤WW−Wv=v⊤Wv=(Kv)⊤W1(Kv)=u1⊤W1−1u1. The right side involves none of the choices.

(b). S0 is the image of the subspace N(Λ⊤) under b↦Xb, so it is a subspace of C(X). Its dimension is r−dim⁡(C(Λ)∩C(X⊤))=r−q by Proposition 9.2.6, because C(Λ)⊆C(X⊤). The solutions of Λ⊤b=d are b0+N(Λ⊤), so the allowed means are Xb0+S0. For the minimum, write y∗=y−Xb0. As b ranges over the constraint set, Xb−Xb0 ranges over S0, so minΛ⊤b=d‖y−Xb‖2=minm∈S0‖y∗−m‖2=‖(I−PS0)y∗‖2 by Theorem 6.3.1. Also SSE=‖(I−M)y‖2=‖(I−M)y∗‖2, since Xb0∈C(X). Because S0⊆C(X), Eq. 6.5.2 turns the difference into ‖(M−PS0)y∗‖2. By Theorem 9.2.4(c) applied to Λ1 and d1, the same difference equals u1⊤W1−1u1, which is SSH by (a).

(c). 𝛃Y−Xb0∼Nn(X(β−b0),σ2I) is a normal linear model with mean in C(X), and by (b) FH is its F statistic for the reduced model space S0, which has dimension r−q. Theorem 11.1.1 gives FH∼F(q,n−r,γ) with 𝛃σ2γ=‖(M−PS0)(Xβ−Xb0)‖2. To evaluate this, note that the identity in (b) holds for every data vector. Apply it to the vector 𝛃Xβ, for which 𝛃β itself is a least squares estimate: 𝛃𝛃σ2γ=(Λ⊤β−d)⊤W−(Λ⊤β−d). Finally, by (a) this equals 𝛃𝛃u1(β)⊤W1−1u1(β) with 𝛃𝛃u1(β)=Λ1⊤β−d1. It is zero iff 𝛃Λ1⊤β=d1, that is, iff 𝛃Λ⊤β=d.

Formula Eq. 11.3.1 needs only the unrestricted fit. Its ingredients have a statistical meaning: 𝛃Λ⊤β^−d is the estimated departure from the hypothesis, and σ2W is its covariance matrix (Theorem 7.5.1(a)). So SSH/σ2 is the squared Mahalanobis length of the estimated departure, and the noncentrality Eq. 11.3.2 is the same length for the true departure. Under H the quadratic form SSH/σ2 is χ2(q), which is Corollary 7.5.3. The theorem adds the reduced model, the distribution under alternatives, and the fact that a redundant Λ can be used as it stands, with any generalized inverse of W.

The test space of the hypothesis is the orthogonal complement of S0 within C(X). By Theorem 6.5.2 it is C(MT), where Λ=X⊤T: the hypothesis constrains the mean vector to be orthogonal to the fitted values of the columns of T.

11.3.3. Least squares under the hypothesis

The minimum in Theorem 11.3.2(b) is attained, and its minimizers have a closed form. The following extends Theorem 9.2.5, which assumed full rank, to every model matrix.

Proposition 11.3.3 (Least squares under a testable hypothesis)

Let 𝛃H:Λ⊤β=d be testable with Λ of full column rank q, and write Λ=X⊤T. Let G be a generalized inverse of X⊤X, 𝛃β^=GX⊤y, W=Λ⊤GΛ and 𝛃u=Λ⊤β^−d. Then W is positive definite, and:

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃β^H=β^−GΛW−1u satisfies 𝛃Λ⊤β^H=d and minimizes ‖y−Xb‖2 subject to Λ⊤b=d. The set of all minimizers is 𝛃β^H+(N(X)∩N(Λ⊤)).

  • (b) The restricted fitted vector is unique: 𝛃Xβ^H=My−MTW−1u=Xb0+PS0(y−Xb0), and 𝛃‖y−Xβ^H‖2=SSE+SSH, with SSH=u⊤W−1u.

  • (c) With 𝛉θ^=W−1u, the restricted estimate solves the constrained normal equations (compare Theorem 6.4.2) 𝛃𝛉𝛃X⊤Xβ^H+Λθ^=X⊤y,Λ⊤β^H=d,

    and 𝛉𝛉SSH=θ^⊤Wθ^.

Proof. W=T⊤XGX⊤T=T⊤MT by Theorem 6.3.6. It is symmetric, and positive definite by Theorem 9.2.4(a). Also XGΛ=XGX⊤T=MT.

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃Λ⊤β^H=Λ⊤β^−WW−1u=d. For any feasible b, Eq. 6.3.1 with u=Xb gives 𝛃‖y−Xb‖2=SSE+‖Xβ^−Xb‖2. Write 𝛃b=β^H+v with Λ⊤v=0. Then 𝛃Xβ^−Xb=MTW−1u−Xv, and the cross term vanishes: (MTW−1u)⊤Xv=u⊤W−1T⊤Xv=u⊤W−1Λ⊤v=0, using MX=X. Hence ‖y−Xb‖2=SSE+‖MTW−1u‖2+‖Xv‖2, which is smallest exactly when Xv=0. The minimizers are 𝛃β^H+v with v∈N(X)∩N(Λ⊤).

  • (b) 𝛃𝛃Xβ^H=Xβ^−XGΛW−1u=My−MTW−1u, and both terms are unique by Theorem 9.2.4(a). The minimum value is SSE+u⊤W−1T⊤MTW−1u=SSE+u⊤W−1u, and u⊤W−1u=SSH by Theorem 11.3.2(a). The fitted vector 𝛃Xβ^H is the point of Xb0+S0 nearest to y, which is Xb0+PS0(y−Xb0) by the argument in the proof of Theorem 11.3.2(b).

  • (c) 𝛃𝛃𝛉𝛉X⊤Xβ^H=X⊤Xβ^−X⊤XGX⊤TW−1u=X⊤y−X⊤MTθ^=X⊤y−Λθ^, because 𝛃X⊤Xβ^=X⊤y and X⊤M=X⊤. The last identity is 𝛉𝛉u⊤W−1u=θ^⊤Wθ^.

Part (c) identifies 𝛉θ^ as a vector of Lagrange multipliers (compare Proposition 1.11.3). Each multiplier measures how hard the data push against its constraint, and the hypothesis sum of squares is their squared Mahalanobis length. This is the “Lagrange multiplier” form of the test met again in Section 11.4.

11.3.4. Nonzero right-hand sides: offsets

When d≠0 the hypothesis restricts the mean to the affine set Xb0+S0, not to a subspace. Theorem 11.3.2(c) shows how to handle it: subtract a known point of the set from the data. The reduced model becomes E(Y−Xb0)∈S0, a linear model for the adjusted response Y−Xb0 with a known offset Xb0. Any solution b0 of Λ⊤b0=d may be used. Two solutions differ by an element of N(Λ⊤), so their offsets differ by an element of S0, which the reduced model absorbs. In practice one fits y−Xb0 on a basis of S0, for example the columns of XU where the columns of U span N(Λ⊤), and takes the difference of residual sums of squares. The formula Eq. 11.3.1 gives the same number with no second fit.

Example (Regional hypotheses with an over-parameterized model)

Return to the state murder data, now with an intercept and an indicator for each of the four regions, so that 𝛃β=(μ,αN,αM,αS,αW,βpov,βsin,βurb)⊤. The model matrix is 50×8 of rank 7. No region effect is estimable on its own, but every contrast among them is.

All four regions equal. Write the hypothesis with all six pairwise differences αi−αj as the columns of Λ. They are consistent and have rank q=3. With the Moore–Penrose inverses of X⊤X and of W, Eq. 11.3.1 gives SSH=14.83 and F=2.440, exactly the reduced-model test of Example (Do regions matter?), as Theorem 11.3.2(b) requires. A second generalized inverse, made by deleting the row and column of αW from X⊤X before inverting, gives a different 𝛃β^ but the same 𝛃Λ⊤β^, W and F.

Midwest, South and West equal. With Λ made of αM−αS and αS−αW, the sum of squares is 3.881 on 2 degrees of freedom, F=0.958, p=0.392. The restricted fit of Proposition 11.3.3 coincides with the fit of the model in which the three regions are merged. Under the constraint, the Northeast lies 1.379 below the common level of the other three, and the coefficient of single parenthood moves from 0.428 to 0.441.

A nonzero right-hand side. The contrast αN−(αM+αS+αW)/3, the Northeast against the average of the other regions, is estimated as ψ^=−1.426 with standard error 0.595. To test whether it equals −1, take the offset b0=(0,−1,0,…,0)⊤ and fit y−Xb0 on a basis of S0. The drop in residual sum of squares is 1.040, equal to 𝛌𝛌(ψ^+1)2/λ⊤Gλ as Eq. 11.3.1 predicts, and F=0.513 with p=0.478. Any other solution of 𝛌λ⊤b0=−1 gives the same answer.

A constraint that is not a hypothesis. The constraint αN=0 is not estimable. Minimizing the residual sum of squares subject to it gives exactly SSE: the constrained model spans the whole of C(X), in agreement with Proposition 11.3.1.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import linalg, stats

data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data
data.index = data.index.str.strip()
data = data.drop(index="District of Columbia")
codes = "SWWSWWNSSSWWMMMMSSNSNMMSMWMWNNWNSMMSWNNSMSSWNSWSMW"
region = np.array(list(codes))
y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)
D = np.column_stack([(region == g).astype(float) for g in "NMSW"])   # all four regions
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), D, data["poverty"], data["single"], data["urban"]])
r = np.linalg.matrix_rank(X)                                          # 7, not 8

G = np.linalg.pinv(X.T @ X)                     # one generalized inverse of X'X
b = G @ X.T @ y                                 # one least squares solution
s2 = np.sum((y - X @ b) ** 2) / (n - r)

def glh_test(Lam, d):
    """F test of the testable hypothesis Lam' beta = d, via any g-inverse of Lam' G Lam."""
    u = Lam.T @ b - d
    W = Lam.T @ G @ Lam
    q = np.linalg.matrix_rank(Lam)
    ss_h = u @ np.linalg.pinv(W) @ u
    F = ss_h / q / s2
    return ss_h, q, F, stats.f.sf(F, q, n - r)

def contrast(pairs):
    """Columns e_i - e_j on the region coefficients (positions 1..4 = N, M, S, W)."""
    L = np.zeros((8, len(pairs)))
    for k, (i, j) in enumerate(pairs):
        L[1 + i, k], L[1 + j, k] = 1.0, -1.0
    return L

all_pairs = contrast([(0, 1), (0, 2), (0, 3), (1, 2), (1, 3), (2, 3)])   # rank 3, redundant
three_equal = contrast([(1, 2), (2, 3)])                                 # M = S = W
for name, L in (("four regions equal", all_pairs), ("M = S = W", three_equal)):
    ss_h, q, F, p = glh_test(L, np.zeros(L.shape[1]))
    print(f"{name:20s} SS_H = {ss_h:.3f}  q = {q}  F = {F:.3f}  p = {p:.4f}")

The offset computation fits the reduced model directly, as a check on the formula.

def sse(Z, target):
    coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Z, target, rcond=None)
    return np.sum((target - Z @ coef) ** 2)

sse_full = sse(X, y)
lam = np.array([0, 1, -1 / 3, -1 / 3, -1 / 3, 0, 0, 0])   # Northeast minus the other three
d = -1.0                                                  # hypothesized difference
b0 = np.array([0, -1.0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0])                 # one solution of lam' b0 = d
U = linalg.null_space(lam[None, :])                       # beta - b0 ranges over C(U)
sse_H = sse(X @ U, y - X @ b0)                            # reduced model, offset X b0
est = lam @ b
se = np.sqrt(s2 * lam @ G @ lam)
print(f"estimate {est:.3f} (se {se:.3f}); SSE_H - SSE = {sse_H - sse_full:.3f}")
print(f"formula: {(est - d) ** 2 / (lam @ G @ lam):.3f}")

The restricted estimates, from two different generalized inverses:

keep = [0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7]
G2 = np.zeros((8, 8))
G2[np.ix_(keep, keep)] = np.linalg.inv((X.T @ X)[np.ix_(keep, keep)])

def restricted(Lam, d, Ginv):
    """Least squares under the testable constraint Lam' b = d (Lam of full column rank)."""
    bh = Ginv @ X.T @ y
    W = Lam.T @ Ginv @ Lam
    return bh - Ginv @ Lam @ np.linalg.solve(W, Lam.T @ bh - d)

bH = restricted(three_equal, np.zeros(2), G)
bH2 = restricted(three_equal, np.zeros(2), G2)
print("constraint holds:", np.allclose(three_equal.T @ bH, 0))
print("fitted values agree:", np.allclose(X @ bH, X @ bH2))

Warning (Hypotheses chosen after seeing the data). The same Northeast contrast, tested against zero, gives t=−2.398 and p=0.021. That p-value would be valid had the contrast been specified before the data were examined. It was not: it was suggested by the estimates, after the joint test of all regional differences had failed to reject. Among the many contrasts one could have tested, the one that looks largest is, by construction, the one most likely to look significant by chance. Chapter 13 gives procedures, notably Scheffé’s, that remain valid when the contrast is chosen by looking at the data.

11.3.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In the one-way layout E(Yij)=μ+αi, i=1,2,3, with all groups nonempty, decide which of the following are testable hypotheses: (i) α1=α2; (ii) α1=0; (iii) α1+α2+α3=0; (iv) μ+α1=5; (v) α1−α2=1, α2−α3=2, α1−α3=3; (vi) α1−α2=1, α2−α3=2, α1−α3=4. Give q for those that are.

Solution. The estimable functions are the combinations c0μ+∑iciαi with c0=∑ici (Example (Four answers, one fit) and Section 8.2). (i) is testable, q=1. (ii) and (iii) are not estimable: their coefficient of μ is 0 while the α coefficients sum to 1 and 3. They are side conditions (Theorem 8.4.2). (iv) is testable, q=1. (v) is testable with q=2: the three differences are estimable, have rank 2, and the right-hand sides are consistent because 1+2=3. (vi) is estimable but inconsistent, since (α1−α2)+(α2−α3)=α1−α3 would need 1+2=4. No parameter satisfies it.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let Λ be p×m and A a nonsingular m×m matrix. Show that the hypotheses 𝛃Λ⊤β=d and 𝛃(ΛA)⊤β=A⊤d are the same, and that Eq. 11.3.1 gives the same SSH for both. Conclude that the test does not depend on how a set of constraints is written.

Solution. (ΛA)⊤b=A⊤d⟺A⊤(Λ⊤b−d)=0⟺Λ⊤b=d, since A⊤ is nonsingular. So the constraint sets, and by Theorem 11.3.2(b) the sums of squares, agree. Directly: with 𝛃u=Λ⊤β^−d, the new departure is A⊤u and the new middle matrix is A⊤WA, and one generalized inverse of it is A−1W−A−⊤, which gives u⊤AA−1W−A−⊤A⊤u=u⊤W−u. The choice of generalized inverse does not matter by Theorem 11.3.2(a).

Exercise (B2)

In Proposition 11.3.3, show that 𝛃𝛃𝛃E(Xβ^H)=Xβ−MTW−1(Λ⊤β−d) and 𝛃Cov(Xβ^H)=σ2PS0, where PS0=M−MTW−1T⊤M. Conclude that restricted fitted values are unbiased when H holds, have smaller variance than MY in every direction, and are biased when H fails.

Solution. 𝛃Xβ^H=MY−MTW−1(T⊤MY−d), using 𝛃Λ⊤β^=T⊤MY. Its mean is 𝛃𝛃Xβ−MTW−1(T⊤Xβ−d), and 𝛃𝛃T⊤Xβ=Λ⊤β. The random part is (M−MTW−1T⊤M)Y. The matrix in parentheses is the projection onto C(X) minus the projection onto C(MT) (Theorem 6.3.6, since W=T⊤MT), which is PS0 by Theorem 6.5.2. It is symmetric idempotent, so the covariance is σ2PS0. The difference σ2(M−PS0) from Cov(MY) is a projection times σ2, so it is nonnegative definite. The bias is 𝛃−MTW−1(Λ⊤β−d), zero iff H holds, because MT has full column rank.

Exercise (B3)

In simple regression E(Yi)=β0+β1xi, derive the F statistic for β1=1 in two ways: from Eq. 11.3.1, and by regressing yi−xi on an intercept and comparing with the full fit. Show that both give F=(β^1−1)2Sxx/s2.

Exercise (B4)

Show that the hypothesis 𝛃𝛃Xβ=Xβ0, with 𝛃β0 known, is testable, that q=r, and that its F statistic is 𝛃{‖M(y−Xβ0)‖2/r}/s2. What is the reduced model?

Exercise (C1)

In the one-way layout with two groups of sizes 2 and 3, take Λ=(0,1,0)⊤, so that the “hypothesis” is α1=0. Evaluate 𝛃(Λ⊤β^)2/(Λ⊤GΛ) for two different generalized inverses G of X⊤X and show that the values differ. Explain which step of the proof of Theorem 11.3.2 fails.

Exercise (C2)

Let X have full column rank and write Λ⊤=[Λ1⊤,Λ2⊤] with Λ2⊤ a nonsingular q×q block, after permuting the coefficients so that 𝛃𝛃𝛃β=(β1⊤,β2⊤)⊤ conformably.

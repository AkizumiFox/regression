11.6  Testing single coefficients and groups of coefficients

The tests most often printed by regression software are instances of the general theory: a t test for each coefficient, an F test that all slopes vanish, and on request an F test for a group of coefficients. This section derives each from the preceding sections, explains what each one tests, and studies how a group test and the individual tests inside it can disagree, in both directions.

11.6.1. The t test

When the hypothesis consists of a single estimable function, q=1, the F statistic is the square of a statistic with a sign, and the sign allows one-sided alternatives.

Theorem 11.6.1 (The t test for an estimable function)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with r<n. Let 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤), 𝛌λ≠0, let d∈R, let G be a generalized inverse of X⊤X, and put

𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌T=λ⊤β^−dsλ⊤Gλ,s2=SSEn−r.
(11.6.1)

  • (a) T∼t(n−r,δ) with 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌δ=(λ⊤β−d)/(σλ⊤Gλ).

  • (b) T2 equals the statistic FH of Eq. 11.3.1 for 𝛌𝛃H:λ⊤β=d, and γ=δ2. The two-sided test that rejects when |T|>tn−r,α/2 is the F test of H, with the same p-value.

  • (c) The one-sided test that rejects when T>tn−r,α has size exactly α for the hypothesis 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β≤d: its rejection probability is at most α whenever 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β≤d, with equality when 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=d. Its power increases strictly with 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β.

Proof.

  • (a) is Corollary 7.5.2. (b) With 𝛌Λ=λ, 𝛌𝛌W=λ⊤Gλ>0 is a scalar, so 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌FH=(λ⊤β^−d)2/(λ⊤Gλs2)=T2, and Eq. 11.3.2 gives γ=δ2. Under H, T∼t(n−r) is symmetric about zero, so Pr{|T|>t}=Pr{T2>t2} for every t>0. Hence tn−r,α/22=Fα(1,n−r), the two rejection regions coincide, and the two-sided t p-value Pr{|T|≥|Tobs|} equals the F p-value Pr{T2≥Tobs2}. (c) By Exercise (A2), the probability that a t(n−r,δ) variable exceeds a fixed constant is strictly increasing in δ, and δ is an increasing function of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β. At δ=0 the probability is α.

For a single coefficient βj in a full-rank model, Eq. 11.6.1 becomes the familiar tj=β^j/{scjj}, with cjj the jth diagonal entry of (X⊤X)−1. By Corollary 9.2.3, tj2 is the F statistic for dropping column j from the model. The t test of βj=0 therefore compares the model with all regressors to the model with all except xj. It asks whether xj adds anything given the others. By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem (Theorem 6.6.2), what it measures is the association of y with the part of xj orthogonal to the other columns. The same coefficient can be “significant” in one model and not in another, and there is no contradiction: the two tests answer different questions.

The one-sided test is appropriate only when the direction was specified before seeing the data, for example when theory says a coefficient cannot be negative, or when only an increase would lead to action. Choosing the direction after seeing the sign of the estimate doubles the actual size.

11.6.2. Tests and intervals are one computation

The two-sided test of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=d at level α accepts exactly when 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌|λ⊤β^−d|≤tn−r,α/2sλ⊤Gλ. Collecting the values of d that are accepted gives the interval 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌λ⊤β^±tn−r,α/2sλ⊤Gλ, which is the 1−α confidence interval Eq. 7.5.1. Every interval of this kind is the set of hypothesized values that a test does not reject, and every test can be read off an interval: reject 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=d iff d lies outside. The interval reports more. It shows which values are ruled out, and so how precisely the data determine 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β, which the verdict of a single test does not. For several functions at once, inverting the F test of Theorem 11.3.2 gives an ellipsoid. Chapter 12 develops both and the duality between them.

11.6.3. The overall F test

The most common reduced model is the one with no regressors at all. Let X contain the intercept column 1 and have rank r. The reduced model E(Y)=β01 has SSE0=SST=∑i(yi−y¯)2, and SST−SSE=SSR is the regression sum of squares. By Theorem 11.1.1 and Theorem 9.5.2, 𝛃F=SSR/(r−1)SSE/(n−r)=R2/(r−1)(1−R2)/(n−r) ∼ F(r−1,n−r,γ),γ=‖(M−n−111⊤)Xβ‖2σ2. The overall F test asks whether any linear combination of the regressors helps to predict the response. The answer is almost always yes in observational data with a sensible choice of regressors, and a significant overall F is correspondingly weak information. It does not show that any particular regressor matters, that the model fits well, or that it predicts usefully: with n large, a model with R2=0.02 has a highly significant overall F. A nonsignificant overall F is more informative. It says that the data give no evidence that the regressors, taken together, relate to the response at all.

11.6.4. Groups of coefficients

Between one coefficient and all of them lies the test of a group: whether the terms of a factor, the powers of a polynomial beyond the linear, or a set of related regressors can be dropped together. The statistic is the extra sum of squares of the group, divided by its degrees of freedom and by s2 (Theorem 11.1.1). How does it relate to the t statistics of its members?

Proposition 11.6.2 (The group statistic is the largest single statistic)

Let 𝛃H:Λ⊤β=d be testable with Λ of full column rank q, let FH be its statistic Eq. 11.3.1, and for a∈Rq, a≠0, let T(a) be the t statistic Eq. 11.6.1 for the single function 𝛃a⊤Λ⊤β=a⊤d. Then qFH=maxa≠0T(a)2, and the maximum is attained at 𝛃a∝W−1(Λ⊤β^−d), where W=Λ⊤GΛ. In particular tj2≤qFH for each coefficient βj in a tested group βj=0, j∈J, with q=|J|.

Proof. Write 𝛃u=Λ⊤β^−d. The function 𝛃a⊤Λ⊤β is estimable with 𝛌λ=Λa≠0 (the columns of Λ are independent), and 𝛌𝛌λ⊤Gλ=a⊤Wa, so T(a)2=(a⊤u)2/(s2a⊤Wa). The matrix W is positive definite (Theorem 9.2.4(a)) and has a positive definite square root (Theorem 1.7.6). By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (Proposition 1.1.1) applied to W1/2a and W−1/2u, (a⊤u)2≤(a⊤Wa)(u⊤W−1u), with equality iff W1/2a is proportional to W−1/2u, that is, a∝W−1u (or u=0). Dividing by s2a⊤Wa gives T(a)2≤u⊤W−1u/s2=qFH, with equality at the stated a. For the coefficient βj, take a to be the corresponding coordinate vector.

The proposition is the geometric heart of Scheffé’s method for multiple comparisons, and it explains the two ways in which a group test and its members can disagree.

Dilution. A single large tj need not make the group significant. The group test rejects when qFH>qFα(q,n−r), and qFα(q,n−r) grows with q (Exercise (B2)). A tj2 just above Fα(1,n−r) can therefore leave qFH below the group’s critical value, especially when the other members of the group contribute nothing. This is what happened in Example (Do regions matter?): the Northeast contrast had |t|=2.519, but the three-degree-of-freedom test of all regional differences was not significant.

Collinearity. Conversely, the group can be highly significant while every member has a small t. The direction a that attains the maximum is generally not a coordinate direction. When the estimates are strongly correlated, the data can say clearly that some combination of the coefficients is nonzero without being able to attribute it to any single one.

Example (Five regressors for the murder rate)

Regress the murder rate of the 50 states on poverty, high-school graduation, single-parent households, percentage white and urbanization, with an intercept (p=6, n−p=44).

Regressor Estimate Standard error t p-value
intercept −10.0586 17.6785 −0.569 0.572
poverty 0.2378 0.1412 1.684 0.099
high-school graduates −0.0047 0.1530 −0.030 0.976
single-parent households 0.4280 0.1372 3.120 0.003
white 0.0108 0.0270 0.398 0.692
urban 0.0046 0.0145 0.315 0.754

The overall test gives R2=0.643 and F=15.87 on 5 and 44 degrees of freedom, p=6.4×10−9. Only single parenthood has a t statistic beyond the critical value 2.015. The group of three demographic and educational regressors, high-school graduation, percentage white and urbanization, has F=0.096 on 3 and 44 degrees of freedom, p=0.962: the data give no evidence that these three add anything once poverty and single parenthood are in the model. Every tj2 of the group is at most 3F=0.29, as Proposition 11.6.2 requires.

This group was chosen here because its members have small t statistics. A group selected that way will tend to have a small F, and its large p-value makes dropping the group look safer than it is. Groups should be defined by the scientific question, before the individual results are seen.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats

data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data
data.index = data.index.str.strip()
data = data.drop(index="District of Columbia")
names = ["poverty", "hs_grad", "single", "white", "urban"]
y = data["murder"].to_numpy()
n = len(y)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), data[names]])
p = X.shape[1]

C = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
b = C @ X.T @ y
sse = np.sum((y - X @ b) ** 2)
s2 = sse / (n - p)
se = np.sqrt(s2 * np.diag(C))
t = b / se
pv = 2 * stats.t.sf(np.abs(t), n - p)
for name, bj, sj, tj, pj in zip(["intercept"] + names, b, se, t, pv):
    print(f"{name:10s} {bj:9.4f} {sj:8.4f} {tj:7.3f} {pj:7.4f}")
sst = np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2)
R2 = 1 - sse / sst
F_all = (R2 / (p - 1)) / ((1 - R2) / (n - p))

def extra_F(keep):
    """F test that the coefficients not in `keep` (columns of X) are zero."""
    Xr = X[:, keep]
    br, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Xr, y, rcond=None)
    sse_r = np.sum((y - Xr @ br) ** 2)
    q = p - len(keep)
    F = ((sse_r - sse) / q) / s2
    return F, stats.f.sf(F, q, n - p)

F_group, p_group = extra_F([0, 1, 3])          # drop hs_grad, white, urban
print(f"overall: R2 = {R2:.3f}, F = {F_all:.2f} on ({p - 1}, {n - p})")
print(f"hs_grad, white, urban: F = {F_group:.3f}, p = {p_group:.3f}")
Example (Jointly significant, individually not)

Longley’s data record US employment for the 16 years 1947 to 1962, with five economic series: the GNP deflator, GNP, unemployment, the size of the armed forces and population. Regress employment on the five series and an intercept, leaving out the time trend, so that n−p=10. For unemployment and population, the t statistics are −0.921 and −1.222, with p-values 0.379 and 0.250. Neither comes near the critical value 2.228. The test that both coefficients are zero gives F=7.175 on 2 and 10 degrees of freedom, p=0.0117.

Figure 11.6.1 shows why. The two estimates have correlation −0.840. Under the hypothesis, their t statistics are then likely to have opposite signs, and the F test’s acceptance region is an ellipse stretched along the line t1=−t2. The observed pair has both statistics negative, a combination the ellipse excludes although each coordinate on its own is unremarkable. The data are clear that unemployment and population together matter, and unclear about how to divide the credit. With 16 consecutive years of trending series, the errors are also unlikely to be independent, a caution taken up in Chapter 21.

lg = sm.datasets.longley.load_pandas()
Z = sm.add_constant(lg.exog.drop(columns="YEAR"))
res = sm.OLS(lg.endog, Z).fit()
joint = res.f_test("UNEMP = 0, POP = 0")
print(res.tvalues[["UNEMP", "POP"]].round(3).to_dict(), res.pvalues[["UNEMP", "POP"]].round(3).to_dict())
print(f"joint F = {float(joint.fvalue):.3f}, p = {float(joint.pvalue):.4f}")

The figure also shows the opposite disagreement. Points inside the ellipse but outside the square have one significant t and a nonsignificant F. Neither verdict is wrong: the individual tests ask about the coordinate directions, and the joint test asks about all directions at once, paying for that breadth with a larger critical value.

Warning (Several t tests are not a test of the group). Rejecting the group hypothesis “all βj=0, j∈J” whenever at least one |tj| exceeds tn−r,α/2 is not a level-α test: when the k coefficient estimates are independent and n−r is large, its size is about 1−(1−α)k, about 0.23 for k=5 at α=0.05. (The t statistics share the estimate s, so they are not exactly independent.) Reporting the smallest of several p-values as if it were the only one overstates the evidence in the same way. The F test of the group, or a multiple-comparison procedure designed for the family (Chapter 13), controls the familywise error rate. Choosing regressors by their t statistics and then testing the chosen model on the same data invalidates all the p-values of the final fit. Section 29.5 returns to this problem.

11.6.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Using the table of Example (Five regressors for the murder rate), compute the F statistic for dropping poverty from the five-regressor model and its p-value, without refitting.

Exercise (A2)

Show that if the direction of a one-sided t test is chosen after seeing the sign of T, and the test then rejects when |T|>tn−r,α, its size is 2α.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

In a full-rank model, show that the t statistic for βj=βk is T=β^j−β^kscjj+ckk−2cjk, where cjk are the entries of (X⊤X)−1. Show that it equals the t statistic of the coefficient of xj in the reparameterized model with regressors xj and xj+xk in place of xj and xk.

Exercise (B2)

Show that qFα(q,ν) is strictly increasing in q for fixed ν and α. Deduce that a single t statistic with t2 just above Fα(1,ν) does not force the rejection of a group containing it.

Solution. qF(q,ν) has the law of Uq/(V/ν) with Uq∼χ2(q) independent of V∼χ2(ν). Taking Uq+1=Uq+W with W∼χ2(1) independent of Uq and V, we have Uq+1/(V/ν)>Uq/(V/ν) with probability one, so Pr{Uq+1/(V/ν)>c}>Pr{Uq/(V/ν)>c} for every c>0 (strictly, since Pr{Uq/(V/ν)≤c<Uq+1/(V/ν)}>0). Taking c=qFα(q,ν), where the right side equals α, shows that the upper α point of Uq+1/(V/ν) exceeds qFα(q,ν). For the deduction, if tj2 lies between Fα(1,ν) and qFα(q,ν) and the other members contribute nothing, then qFH can equal tj2<qFα(q,ν), and the group is not rejected.

Exercise (B3)

For two coefficients with t statistics t1,t2 whose estimates have correlation ρ, show that the F statistic for both being zero is F=t12+t22−2ρt1t22(1−ρ2). Check the value 7.175 of Example (Jointly significant, individually not) from t1=−0.921, t2=−1.222 and ρ=−0.840, and find the value of F had the correlation been +0.840.

Solution. Let D=diag(se1,se2) and R the correlation matrix of the estimates, so that the estimated covariance is DRD. With Λ selecting the two coefficients, 𝛃𝛃2F=β^J⊤(DRD)−1β^J=t⊤R−1t, where 𝛃t=D−1β^J, and R−1=(1−ρ2)−1(1−ρ−ρ1). Numerically, t12+t22=2.341, −2ρt1t2=1.890 and 2(1−ρ2)=0.589, so F=7.175, as in the example, up to the rounding of these three terms. With ρ=+0.840 the middle term changes sign and F=0.765, about (2.341−1.890)/0.589: the same two t statistics would then be entirely unremarkable.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Show that 𝛌maxT(λ)2 over all nonzero estimable 𝛌λ, for the hypotheses 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=0, equals rF∗, where F∗ is the statistic for the reduced model E(Y)=0. Deduce that 𝛌𝛌Pr{T(λ)2≤rFα(r,n−r) for all estimable λ}=1−α when 𝛃β=0. What changes if 𝛃β≠0 and 𝛌T(λ) is centred at 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β? (Chapter 13 builds simultaneous inference on this.)

Exercise (C2)

Find the likelihood ratio test of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β≤d against 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β>d for estimable 𝛌λ, and show that it rejects for large values of T in Eq. 11.6.1. (Maximize the likelihood under the inequality constraint: if 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^≤d the unrestricted maximum is feasible; otherwise the maximum is on the boundary.)

Solution. If 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^≤d, the unrestricted maximizer satisfies the constraint and Λ=1. Otherwise the log-likelihood, maximized over σ2, is a decreasing function of ‖y−Xb‖2, which is a convex quadratic in b with minimum at 𝛃β^. Its minimum over the half-space 𝛌λ⊤b≤d therefore lies on the boundary 𝛌λ⊤b=d, where it equals 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌SSE+(λ⊤β^−d)2/λ⊤Gλ (Eq. 7.3.3). So Λ={1+T2/(n−r)}−n/2 when T>0 and Λ=1 when T≤0. This is a nonincreasing function of T, strictly decreasing for T>0, so Λ≤k<1 iff T≥c for some c>0, which is the one-sided t test.

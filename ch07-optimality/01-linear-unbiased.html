7.1  Linear unbiased estimation

To call an estimator the best, we have to say what it is compared with and by what criterion. The comparison can’t be made against every estimator. The constant estimator β~1≡0.5 of a slope has variance zero, and it is perfect when the true slope happens to be 0.5 and useless otherwise. No estimator has smaller variance, yet nobody would call it good. Some restriction on the class of competitors is unavoidable. The classical restriction has two parts. The estimator must be unbiased, which rules out estimators that buy precision by ignoring the data. And it must be linear in the response, which makes its variance computable from the first two moments of the errors alone. This section describes the class these two conditions carve out. The description is geometric, and the Gauss–Markov theorem of Section 7.2 will follow from it in a few lines.

7.1.1. The setting

Throughout this chapter the response satisfies the linear model of Chapter 5 (Definition 5.3.1),

𝛃𝛆𝛆𝛆Y=Xβ+ε,E(ε)=0,Cov(ε)=σ2I,
(7.1.1)

with X a fixed n×p matrix of rank r≤p, 𝛃β∈Rp unknown and σ2>0 unknown. These are the second-moment assumptions. The errors have mean zero, a common variance, and are uncorrelated. Nothing is assumed about their distribution beyond that. From Section 7.3 on we add normality, and we will say so each time. As in Chapter 6, M denotes the orthogonal projection onto C(X), 𝛃β^ is any least squares estimate, and SSE=‖(I−M)Y‖2 is the residual sum of squares.

The quantities we want to estimate are linear functions 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β, for fixed 𝛌λ∈Rp. This covers a single coefficient (𝛌λ a coordinate vector), a difference of two coefficients, and the mean response 𝛃x0⊤β at a new point x0. Several such functions at once are collected as 𝛃Λ⊤β, where Λ is a p×q matrix.

7.1.2. Linear and unbiased

Definition 7.1.1 (Linear unbiased and best linear unbiased estimators)

Let 𝛌λ∈Rp.

  • (a) A linear estimator is a statistic of the form c+a⊤Y, with a constant c∈R and a constant vector a∈Rn (constant means not depending on Y or on the parameters).

  • (b) A linear estimator is a linear unbiased estimator (LUE) of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β if 𝛌𝛃E(c+a⊤Y)=λ⊤β for every 𝛃β∈Rp and every σ2>0.

  • (c) An LUE c+a⊤Y of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β is a best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) if Var(c+a⊤Y)≤Var(d+b⊤Y) for every LUE d+b⊤Y of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β, for every 𝛃β and every σ2>0.

  • (d) For q functions 𝛃Λ⊤β, a linear estimator is c+AY with a constant q×n matrix A and c∈Rq. It is an LUE of 𝛃Λ⊤β if each coordinate is an LUE of the corresponding coordinate of 𝛃Λ⊤β, and a BLUE if Cov(BY+d)−Cov(AY+c) is nonnegative definite for every LUE BY+d (Definition 1.7.1).

The words “for every 𝛃β” in (b) carry all the weight. An estimator that is unbiased at one parameter value only, like the constant 0.5 when β1=0.5, does not qualify. The first result (Proposition 7.1.1) turns unbiasedness into linear equations.

Proposition 7.1.1 (Unbiasedness as a linear equation)

The linear estimator c+a⊤Y is an LUE of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β iff c=0 and 𝛌X⊤a=λ. Consequently 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β has an LUE iff 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤).

Proof. By Theorem 2.2.1, 𝛃E(c+a⊤Y)=c+a⊤Xβ. This equals 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β for all 𝛃β iff the affine function 𝛃𝛌𝛃β↦c+(X⊤a−λ)⊤β vanishes identically. Putting 𝛃β=0 gives c=0. Putting 𝛃β equal to each coordinate vector in turn then gives 𝛌X⊤a=λ. The converse is immediate. An LUE therefore exists iff the equation 𝛌X⊤a=λ has a solution, that is, iff 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤).

So the constant c is always zero, and from now on we drop it. A linear function 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β with 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤) is called estimable. For these functions the data carry linear information. For the others no linear combination of the observations is centred on the target. Chapter 8 takes this as the definition of estimability (Definition 8.2.1) and develops its many equivalent forms. For now we need only Proposition 7.1.1 and one comparison. Theorem 6.4.4 of Chapter 6 found the same condition 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤) as the answer to a different question: when is 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ the same for every least squares estimate 𝛃β^? The functions that least squares estimates unambiguously are exactly the functions that have linear unbiased estimators at all. When X has full column rank, C(X⊤)=Rp, so every linear function of 𝛃β is estimable.

7.1.3. The geometry of the unbiased class

Fix an estimable 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β and write 𝛌𝛒λ=X⊤ρ for some 𝛒ρ∈Rn. The coefficient vectors of its LUEs form the set 𝛌𝛌Aλ={a∈Rn:X⊤a=λ}. This set is the key object of the chapter’s first half.

Proposition 7.1.2 (The set of unbiased coefficient vectors)

Let 𝛌𝛒λ=X⊤ρ. Then:

  • (a) 𝛌𝛒Aλ=Mρ+C(X)⊥, an affine subspace of Rn of dimension n−r, parallel to C(X)⊥;

  • (b) every 𝛌a∈Aλ has the same projection 𝛒Ma=Mρ, and 𝛒Mρ is the only element of 𝛌Aλ that lies in C(X);

  • (c) 𝛒𝛌Mρ=X(X⊤X)−λ for every generalized inverse, and 𝛒𝛌𝛃(Mρ)⊤Y=λ⊤β^ for every least squares estimate 𝛃β^.

Proof.

  • (a) By Proposition 7.1.1, 𝛌a∈Aλ means 𝛌X⊤a=λ. Now 𝛒X⊤a=X⊤ρ iff 𝛒X⊤(a−ρ)=0, iff 𝛒a−ρ∈N(X⊤)=C(X)⊥ (Lemma 6.2.1). So 𝛌𝛒Aλ=ρ+C(X)⊥. Since 𝛒𝛒𝛒ρ−Mρ=(I−M)ρ∈C(X)⊥, the same set is 𝛒Mρ+C(X)⊥. Its dimension is dim⁡C(X)⊥=n−r.

  • (b) If 𝛒a=Mρ+v with v∈C(X)⊥, then 𝛒𝛒Ma=Mρ+Mv=Mρ. If moreover a∈C(X), then 𝛒a=Ma=Mρ.

  • (c) By Theorem 6.3.7, 𝛒𝛒𝛌Mρ=X(X⊤X)−X⊤ρ=X(X⊤X)−λ. By Theorem 6.4.4, 𝛌𝛃𝛒𝛒λ⊤β^=ρ⊤MY=(Mρ)⊤Y.

Part (c) says that least squares is itself one of the linear unbiased estimators. Its coefficient vector is 𝛒a∗=Mρ, the one member of the class that lies in the model space. It does not depend on which 𝛒ρ was used to represent 𝛌λ, because every such 𝛒ρ has the same projection. That a∗⊤Y is unbiased can also be seen directly: 𝛒𝛃𝛒𝛃𝛌𝛃E(a∗⊤Y)=ρ⊤MXβ=ρ⊤Xβ=λ⊤β.

The variance of any linear estimator follows from Theorem 2.2.1:

Var(a⊤Y)=a⊤(σ2I)a=σ2‖a‖2.
(7.1.2)

Under the second-moment assumptions, then, the variance of a linear estimator is σ2 times the squared length of its coefficient vector. Finding the BLUE means finding the shortest vector in the affine set 𝛌Aλ.

Idea. The linear unbiased estimators of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β correspond to the points of a flat 𝛌Aλ=a∗+C(X)⊥ in Rn. The variance of each is σ2 times its squared distance from the origin. The question “which LUE is best?” is a nearest-point problem, the same kind of problem that least squares solves in Chapter 6, now posed in the space of coefficient vectors.

A second description of the class is useful too. Every 𝛌a∈Aλ is a∗+(I−M)w for some w, so every LUE has the form

𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛆a⊤Y=λ⊤β^+w⊤(I−M)Y=λ⊤β^+w⊤ε^.
(7.1.3)

An LUE is the least squares estimate plus a linear function of the residuals. The linear functions of the residuals are exactly the linear unbiased estimators of zero (Exercise (B1)). Adding one to an estimator keeps it unbiased and can only add noise. Section 7.2 makes “can only add noise” precise.

7.1.4. Three estimators of a slope

Example (Three linear unbiased estimators of a slope)

A straight line E(Yi)=β0+β1xi is fitted to n=12 observations at the regressor values x=1,2,2.5,3,4,5,5.5,7,8,9,10.5,12, for which Sxx=∑i(xi−x¯)2=137.229. Here X=[1,x] has full rank and the target is 𝛌λ=(0,1)⊤. Three natural estimators of the slope are linear in Y.

  • (i) Least squares, β^1=∑i(xi−x¯)Yi/Sxx (Theorem 5.2.1), with ai=(xi−x¯)/Sxx.

  • (ii) End points, the slope of the line through the first and last observations, (Y12−Y1)/(x12−x1).

  • (iii) Group means, which splits the data into the six smallest and six largest x values and takes (Y¯high−Y¯low)/(x¯high−x¯low).

Each coefficient vector satisfies 1⊤a=0 and x⊤a=1, which is the condition 𝛌X⊤a=λ of Proposition 7.1.1. So all three are unbiased, whatever the distribution of the errors. By Eq. 7.1.2 their variances are σ2‖a‖2: 0.00729σ2,0.01653σ2,0.01008σ2. Least squares has the smallest, and the other two have efficiencies 0.441 and 0.723 relative to it. The listing also confirms what Proposition 7.1.2(b) predicts: the three coefficient vectors differ, but their projections onto C(X) all equal the least squares vector.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 4.0, 5.0, 5.5, 7.0, 8.0, 9.0, 10.5, 12.0])
n = len(x)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])
lam = np.array([0.0, 1.0])                      # target: the slope beta_1
M = X @ np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T)           # projection onto C(X)

# coefficient vectors a of three linear estimators a^T y of the slope
a_ls = X @ np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, lam)        # least squares: a = X (X^T X)^{-1} lambda
a_end = np.zeros(n)
a_end[[0, -1]] = [-1.0, 1.0]
a_end /= x[-1] - x[0]                           # slope through the two end points
low, high = np.arange(n) < n // 2, np.arange(n) >= n // 2
a_grp = (high / high.sum() - low / low.sum()) / (x[high].mean() - x[low].mean())  # group means

for name, a in [("least squares", a_ls), ("end points", a_end), ("group means", a_grp)]:
    print(f"{name:14s} X^T a = {X.T @ a}   Var/sigma^2 = {a @ a:.5f}   Ma = a_ls: {np.allclose(M @ a, a_ls)}")

The end-point estimator throws away ten of the twelve observations, so its poor showing is no surprise. The group-means estimator uses every observation, and it is still clearly worse than least squares. It weights all observations in a half equally, while least squares weights each observation in proportion to its distance from x¯. Estimators of this grouping type were proposed by Wald (1940) for regressions in which x itself is measured with error. Chapter 24 returns to that problem.

7.1.5. Why these restrictions?

Linearity and unbiasedness are restrictions of convenience as well as of principle, and it is worth being clear about what each one does.

Unbiasedness removes estimators that are good only near one parameter value. It is not sacred. Section 7.2 shows biased estimators with smaller mean squared error, and Section 7.6 derives a whole family of them from prior information. Unbiasedness can also be too demanding: a nonestimable 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β has no linear unbiased estimator, and under normality no unbiased estimator of any kind (Exercise (C1)).

Linearity is what makes the second-moment assumptions enough. The variance of a nonlinear estimator, such as a median or a trimmed mean, depends on the whole error distribution. Its variance can’t be compared with that of least squares until that distribution is specified. For linear estimators Eq. 7.1.2 needs only σ2. The restriction therefore has a cost: optimality among linear estimators says nothing about nonlinear ones. Section 7.2 gives an error distribution under which a simple nonlinear estimator is far better than least squares. Section 7.4 shows that under normality this cannot happen.

7.1.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

In the location model Yi=μ+εi (X=1), show that the LUEs of μ are the weighted averages ∑iaiYi with ∑iai=1. Identify 𝛌Aλ and a∗ and find the LUE of smallest variance directly.

Exercise (A2)

In the one-way layout E(Yij)=μ+αi with three groups and an intercept (Example (Four answers, one fit)), show that α1 has no linear unbiased estimator. Write down two different LUEs of α1−α2, and verify that their coefficient vectors have the same projection onto C(X).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

A linear estimator a⊤Y is an unbiased estimator of zero if E(a⊤Y)=0 for every 𝛃β. Show that this holds iff a∈C(X)⊥, iff a⊤Y is a linear function of the residual vector 𝛆ε^. Show that every unbiased estimator of zero is uncorrelated with 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ for every estimable 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β.

Solution. By Proposition 7.1.1 with 𝛌λ=0, a⊤Y is unbiased for zero iff X⊤a=0, iff a∈C(X)⊥. If so, a=(I−M)a and 𝛆a⊤Y=a⊤(I−M)Y=a⊤ε^. Conversely, 𝛆w⊤ε^=((I−M)w)⊤Y has coefficient vector in C(X)⊥. For the last claim, write 𝛌𝛃𝛒λ⊤β^=ρ⊤MY. By Theorem 2.2.1, 𝛒𝛒Cov(ρ⊤MY,a⊤Y)=σ2ρ⊤Ma=0, because Ma=0.

Exercise (B2)

Let Λ be p×q with C(Λ)⊆C(X⊤). Show that AY is an LUE of 𝛃Λ⊤β iff AX=Λ⊤, and that the set of such A is {Λ⊤(X⊤X)−X⊤+W(I−M):W∈Rq×n}.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Assume 𝛃Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I) and 𝛌λ∉C(X⊤). Show that no statistic δ(Y), linear or not, satisfies 𝛌𝛃Eδ(Y)=λ⊤β for all 𝛃β. Hint: the distribution of Y depends on 𝛃β only through 𝛃Xβ.

Solution. Since 𝛌λ∉C(X⊤)=N(X)⊥, there is v∈N(X) with 𝛌λ⊤v≠0. The parameters 𝛃β and 𝛃β+v give the same mean 𝛃Xβ, hence the same distribution of Y, hence the same value of Eδ(Y). An unbiased δ would need 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=λ⊤(β+v), which is false. The argument uses only that the law of Y is a function of 𝛃Xβ and σ2. Chapter 8 calls this nonidentifiability (Definition 8.1.1).

Exercise (C2)

For the group-means estimator of Example (Three linear unbiased estimators of a slope) with general x1≤s˙≤xn (n=2m), find its variance in terms of x¯high−x¯low. Show that its efficiency relative to least squares is (x¯high−x¯low)2n/(4Sxx), and that this is 1 iff the x values take only two distinct values, with half of the observations at each.

7.5  Sampling distributions of the estimates

A point estimate is of little use without a measure of its uncertainty. Under the second-moment assumptions we know the mean and variance of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ and the mean of s2, but not their distributions. Under normality we know everything. The estimates of the estimable functions are jointly normal, the residual sum of squares is a scaled chi-squared variable, and the two are independent. These three facts are what the whole of Part III rests on. Tests, confidence intervals, confidence ellipsoids and prediction intervals are all built from them. The machinery was assembled in Chapter 3 and Chapter 4. Here we apply it.

7.5.1. The main theorem

Theorem 7.5.1 (Sampling distributions in the normal linear model)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with r=rank(X)<n. Let Λ be p×q with C(Λ)⊆C(X⊤), let (X⊤X)− be any generalized inverse, and let s2=SSE/(n−r).

  • (a) 𝛃𝛃Λ⊤β^∼Nq(Λ⊤β, σ2Λ⊤(X⊤X)−Λ). In particular 𝛃Y^=MY∼Nn(Xβ,σ2M), and if rank(X)=p, 𝛃𝛃β^∼Np(β,σ2(X⊤X)−1).

  • (b) SSE/σ2=(n−r)s2/σ2∼χ2(n−r).

  • (c) MY and SSE are independent. Hence 𝛃Λ⊤β^, and every function of the fitted values, is independent of s2.

  • (d) E(s2)=σ2 and Var(s2)=2σ4/(n−r).

Proof. Write Λ=X⊤P for an n×q matrix P. As in the proof of Theorem 7.2.1(d), 𝛃Λ⊤β^=P⊤MY for every least squares estimate 𝛃β^.

  • (a) By Theorem 3.3.1, P⊤MY is normal with mean 𝛃𝛃𝛃P⊤MXβ=P⊤Xβ=Λ⊤β and covariance σ2P⊤MM⊤P=σ2P⊤MP=σ2Λ⊤(X⊤X)−Λ, by Theorem 6.3.7. The special cases are Λ=X⊤, with P=I, and Λ=Ip in full rank.

  • (b) SSE=Y⊤(I−M)Y, and I−M is symmetric and idempotent of rank n−r (Theorem 6.3.3 and Proposition 6.3.4). By Theorem 4.3.2(a), 𝛃SSE/σ2∼χ2(n−r,‖(I−M)Xβ‖2/σ2), and the noncentrality is zero because (I−M)X=0.

  • (c) Apply Theorem 4.4.1 with B=M, A=I−M and Σ=σ2I. Then BΣA=σ2M(I−M)=0, so MY and Y⊤(I−M)Y are independent. Functions of independent random vectors are independent.

  • (d) A χ2(k) variable has mean k and variance 2k (Theorem 4.1.2(b) with γ=0). So ESSE=σ2(n−r) and Var(SSE)=2σ4(n−r). Divide by n−r and (n−r)2.

The proof is short because the work was done earlier. It is worth seeing where each ingredient comes from geometrically. The fitted vector MY and the residual vector (I−M)Y are projections of Y onto orthogonal subspaces. For a spherical normal vector, orthogonal projections are independent (Theorem 4.4.4). Everything about 𝛃β lives in the first projection. The second is pure noise, and its squared length, measured in units of σ2, counts n−r independent squared standard normals.

The independence in (c) has no analogue under the second-moment assumptions. If the errors are independent with zero third moment, 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ and s2 are still uncorrelated, but in general they are not independent. The simulation at the end of the section shows the difference.

7.5.2. Standardizing: the t statistic

The distribution in (a) involves the unknown σ. Since E(s2)=σ2, the matrix s2Λ⊤(X⊤X)−Λ is an unbiased estimate of 𝛃Cov(Λ⊤β^), and in full rank s2(X⊤X)−1 is unbiased for 𝛃Cov(β^); this needs only the second-moment assumptions. The square roots of its diagonal entries are the standard errors reported by regression software. Replacing σ by s changes the normal distribution into a t distribution, and independence is what makes the change exact.

Corollary 7.5.2 (The t statistic for an estimable function)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with r<n, let 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤) with 𝛌λ≠0, and put 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌se(λ⊤β^)=sλ⊤(X⊤X)−λ. For every constant d, 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌Td=λ⊤β^−dse(λ⊤β^)∼t(n−r,δ),δ=λ⊤β−dσλ⊤(X⊤X)−λ. In particular 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃T=(λ⊤β^−λ⊤β)/se(λ⊤β^)∼t(n−r), and

𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃Pr{|λ⊤β^−λ⊤β|≤tn−r,α/2se(λ⊤β^)}=1−α,
(7.5.1)

where tn−r,α/2 is the upper α/2 point of t(n−r).

Proof. Put 𝛌𝛌c2=λ⊤(X⊤X)−λ. Then c>0, since 𝛒c2=‖Mρ‖2 with 𝛒𝛌X⊤Mρ=λ≠0. By Theorem 7.5.1(a), 𝛌𝛃Z=(λ⊤β^−d)/(σc)∼N(δ,1). By (b), V=(n−r)s2/σ2∼χ2(n−r), and by (c) Z and V are independent. Now Td=Z/V/(n−r), which is t(n−r,δ) by Definition 4.1.3. With 𝛌𝛃d=λ⊤β, δ=0, and Eq. 7.5.1 restates Pr{|T|≤tn−r,α/2}=1−α.

Eq. 7.5.1 is the familiar confidence interval 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^±tn−r,α/2se, and Td is the statistic for testing 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=d. Its noncentrality δ governs the power. Chapter 11 and Chapter 12 develop both. For the air-flow coefficient in the stack loss regression this interval was (0.431, 1.000) (Example (Profile likelihood for the air-flow coefficient)). Two features of the corollary are easy to overlook. The degrees of freedom are n−r, the dimension of the residual space. They are not n, and when X is rank deficient they are not n−p either. And the interval is exact for every sample size, which no large-sample argument delivers.

7.5.3. Several functions at once

For a vector of estimable functions, the natural summary of Theorem 7.5.1(a) is a quadratic form. The covariance matrix Λ⊤(X⊤X)−Λ may be singular when the columns of Λ are linearly dependent, so a generalized inverse is needed.

Corollary 7.5.3 (A chi-squared pivot for several functions)

In the setting of Theorem 7.5.1, let W=Λ⊤(X⊤X)−Λ, let W− be any generalized inverse of W, and put 𝛃𝛃D=Λ⊤β^−Λ⊤β. Then Q=D⊤W−Dσ2∼χ2(rank(Λ)), Q does not depend on the choice of W− (with probability one), and Q is independent of SSE. Consequently D⊤W−D/rank(Λ)s2∼F(rank(Λ), n−r).

Proof. By Theorem 7.5.1(a), D∼Nq(0,σ2W). The quadratic form is unchanged if W− is replaced by its symmetric part 12(W−+W−⊤), which is again a generalized inverse of the symmetric matrix W (Proposition 1.3.3(d)). So we may assume W− symmetric. Apply Theorem 4.3.2(c) with Σ=σ2W, 𝛍μ=0 and A=W−/σ2. Conditions (ii) and (iii) hold trivially because 𝛍μ=0. Condition (i) is ΣAΣAΣ=σ2WW−WW−W=σ2W=ΣAΣ, by two uses of WW−W=W. So Q∼χ2(tr(AΣ)), where tr(AΣ)=tr(W−W)=rank(W), because W−W is idempotent with the same rank as W (Proposition 1.3.3(a)), and the trace of an idempotent matrix is its rank (Proposition 1.9.1).

It remains to show rank(W)=rank(Λ). With Λ=X⊤P, W=P⊤MP=(MP)⊤(MP), so rankW=rank(MP). Also Λ=X⊤P=X⊤MP. If X⊤MPu=0, then MPu∈C(X)∩C(X)⊥={0}, so X⊤ is one-to-one on C(MP) and rankΛ=rank(MP).

For invariance: D∈C(W) with probability one by Theorem 2.2.3(d). If D=Wu, then D⊤W−D=u⊤WW−Wu=u⊤Wu, whatever W− is. Independence follows from Theorem 7.5.1(c), since D is a function of MY. The F ratio is then a ratio of independent chi-squared variables divided by their degrees of freedom (Definition 4.1.2).

The set of 𝛃Λ⊤β values for which this F ratio stays below an F quantile is a confidence ellipsoid. Chapter 12 studies these regions, and Chapter 11 turns the same statistic into the test of the general linear hypothesis. Here we note only that the degrees of freedom in the numerator are the number of linearly independent functions being estimated, not the number of rows of Λ⊤.

Example (A rank-deficient model, simulated)

Consider a one-way layout with three groups of sizes 4, 5, 6, an intercept, one indicator column per group, and a covariate. So n=15 and p=5, but the rank is r=4, because the indicators add up to the intercept. The contrast 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=α1−α2, with 𝛌λ=(0,1,−1,0,0)⊤, is estimable. With σ=2, Theorem 7.5.1 gives 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌Var(λ⊤β^)=σ2λ⊤(X⊤X)−λ=1.9007, computed with the Moore–Penrose inverse. In 100,000 simulated data sets the variance of the estimated contrast is 1.9017, and the average of SSE/σ2 is 11.001, against n−r=11. The sample correlation between the contrast estimate and s2 is 0.0036. The standardized contrast T falls within ±2.201, the 97.5% point of t(11), in a proportion 0.9498 of the samples. Figure 7.5.1 compares the simulated distributions with the theory. Near the centre the t(11) and N(0,1) densities are hard to tell apart, so panel (b) uses a logarithmic density scale, on which the tails separate: the simulated points follow t(11), and the normal density falls away too fast. The tail mass says the same in numbers. A proportion 0.0758 of the samples have |T|>1.96, against 0.05 under N(0,1) and 0.0758 under t(11). A nominal 95% interval built with the normal point 1.96 would therefore undercover.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

groups = np.repeat([0, 1, 2], [4, 5, 6])
z = np.array([2.1, 3.4, 1.8, 4.0, 2.9, 3.3, 5.1, 2.2, 3.8, 1.5, 4.4, 2.7, 3.9, 5.0, 3.1])
n = len(z)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), np.eye(3)[groups], z])   # p = 5 columns, rank 4
r = np.linalg.matrix_rank(X)
G = np.linalg.pinv(X.T @ X)                                # one generalized inverse
M = X @ G @ X.T
lam = np.array([0.0, 1.0, -1.0, 0.0, 0.0])                 # group 1 minus group 2: estimable
beta, sigma = np.array([1.0, 0.5, -0.5, 0.0, 0.8]), 2.0
var_lam = sigma ** 2 * lam @ G @ lam                       # Var(lambda^T beta_hat)
print(f"n = {n}, p = {X.shape[1]}, rank = {r}, Var(lambda^T beta_hat) = {var_lam:.4f}")
rng = np.random.default_rng(75)
reps = 100_000
Y = X @ beta + sigma * rng.normal(size=(reps, n))          # one data set per row
est = Y @ (X @ G @ lam)                                    # lambda^T beta_hat = lambda^T G X^T y
sse = np.sum((Y - Y @ M) ** 2, axis=1)                     # y^T (I - M) y
s2 = sse / (n - r)
t = (est - lam @ beta) / np.sqrt(s2 * (lam @ G @ lam))
print(f"mean {est.mean():.4f}, variance {est.var():.4f}")
print(f"E(SSE)/sigma^2 = {np.mean(sse) / sigma**2:.3f}   (n - r = {n - r})")
print(f"corr(estimate, s^2) = {np.corrcoef(est, s2)[0, 1]:.4f}")
print("KS p-value, SSE/sigma^2 vs chi^2(n-r):", round(stats.kstest(sse / sigma**2, "chi2", args=(n - r,)).pvalue, 3))
print("KS p-value, t vs t(n-r):              ", round(stats.kstest(t, "t", args=(n - r,)).pvalue, 3))

7.5.4. Without normality

Which parts of Theorem 7.5.1 survive when the errors are merely uncorrelated with common variance? The first two moments of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ do, by Theorem 7.2.1, and so does E(s2)=σ2. The normality of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ is lost, but it often returns approximately. 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ is a weighted sum of independent errors, and a central limit theorem applies when no single observation dominates the weights. Chapter 19 makes this precise (Theorem 19.3.2). The distribution of SSE is less forgiving, because its variance depends on the fourth moment of the errors.

Proposition 7.5.4 (Variance of SSE without normality)

Let the errors be independent with mean zero, variance σ2, third moment μ3 and fourth moment μ4, and let hii be the leverages. Then Var(SSE)=2σ4(n−r)+(μ4−3σ4)∑i=1n(1−hii)2.

Proof. Apply Theorem 2.3.3 to SSE=Y⊤(I−M)Y, with 𝛉𝛃θ=Xβ and A=I−M, whose diagonal entries are 1−hii. The terms 𝛉𝛉4σ2θ⊤A2θ and 𝛉4μ3θ⊤Aa vanish because 𝛉𝛃Aθ=(I−M)Xβ=0, and tr(A2)=tr(A)=n−r.

The factor μ4−3σ4 is σ4 times the excess kurtosis. For normal errors it vanishes and we recover 2σ4(n−r). For heavy-tailed errors it is positive, and SSE is more variable than the chi-squared theory says. Intervals for σ2 built from Theorem 7.5.1(b) are then too short, and this does not improve with sample size: the ratio of the true variance to the nominal one tends to 1+12(μ4/σ4−3) when the leverages are small. The t interval for 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β is much less affected, because s2 enters only through s, and s converges to σ whatever the kurtosis.

Example (Laplace errors)

Rerun the simulation of Example (A rank-deficient model, simulated) with Laplace (double-exponential) errors of the same variance σ2=4. For these μ4=6σ4. The contrast still has variance 1.9007. For SSE, ∑i(1−hii)2=8.138, and Proposition 7.5.4 gives Var(SSE)=742.6, more than twice the normal-theory value 352.0. The simulated variance is 741.5. Independence fails too. The contrast and SSE are still uncorrelated, because the errors are symmetric, but the squared error of the contrast has correlation 0.211 with SSE. Samples with a few large errors inflate both.

E_lap = rng.laplace(scale=sigma / np.sqrt(2), size=(reps, n))   # variance sigma^2, mu_4 = 6 sigma^4
Y_lap = X @ beta + E_lap
est_lap = Y_lap @ (X @ G @ lam)
sse_lap = np.sum((Y_lap - Y_lap @ M) ** 2, axis=1)
h = np.diag(np.eye(n) - M)
var_sse_lap = (6 - 3) * sigma**4 * h @ h + 2 * sigma**4 * (n - r)
print(f"Var(SSE): normal theory {2 * sigma**4 * (n - r):.1f}, Laplace formula {var_sse_lap:.1f}, "
      f"simulated {sse_lap.var():.1f}")
print(f"corr(squared error of estimate, SSE) under Laplace errors: "
      f"{np.corrcoef((est_lap - lam @ beta) ** 2, sse_lap)[0, 1]:.3f}")

In the listing, h holds the diagonal of I−M, that is, 1−hii.

7.5.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Under Eq. 7.3.1, find the joint distribution of 𝛆(Y^,ε^). Show that Y^ and 𝛆ε^ are independent, and that 𝛆ε^∼Nn(0,σ2(I−M)) whatever 𝛃β is. Is 𝛆ε^ independent of SSE?

Exercise (A2)

In simple linear regression, write out T of Corollary 7.5.2 for the slope, in terms of Sxx and s, and state its distribution. What is r?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let 𝛌𝛌λ1,λ2∈C(X⊤). Show that 𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛃𝛌𝛌Cov(λ1⊤β^,λ2⊤β^)=σ2λ1⊤(X⊤X)−λ2, and that under normality the two estimates are independent iff 𝛌𝛌λ1⊤(X⊤X)−λ2=0. In a one-way layout with a groups of m observations each and cell-means parameterization E(Yij)=μi, show that the estimates of ∑iciμi and ∑idiμi are independent iff ∑icidi=0.

Solution. The covariance is the off-diagonal entry of Theorem 7.5.1(a) with 𝛌𝛌Λ=[λ1,λ2]. Under normality the pair is bivariate normal, so zero covariance is equivalent to independence (Theorem 3.4.1). In the cell-means model X⊤X=mIa, so 𝛌𝛌λ1⊤(X⊤X)−1λ2=∑icidi/m, which vanishes iff ∑icidi=0.

Exercise (B2)

A new observation 𝛃Y0=x0⊤β+ε0 will be taken, with ε0∼N(0,σ2) independent of Y and x0∈C(X⊤). Show that 𝛃Y0−x0⊤β^s1+x0⊤(X⊤X)−x0∼t(n−r), and deduce a prediction interval for Y0. Why is the extra 1 in the denominator never negligible?

Solution. Y0 is independent of Y, so 𝛃Y0−x0⊤β^ is normal with mean 𝛃𝛃x0⊤β−x0⊤β=0 and variance σ2+σ2x0⊤(X⊤X)−x0. It is a function of (Y0,MY), which is independent of SSE. Dividing by σ1+x0⊤(X⊤X)−x0 gives a standard normal, independent of (n−r)s2/σ2∼χ2(n−r), and the ratio is t(n−r) by Definition 4.1.3. The interval is 𝛃x0⊤β^±tn−r,α/2s1+x0⊤(X⊤X)−x0. The 1 is the variance of the new error itself, which no amount of data about 𝛃β can reduce. The second term tends to zero as the design grows. The first does not.

Exercise (B3)

Use Theorem 7.5.1(b) to construct an exact 1−α confidence interval for σ2. Then, using Proposition 7.5.4 with small leverages, explain why its coverage does not tend to 1−α as n→∞ when the errors have nonzero excess kurtosis.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Give a second proof of Theorem 7.5.1(b) and (c) using Theorem 4.4.4 with the two projections M and I−M. Extend it: if C(X0)⊆C(X), show that ‖(M−M0)Y‖2 and SSE are independent, and find the distribution of ‖(M−M0)Y‖2/σ2 when 𝛃E(Y)=Xβ is arbitrary.

Exercise (C2)

For the test that rejects 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β=d when |Td|>tn−r,α/2, show that the power depends on the parameters only through |δ| of Corollary 7.5.2. For a fixed design, how does the power change when the sample is replicated m times (X replaced by m stacked copies)?

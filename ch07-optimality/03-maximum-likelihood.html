7.3  Maximum likelihood under normality

The Gauss–Markov theorem needs only two moments. That is its strength, and also the reason it can say nothing about σ2, nothing about nonlinear estimators, and nothing about the distribution of the estimates. From now on we assume more: the errors are normal. The linear model becomes a fully specified family of distributions,

Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2I),β∈Rp, σ2>0,
(7.3.1)

the normal linear model of Definition 5.3.1. The first general-purpose method of estimation in such a family is maximum likelihood. This section shows that it gives back least squares for β, and gives a slightly different estimate of σ2.

7.3.1. The likelihood

By Theorem 3.2.2 with Σ=σ2I, which is positive definite with det(σ2I)=σ2n, the density of Y at the observed y is L(β,σ2)=(2πσ2)−n/2exp⁡{−‖y−Xβ‖22σ2}. As a function of the parameters with y held fixed, this is the likelihood. Its logarithm is

ℓ(β,σ2)=−n2log⁡(2π)−n2log⁡σ2−‖y−Xβ‖22σ2.
(7.3.2)

The data enter the log-likelihood only through the sum of squares ‖y−Xβ‖2. That is the first sign that normality and least squares belong together. The Gaussian density penalizes a residual by its square, so the most likely parameter values are those with the smallest squared residuals.

One technical point matters. If y∈C(X), the residual sum of squares at the least squares fit is zero, and ℓ→∞ as σ2→0, so no maximum exists. This happens with probability zero when n>r. The subspace C(X) has dimension r<n, so it has Lebesgue measure zero, and Y has a density. We therefore assume n>r throughout, and SSE>0 for the observed data.

7.3.2. The maximum likelihood estimates

Theorem 7.3.1 (Maximum likelihood in the normal linear model)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with n>r, and let y be such that SSE=‖(I−M)y‖2>0. Then (β~,σ~2) maximizes the likelihood iff β~ is a least squares estimate and σ~2=σ^ML2=SSEn. The maximized log-likelihood is ℓmax=−n2{log⁡(2πSSE/n)+1}. The maximum likelihood estimate of Xβ is My, and for estimable λ⊤β it is λ⊤β^. Both are unique.

Proof. Fix σ2>0. By Eq. 7.3.2, ℓ(b,σ2) is a decreasing function of ‖y−Xb‖2. So it is maximized over b exactly at the least squares estimates, and there ‖y−Xb‖2=SSE (Theorem 6.4.1). This gives ℓ(b,σ2)≤ℓ(β^,σ2)=g(σ2):=−n2log⁡(2π)−n2log⁡σ2−SSE2σ2, with equality iff b is a least squares estimate. Now put v0=SSE/n and u=v0/σ2>0. Then SSE/(2σ2)=nu/2 and log⁡σ2=log⁡v0−log⁡u, so g(v0)−g(σ2)=n2(u−1−log⁡u)≥0, because log⁡u≤u−1 for all u>0, with equality only at u=1. Hence ℓ(b,σ2)≤g(σ2)≤g(v0), with equality throughout iff b is a least squares estimate and σ2=v0. The value g(v0) is the stated maximum. The least squares estimates all give the fitted vector My (Theorem 6.4.1), and all give the same value of λ⊤β^ for λ∈C(X⊤) (Theorem 6.4.4).

The proof finds a global maximum without solving any equations. Calculus gives the same answer and is the usual route in a first course: set the gradient ∂ℓ/∂β=X⊤(y−Xβ)/σ2 to zero, which gives the normal equations, and ∂ℓ/∂σ2=−n/(2σ2)+‖y−Xβ‖2/(2σ4) to zero. But the stationarity argument still has to be completed by showing that the stationary point is a maximum (Exercise (B1)). The inequality log⁡u≤u−1 handles existence, maximality and uniqueness together.

When X is rank deficient, the likelihood is constant along the affine set of least squares estimates β^+N(X), so the MLE of β is not unique. This is a general fact about nonidentified parameters. If Xβ1=Xβ2, the two parameter values give the same distribution of Y and hence the same likelihood for every data set. Maximum likelihood cannot separate what the model itself does not separate. What it does determine uniquely, Xβ and the estimable functions, is exactly what least squares determines uniquely.

Example (Stack loss)

Brownlee’s stack loss data record n=21 days of operation of a plant that oxidizes ammonia to nitric acid. The response is the percentage of ammonia lost (times ten). The regressors are air flow, the temperature of the cooling water, and the acid concentration. With an intercept, p=4, and least squares gives β^=(−39.9197, 0.7156, 1.2953, −0.1521)⊤,SSE=178.830. By Theorem 7.3.1 these are also the maximum likelihood estimates of β. The MLE of σ2 is 178.830/21=8.516, while the unbiased estimate is s2=178.830/17=10.519. The maximized log-likelihood is −52.288. A general-purpose numerical optimizer, started far from the answer and knowing nothing about least squares, arrives at the same point.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import optimize, stats

data = sm.datasets.stackloss.load_pandas().data
y = data["STACKLOSS"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), data[["AIRFLOW", "WATERTEMP", "ACIDCONC"]]])
n, p = X.shape

beta_hat, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
sse = np.sum((y - X @ beta_hat) ** 2)
sigma2_mle, s2 = sse / n, sse / (n - p)
loglik_max = -n / 2 * (np.log(2 * np.pi * sigma2_mle) + 1)
print("beta_hat   ", np.round(beta_hat, 4))
print(f"SSE = {sse:.3f}   MLE of sigma^2 = {sigma2_mle:.3f}   s^2 = {s2:.3f}")
print(f"maximized log-likelihood = {loglik_max:.3f}")
def negloglik(theta):
    """Minus the normal log-likelihood; theta = (beta, log sigma^2)."""
    b, log_s2 = theta[:p], theta[p]
    r = y - X @ b
    return n / 2 * (np.log(2 * np.pi) + log_s2) + r @ r / (2 * np.exp(log_s2))

start = np.r_[np.zeros(p), np.log(np.var(y))]
opt = optimize.minimize(negloglik, start, method="BFGS", options={"gtol": 1e-8})
print("numerical MLE of beta  ", np.round(opt.x[:p], 4))
print("numerical MLE sigma^2  ", round(float(np.exp(opt.x[p])), 3))

7.3.3. The bias of the variance estimate

Unlike least squares for β, the MLE of σ2 is biased. Since E(SSE)=σ2(n−r) (Theorem 5.6.1 in full rank, and Section 6.4 in general), Eσ^ML2=n−rnσ2. The bias is downward, by the fraction r/n. The residual vector lives in the (n−r)-dimensional space C(X)⊥, and the MLE divides its squared length by n as if it had n dimensions. The r dimensions spent on fitting the mean are the dimensions in which the fit has pulled the data toward itself. With a few regressors and many observations the bias is negligible. It is not negligible when the number of parameters grows with the sample size.

Example (Many means, one variance)

Suppose m laboratories each make two measurements, Yi1,Yi2∼iidN(μi,σ2), with a different μi for each laboratory and a common measurement variance. This is a linear model with n=2m and r=m, one indicator column per laboratory. The fitted value for each pair is its mean, and σ^ML2=12m∑i=1m∑j=12(Yij−Y¯i⋅)2=14m∑i=1m(Yi1−Yi2)2,Eσ^ML2=σ22. The bias does not disappear as m→∞. By the law of large numbers, σ^ML2→σ2/2 with probability one, so the MLE is inconsistent. The unbiased s2=SSE/(n−r) is exactly twice as large and is consistent. This is the example of Neyman and Scott (1948). It shows that the good large-sample behaviour of maximum likelihood can fail when the number of nuisance parameters grows with the data. Restricted maximum likelihood, which removes the mean before estimating the variance (Exercise (C2)), repairs it, and Chapter 32 builds variance-component estimation on that idea.

Unbiasedness is not the only possible criterion for choosing a divisor. Under normality we can compare estimators of the form SSE/d by mean squared error.

Proposition 7.3.2 (The best multiple of SSE)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with k=n−r≥1. Among estimators SSE/d with d>0, E(SSE/d−σ2)2σ4=2kd2+(kd−1)2, which is minimized at d=k+2, with minimum value 2/(k+2). The unbiased choice d=k gives 2/k, and the maximum likelihood choice d=n gives (2k+r2)/n2.

Proof. Write SSE=Y⊤(I−M)Y. By Theorem 4.2.3 with A=I−M, Σ=σ2I and μ=Xβ, for which Aμ=0, E(SSE)=σ2tr(I−M)=σ2k,Var(SSE)=2σ4tr((I−M)2)=2σ4k. The mean squared error is variance plus squared bias: 2σ4k/d2+(k/d−1)2σ4. As a function of t=1/d, this is σ4{(2k+k2)t2−2kt+1}, a convex quadratic minimized at t=k/(2k+k2)=1/(k+2). Substituting d=k+2, k and n=k+r gives the three values. For the last, 2k/n2+(k−n)2/n2=(2k+r2)/n2.

For the stack loss data, k=17, and the three relative mean squared errors are 0.1176 for s2, 0.1134 for the MLE and 0.1053 for SSE/(k+2). The unbiased estimator is the worst of the three by this criterion. Comparing (2k+r2)/n2 with 2/k shows that the MLE beats s2 iff (r−4)k<2r. So it always does when r≤4, and it loses once many parameters are fitted relative to the residual degrees of freedom, as in Example (Many means, one variance). In practice s2 is used anyway, because it is what makes the t and F statistics of Section 7.5 exact. The choice of divisor matters for point estimation of σ2 and for nothing else. Any fixed multiple of SSE leads to the same tests and intervals once the matching distribution is used.

7.3.4. Profile likelihood

In most problems only some parameters are of interest. The others, called nuisance parameters, still appear in the likelihood. A simple and general way to eliminate them is to maximize them out.

Definition 7.3.1 (Profile likelihood)

Let the parameter be θ=(ψ,η), where ψ is of interest and η is a nuisance parameter. The profile log-likelihood of ψ is ℓp(ψ)=supηℓ(ψ,η).

The profile log-likelihood is maximized at the MLE of ψ, with maximum ℓmax. The drop ℓmax−ℓp(ψ) measures how much less plausible the value ψ is than the best value. In the linear model the profiles have closed forms. The proof of Theorem 7.3.1 computed two of them. With σ2 as nuisance, the profile of β is ℓp(β)=−n2{log⁡(2π‖y−Xβ‖2n)+1}, a decreasing function of ‖y−Xβ‖2. Its level sets are the level sets of the residual sum of squares. When X has full rank they are ellipsoids centred at β^, and they are the basis of the confidence ellipsoids of Chapter 12. With β as nuisance, the profile of σ2 is the function g in the proof.

The most useful case is a single estimable function. Here the profile likelihood turns out to be a function of the familiar t statistic.

Proposition 7.3.3 (Profile likelihood of an estimable function)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with n>r, and let λ∈C(X⊤), λ≠0. For ψ∈R, put t(ψ)=λ⊤β^−ψsλ⊤(X⊤X)−λ,s2=SSEn−r. Then the smallest residual sum of squares subject to λ⊤β=ψ is

SSE(ψ)=SSE+(λ⊤β^−ψ)2λ⊤(X⊤X)−λ,
(7.3.3)

and the profile log-likelihood of ψ=λ⊤β, with (β,σ2) otherwise free, satisfies

2{ℓmax−ℓp(ψ)}=nlog⁡(1+t(ψ)2n−r).
(7.3.4)

Proof. Write λ=X⊤ρ and c=Mρ. Then c≠0, since X⊤c=X⊤ρ=λ≠0, and ‖c‖2=λ⊤(X⊤X)−λ as in the proof of Theorem 7.2.1(a). For any b, Eq. 6.3.1 gives ‖y−Xb‖2=SSE+‖Xβ^−Xb‖2. Put u=X(β^−b)∈C(X). The constraint λ⊤b=ψ reads ρ⊤X(β^−b)=λ⊤β^−ψ=:δ, that is, ρ⊤u=δ. Since u∈C(X), ρ⊤u=ρ⊤Mu=c⊤u. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, |δ|=|c⊤u|≤‖c‖‖u‖, so ‖u‖2≥δ2/‖c‖2. Equality holds for u=δc/‖c‖2, which lies in C(X) and so equals X(β^−b) for some b. That b satisfies the constraint. This proves Eq. 7.3.3. Maximizing over σ2 as in the proof of Theorem 7.3.1, with SSE replaced by SSE(ψ), gives ℓp(ψ)=−n2{log⁡(2πSSE(ψ)/n)+1}. Hence 2{ℓmax−ℓp(ψ)}=nlog⁡SSE(ψ)SSE=nlog⁡(1+δ2‖c‖2SSE)=nlog⁡(1+t(ψ)2n−r), since δ2/(‖c‖2SSE)=t(ψ)2/(n−r).

Two consequences show up immediately. First, any procedure based on the profile likelihood of λ⊤β is a procedure based on |t|, because Eq. 7.3.4 is increasing in t2. The likelihood ratio test of λ⊤β=ψ0 is therefore the t test, a fact Chapter 11 extends to general hypotheses. Second, the large-sample calibration of the likelihood ratio, “reject when 2{ℓmax−ℓp} exceeds a χ2(1) quantile”, is only approximate. The exact calibration uses the t(n−r) distribution of Section 7.5.

Example (Profile likelihood for the air-flow coefficient)

For the stack loss model, Figure 7.3.1 plots ℓp(β1)−ℓmax for the air-flow coefficient β1, computed by refitting the model with β1 fixed on a grid. The listing confirms Eq. 7.3.4 to rounding error. The standard error of β^1 is 0.1349. The set of β1 with 2{ℓmax−ℓp}≤3.841, the 95% point of χ2(1), is the likelihood interval (0.467, 0.965). By Eq. 7.3.4 it consists of the values with |t|≤1.847. The exact 95% interval uses the t(17) quantile 2.110 instead, and is (0.431, 1.000). With only 17 residual degrees of freedom, the χ2 calibration makes the interval about 12% too short.

def profile_loglik(b1):
    """Profile log-likelihood of the air-flow coefficient: maximize over everything else."""
    others = np.delete(X, 1, axis=1)
    z = y - b1 * X[:, 1]                                 # move the fixed term to the left side
    coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(others, z, rcond=None)
    sse_b = np.sum((z - others @ coef) ** 2)             # SSE(b1): smallest SSE with beta_1 = b1
    return -n / 2 * (np.log(2 * np.pi * sse_b / n) + 1)

se1 = np.sqrt(s2 * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)[1, 1])
grid = np.linspace(beta_hat[1] - 4 * se1, beta_hat[1] + 4 * se1, 201)
lp = np.array([profile_loglik(b) for b in grid])
t = (beta_hat[1] - grid) / se1
print("max |2(l_max - l_p) - n log(1 + t^2/(n-p))| =",
      np.abs(2 * (loglik_max - lp) - n * np.log1p(t ** 2 / (n - p))).max())

Remark (Other error distributions). The agreement between maximum likelihood and least squares is special to normal errors. If the errors have the Laplace density (2τ)−1e−|ε|/τ, the log-likelihood is −nlog⁡(2τ)−∑i|yi−x(i)⊤β|/τ, and maximum likelihood minimizes the sum of absolute residuals; Exercise (C1) works out the location case. For errors with a t distribution it downweights large residuals. For each error distribution the likelihood picks its own loss function. Least squares is the one that goes with the Gaussian. Chapter 20 uses robust fitting as a diagnostic, and Chapter 45 treats median and quantile regression.

7.3.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For Y1,s˙,Yn∼iidN(μ,σ2), write the model in the form Eq. 7.3.1 and read off the MLEs from Theorem 7.3.1. Show that σ^ML2=(n−1)s2/n, where s2 is the sample variance.

Exercise (A2)

Let C(X0)⊆C(X) with residual sums of squares SSE0≥SSE. Show that the ratio of maximized likelihoods is L0,max/Lmax=(SSE/SSE0)n/2, and write it as a decreasing function of (SSE0−SSE)/SSE.

Solution. By Theorem 7.3.1, Lmax=(2πSSE/n)−n/2e−n/2, and similarly for the smaller model. The ratio is (SSE0/SSE)−n/2={1+(SSE0−SSE)/SSE}−n/2, which decreases in (SSE0−SSE)/SSE. That quantity is, up to degrees of freedom, the F statistic of Theorem 4.4.6. So the likelihood ratio test for nested normal linear models is the F test. Chapter 11 develops this.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Assume rank(X)=p. Compute the gradient and the Hessian of ℓ(β,σ2), show that the only stationary point is (β^,SSE/n), and show that the Hessian there is negative definite. Why does this not by itself prove that the stationary point is a global maximum? What additional argument completes the proof?

Exercise (B2)

In Example (Many means, one variance), verify the identity ∑j(Yij−Y¯i⋅)2=(Yi1−Yi2)2/2, compute Eσ^ML2 and Varσ^ML2, and prove that σ^ML2→σ2/2 in probability as m→∞.

Solution. Yi1−Y¯i⋅=(Yi1−Yi2)/2=−(Yi2−Y¯i⋅), so the sum of squares is 2⋅(Yi1−Yi2)2/4. Now Di=Yi1−Yi2∼N(0,2σ2) independently, so Di2/(2σ2)∼χ2(1), with mean 1 and variance 2. Hence σ^ML2=14m∑iDi2 has mean 2σ2/4=σ2/2 and variance m⋅4σ4⋅2/(16m2)=σ4/(2m). The variance tends to zero, so by Chebyshev’s inequality σ^ML2→σ2/2 in probability.

Exercise (B3)

Suppose Y∼Nn(Xβ,σ2V) with V known and positive definite and X of full rank. Find the MLEs of β and σ2.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

In the location model Yi=μ+εi, i=1,s˙,n, let the errors be independent with the Laplace density (2τ)−1e−|ε|/τ, so Var(εi)=2τ2.

  • (a) Show that every sample median maximizes the likelihood in μ, whatever τ is, and that the MLE of τ is the mean absolute deviation n−1∑i|Yi−μ~| about a median μ~.

  • (b) The sample median of n observations from a density f that is positive and continuous at its median μ has asymptotic variance 1/{4nf(μ)2}. Compute this for the Laplace density and compare it with Var(Y¯). Which is larger, and by what factor?

  • (c) Y¯ is the BLUE of μ (Corollary 7.2.2). Explain why (b) does not contradict Theorem 7.2.1, and contrast the answer with Example (Uniform errors and the midrange), where the winning nonlinear estimator used the extremes rather than the middle of the sample.

Solution.

  • (a) The log-likelihood is −nlog⁡(2τ)−τ−1∑i|Yi−μ|. For fixed τ it is maximized by minimizing g(μ)=∑i|Yi−μ|. The function g is convex and piecewise linear, with slope #{i:Yi<μ}−#{i:Yi>μ} between data points. The slope is ≤0 to the left of every median and ≥0 to the right, so the minimizers are exactly the sample medians. Substituting μ~ and maximizing −nlog⁡τ−g(μ~)/τ over τ>0 gives τ^=g(μ~)/n.
  • (b) f(μ)=1/(2τ), so the median has asymptotic variance τ2/n, while Var(Y¯)=2τ2/n. The mean has twice the variance of the median.
  • (c) The median is not a linear function of Y: its weights depend on the ordering of the sample. The Gauss–Markov theorem compares Y¯ only with linear unbiased estimators. With Laplace errors, which have heavier tails than the normal, the extremes are unreliable and the median gains by ignoring them; with uniform errors the extremes are the most informative observations and the midrange gains by using only them.
Exercise (C2)

Let Q2 be an n×(n−r) matrix whose columns are an orthonormal basis of C(X)⊥, and let Z=Q2⊤Y (“error contrasts”). Show that Z∼Nn−r(0,σ2I), whatever β is, and that ‖Z‖2=SSE. Show that the MLE of σ2 based on Z alone is SSE/(n−r). This is restricted (or residual) maximum likelihood.

Solution. Q2⊤X=0, so Z=Q2⊤ε∼Nn−r(0,σ2Q2⊤Q2)=Nn−r(0,σ2I) by Theorem 3.3.1. Also Q2Q2⊤=I−M (Proposition 6.3.2), so ‖Z‖2=Y⊤Q2Q2⊤Y=Y⊤(I−M)Y=SSE. The log-likelihood of Z is −n−r2log⁡(2πσ2)−‖Z‖2/(2σ2), which is the function g of the proof of Theorem 7.3.1 with n replaced by n−r. It is maximized at ‖Z‖2/(n−r)=SSE/(n−r). In Example (Many means, one variance) this gives s2, which is consistent.

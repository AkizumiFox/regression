7.6  Bayesian thread I: the conjugate linear model

The previous sections judged estimators by their behaviour over repeated samples, for each fixed value of the parameters. The Bayesian approach treats (β,σ2) as uncertain quantities with a probability distribution. It combines a prior distribution, expressing what is known before the data, with the likelihood of Section 7.3, and the result is a posterior distribution. For the normal linear model there is a family of priors for which the posterior is available in closed form, and it connects directly to the rest of the chapter. The posterior mean is a matrix-weighted combination of the prior mean and the least squares estimate. It is biased in the frequentist sense, which is how it escapes the Gauss–Markov bound. And as the prior becomes flat, the posterior reproduces least squares and the t intervals of Section 7.5 exactly. This is the first of several Bayesian threads in the book. Chapter 12 continues it with credible regions and predictive distributions, Chapter 27 with shrinkage priors, and Chapter 39 with generalized linear models.

7.6.1. The normal-inverse-gamma family

Definition 7.6.1 (Inverse gamma and normal-inverse-gamma distributions)

  • (a) A positive random variable V has the inverse gamma distribution IG(a,b), with a,b>0, if 1/V has the gamma distribution with shape a and rate b. Its density is π(v)=baΓ(a)v−a−1e−b/v,v>0.

  • (b) The pair (β,σ2) has the normal-inverse-gamma distribution NIG(m,V,a,b), with m∈Rp, V positive definite and a,b>0, if σ2∼IG(a,b) and, given σ2, β∼Np(m,σ2V). Its joint density is

    π(β,σ2)=baΓ(a)(2π)p/2det(V)1/2(σ2)−(a+p/2)−1×exp⁡{−2b+(β−m)⊤V−1(β−m)2σ2}.
    (7.6.1)

The prior on β is scaled by σ2: prior uncertainty about the coefficients is measured in units of the noise level. This is a modelling choice, and it is what makes the family conjugate. It also makes V dimensionless relative to the likelihood’s (X⊤X)−1, which is the comparison that matters below. The marginal distribution of β is a multivariate t.

Definition 7.6.2 (Multivariate t)

Let Z∼Np(0,S) and W∼χ2(ν) be independent, with S positive definite. The law of m+Z/W/ν is the multivariate t distribution tν(m,S).

For p=1 and S=1, this is the t(ν) distribution of Definition 4.1.3 shifted by m. For every c≠0, c⊤u with u∼tν(m,S) is c⊤m+c⊤ScT with T∼t(ν), because c⊤Z/c⊤Sc∼N(0,1) is independent of W.

Lemma 7.6.1 (Marginals of the normal-inverse-gamma)

If (β,σ2)∼NIG(m,V,a,b), then 2b/σ2∼χ2(2a), E(σ2)=b/(a−1) for a>1, and β∼t2a(m, (b/a)V). In particular, for every λ≠0, (λ⊤β−λ⊤m)/(b/a)λ⊤Vλ∼t(2a).

Proof. Put W=2b/σ2. Changing variables v=2b/w in the inverse gamma density, with |dv/dw|=2b/w2, gives the density of W: baΓ(a)(2bw)−a−1e−w/22bw2=wa−1e−w/22aΓ(a),w>0, which is the χ2(2a) density (Eq. 4.1.1). Then Eσ2=2bE(1/W)=2b/(2a−2), since E(1/W)=1/(ν−2) for W∼χ2(ν), ν>2. Next let Z0=(β−m)/σ. Given σ2, Z0∼Np(0,V), a law that does not depend on σ2. So Z0 is independent of σ2, and hence of W, and β=m+σZ0=m+2bWZ0=m+b/aZ0W/(2a). Since b/aZ0∼Np(0,(b/a)V), this is t2a(m,(b/a)V) by Definition 7.6.2. The last statement is the remark after Definition 7.6.2.

7.6.2. The conjugate update

Theorem 7.6.2 (Conjugate posterior)

Let y follow the normal linear model Eq. 7.3.1, with X of any rank, and let the prior be (β,σ2)∼NIG(m0,V0,a0,b0). Then the posterior is NIG(mn,Vn,an,bn), where

Vn=(V0−1+X⊤X)−1,mn=Vn(V0−1m0+X⊤y),an=a0+n2,
(7.6.2)
bn=b0+12(y⊤y+m0⊤V0−1m0−mn⊤Vn−1mn)=b0+12{‖y−Xmn‖2+(mn−m0)⊤V0−1(mn−m0)}.
(7.6.3)

Consequently β∣y∼t2an(mn,(bn/an)Vn), σ2∣y∼IG(an,bn), and E(β∣y)=mn.

Proof. Write P0=V0−1 and Pn=P0+X⊤X. The matrix Pn is positive definite, because P0 is and X⊤X is nonnegative definite. This holds whatever the rank of X. Expanding both sides and using Pnmn=P0m0+X⊤y gives the completion of the square ‖y−Xβ‖2+(β−m0)⊤P0(β−m0)=(β−mn)⊤Pn(β−mn)+C, with C=y⊤y+m0⊤P0m0−mn⊤Pnmn. Indeed, the left side is β⊤Pnβ−2β⊤(X⊤y+P0m0)+y⊤y+m0⊤P0m0, and (β−mn)⊤Pn(β−mn)=β⊤Pnβ−2β⊤Pnmn+mn⊤Pnmn. Putting β=mn in the identity shows that C=‖y−Xmn‖2+(mn−m0)⊤P0(mn−m0)≥0, which is the second form of Eq. 7.6.3.

By Bayes’ theorem the posterior density is proportional, as a function of (β,σ2), to prior times likelihood. Using Eq. 7.6.1 and dropping factors free of the parameters, π(β,σ2∣y)∝(σ2)−(a0+p/2)−1e−{2b0+(β−m0)⊤P0(β−m0)}/(2σ2)×(σ2)−n/2e−‖y−Xβ‖2/(2σ2). By the completion of the square, the exponent is −{2b0+C+(β−mn)⊤Pn(β−mn)}/(2σ2), and 2b0+C=2bn. The power of σ2 is −(a0+n/2+p/2)−1=−(an+p/2)−1. So the posterior is proportional to the NIG(mn,Vn,an,bn) density Eq. 7.6.1. Two densities that are proportional are equal, since both integrate to one. The marginals follow from Lemma 7.6.1. The posterior mean of β is mn: by the tower property, E(β∣y)=E{E(β∣σ2,y)∣y}=mn.

The update has a simple structure. Precisions add: the posterior precision (per unit σ2) is the prior precision V0−1 plus the data precision X⊤X. Shape parameters count observations: each observation adds 1/2 to a. Scale parameters accumulate squared discrepancies: bn adds half of the residual sum of squares at the posterior mean and half of the prior-weighted distance between the posterior and prior means. Because Pn is positive definite even when X is rank deficient, the posterior is proper for every design. The prior supplies information about the directions of N(X) that the data cannot, and there the posterior equals the prior (Exercise (B2)).

7.6.3. The posterior mean as shrinkage

Proposition 7.6.3 (Posterior mean as a combination)

In Theorem 7.6.2, let X have full column rank and put W=VnV0−1. Then:

  • (a) mn=Wm0+(I−W)β^, with I−W=VnX⊤X. Equivalently Vn−1mn=V0−1m0+(X⊤X)β^: the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the least squares estimate.

  • (b) bn=b0+12{SSE+(β^−m0)⊤(V0+(X⊤X)−1)−1(β^−m0)}.

  • (c) (g-prior.) If V0=g(X⊤X)−1 with g>0, then W=I/(1+g) and mn=(m0+gβ^)/(1+g).

  • (d) (Ridge.) If m0=0 and V0=k−1I with k>0, then mn=(X⊤X+kI)−1X⊤y.

Proof.

  • (a) X⊤y=X⊤Xβ^ by the normal equations (Theorem 6.4.2), so mn=VnV0−1m0+VnX⊤Xβ^, and VnV0−1+VnX⊤X=VnVn−1=I.
  • (b) Write P0=V0−1 and K=X⊤X. The completion of the square in the proof of Theorem 7.6.2 shows that 2(bn−b0) is the minimum over β of ‖y−Xβ‖2+(β−m0)⊤P0(β−m0), because the remaining term (β−mn)⊤Pn(β−mn) is nonnegative and vanishes at mn. By Eq. 6.3.1, ‖y−Xβ‖2=SSE+(β−β^)⊤K(β−β^). Put d=β^−m0 and β=m0+u. The quantity to minimize becomes SSE+(u−d)⊤K(u−d)+u⊤P0u, whose minimum over u, attained at u=(K+P0)−1Kd, is SSE+d⊤{K−K(K+P0)−1K}d. By Theorem 1.6.5, K−K(K+P0)−1K=(K−1+P0−1)−1=(V0+(X⊤X)−1)−1.
  • (c) With V0−1=X⊤X/g, Vn−1=(1+1/g)X⊤X, so W=VnV0−1={g/(1+g)}(X⊤X)−1X⊤X/g=I/(1+g). (d) Substitute into Eq. 7.6.2.

Part (a) is the Bayesian counterpart of Gauss–Markov. The posterior mean is linear in y, and it is biased unless m0=β: E(mn)=Wm0+(I−W)β. So the Gauss–Markov theorem does not apply to it, and it can have smaller mean squared error than least squares when the prior mean is close to the truth. Part (d) identifies ridge regression, whose mean squared error was studied in Exercise (B1), as a posterior mean. Chapter 27 develops the connection. When the prior is weak relative to the data, W is close to 0 and the posterior mean is close to β^. When it is strong, W is close to I and the data barely move it.

In general W is a full matrix, not a multiple of the identity. Shrinkage then happens along the eigenvectors of the problem, not coordinate by coordinate. A single coefficient’s posterior mean need not lie between its prior mean and its least squares estimate, as the next example shows. Only the g-prior of part (c) shrinks every coordinate by the same factor. Its prior covariance copies the shape of the sampling covariance of β^, and that makes it convenient for model comparison. Zellner (1986) introduced it.

7.6.4. The flat-prior limit

What happens when the prior carries no information? Letting V0−1→0 in Eq. 7.6.2 gives mn→β^ when X has full rank. The shape parameter needs more care. The NIG density Eq. 7.6.1 carries the factor (σ2)−(a0+p/2)−1, so letting only a0,b0→0 leads to the prior (σ2)−p/2−1, with an=n/2 and t marginals on n degrees of freedom rather than n−p. The improper prior π(β,σ2)∝1/σ2, uniform in β and in log⁡σ, is the limit V0−1→0, a0→−p/2, b0→0. A negative shape a0=−p/2 lies outside the proper NIG family, so this prior cannot be reached inside Theorem 7.6.2 and has to be handled directly. The result is clean.

Corollary 7.6.4 (The flat prior reproduces least squares)

Let rank(X)=p<n, let SSE>0, and take the improper prior π(β,σ2)∝1/σ2. Then the posterior is proper and equals NIG(β^,(X⊤X)−1,(n−p)/2,SSE/2). In particular, for every λ≠0, λ⊤β−λ⊤β^sλ⊤(X⊤X)−1λ | y ∼ t(n−p), so the 1−α equal-tailed credible interval for λ⊤β is the t interval Eq. 7.5.1.

Proof. By Eq. 6.3.1, ‖y−Xβ‖2=SSE+(β−β^)⊤X⊤X(β−β^). So prior times likelihood is proportional to (σ2)−1−n/2exp⁡{−SSE+(β−β^)⊤X⊤X(β−β^)2σ2}=(σ2)−(a+p/2)−1exp⁡{−2b+(β−β^)⊤X⊤X(β−β^)2σ2}, with a=(n−p)/2>0 and b=SSE/2>0. This is proportional to the density Eq. 7.6.1 of NIG(β^,(X⊤X)−1,a,b), which is integrable. So the posterior is proper and equal to it. By Lemma 7.6.1 with 2a=n−p and b/a=SSE/(n−p)=s2, the stated ratio has the t(n−p) distribution.

The frequentist and Bayesian statements now read alike, but they say different things. The t interval of Section 7.5 has probability 1−α of covering the fixed λ⊤β before the data are seen. The credible interval has posterior probability 1−α of containing the random λ⊤β given the observed data. That the two coincide is a special property of this model and this prior. It is one reason why 1/σ2 is regarded as the natural “noninformative” prior for the normal linear model.

7.6.5. A worked example

Example (A prior for the stack loss plant)

Return to the stack loss regression of Example (Stack loss). Suppose an engineer, before seeing these 21 days, believed from the chemistry of the process that stack loss should rise roughly one for one with air flow and with cooling water temperature, and should not depend on acid concentration. This belief is hypothetical and is used only for illustration. We encode it as m0=(0, 1, 1, 0)⊤,V0=diag(1002, 0.12, 0.22, 0.12),a0=3, b0=18. So the prior standard deviations of the slopes are 0.1σ, 0.2σ and 0.1σ, the intercept is left essentially free, and the prior mean of σ2 is b0/(a0−1)=9.

The posterior has an=13.5, so the marginal posteriors are t with 27 degrees of freedom, and bn=112.523. The posterior means of the coefficients are mn=(−41.1490, 0.7616, 1.1606, −0.1371)⊤, against the least squares estimates (−39.9197, 0.7156, 1.2953, −0.1521). Each slope has moved toward its prior mean. The intercept has moved too, though its prior is vague, because it is strongly correlated with the slopes when the regressors are not centred. The 95% credible interval for the air-flow coefficient, using the t(27) quantile 2.052, is (0.550, 0.973), narrower than the least squares t interval (0.431, 1.000), because the prior adds information. For water temperature and acid concentration the intervals are (0.594, 1.727) and (−0.392, 0.118). The posterior mean of σ2 is 9.002, almost exactly the prior mean 9, although s2=10.519. The two parts of Eq. 7.6.3 explain why. The lack of fit at the posterior mean is ‖y−Xmn‖2=180.667, and on its own, with b0, it would give (b0+180.667/2)/(an−1)=8.667: the shape an=a0+n/2 counts all n observations, while the residuals have spent some of them on β. The prior–data conflict term (mn−m0)⊤V0−1(mn−m0)=8.378 adds to the scale and makes up the difference, so the data barely move σ2 from where the prior put it.

Figure 7.6.1(a) shows the three marginal densities for the air-flow coefficient. Panel (b) traces the posterior means of the slopes as the prior precision of the slopes is multiplied by a factor τ, from 10−3 (essentially least squares) to 103 (essentially the prior mean). The water-temperature coefficient does not move monotonically. It overshoots its prior mean of 1, down to 0.946, before returning. Matrix shrinkage acts on combinations of coefficients, and a single coordinate can pass beyond its prior mean on the way.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats

data = sm.datasets.stackloss.load_pandas().data
y = data["STACKLOSS"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), data[["AIRFLOW", "WATERTEMP", "ACIDCONC"]]])
n, p = X.shape
XtX, Xty = X.T @ X, X.T @ y
beta_hat = np.linalg.solve(XtX, Xty)
sse = np.sum((y - X @ beta_hat) ** 2)
def nig_posterior(m0, V0, a0, b0):
    """Conjugate update of the normal-inverse-gamma prior NIG(m0, V0, a0, b0)."""
    P0 = np.linalg.inv(V0)                         # prior precision (per unit sigma^2)
    Vn = np.linalg.inv(P0 + XtX)
    mn = Vn @ (P0 @ m0 + Xty)
    an = a0 + n / 2
    bn = b0 + (y @ y + m0 @ P0 @ m0 - mn @ (P0 + XtX) @ mn) / 2
    return mn, Vn, an, bn

# prior: vague intercept; slopes centred on 1, 1, 0 with sd 0.1, 0.2, 0.1 in units of sigma
m0 = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 1.0, 0.0])
V0 = np.diag([100.0**2, 0.1**2, 0.2**2, 0.1**2])
a0, b0 = 3.0, 18.0                                 # prior mean of sigma^2 is b0/(a0-1) = 9
mn, Vn, an, bn = nig_posterior(m0, V0, a0, b0)
scale = np.sqrt(np.diag(Vn) * bn / an)             # marginal posterior: t with 2 a_n d.f.
q = stats.t.ppf(0.975, 2 * an)
for j, name in enumerate(["intercept", "air flow", "water temp", "acid conc"]):
    print(f"{name:10s} LS {beta_hat[j]:8.4f}   posterior mean {mn[j]:8.4f}   "
          f"95% interval ({mn[j] - q * scale[j]:.3f}, {mn[j] + q * scale[j]:.3f})")
print(f"posterior mean of sigma^2 = {bn / (an - 1):.3f}")

The script behind this example also checks the theorem numerically. The log posterior computed from Eq. 7.6.2 differs from log prior plus log-likelihood by the same constant at 50 random parameter values. The two forms of bn agree. A Monte Carlo sample drawn from σ2 and then β∣σ2 reproduces the marginal t quantiles. And a nearly flat prior reproduces least squares and the t interval.

Remark (How the Bayesian answer relates to the optimality results). Seen from the frequentist side, the posterior mean is a linear estimator with bias W(m0−β) and covariance σ2(I−W)(X⊤X)−1(I−W)⊤, which is smaller than that of β^ in the nonnegative definite order. Whether its mean squared error is smaller depends on how far m0 is from β, in the metric that W defines. Averaged over the prior, the posterior mean is the estimator with the smallest expected squared error, because a conditional mean minimizes mean squared error. Different criteria single out different estimators. The Gauss–Markov theorem, the Lehmann–Scheffé theorem and the conjugate posterior (Theorem 7.6.2) answer three different questions, and in the flat-prior limit their answers agree.

7.6.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Show that the IG(a,b) distribution has mean b/(a−1) for a>1 and mode b/(a+1). What are the mean and mode of σ2∣y under the flat prior of Corollary 7.6.4, and how do they compare with s2 and with the MLE of Theorem 7.3.1?

Exercise (A2)

Suppose σ2 is known and the prior is β∼Np(m0,σ2V0). Show that the posterior is Np(mn,σ2Vn) with mn and Vn as in Eq. 7.6.2.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Under the g-prior of Proposition 7.6.3(c) with m0=0, show that bn=b0+12{SSE+β^⊤X⊤Xβ^/(1+g)}. Show that as g→∞ the posterior mean tends to β^, and that for finite g the frequentist mean squared error of mn is {g2σ2tr(X⊤X)−1+‖β‖2}/(1+g)2.

Solution. By Proposition 7.6.3(b), with V0+(X⊤X)−1=(1+g)(X⊤X)−1, the quadratic term is β^⊤X⊤Xβ^/(1+g). As g→∞, mn=gβ^/(1+g)→β^. For the mean squared error, mn−β={g(β^−β)−β}/(1+g), and β^−β has mean zero and covariance σ2(X⊤X)−1. So the expected squared norm is {g2σ2tr(X⊤X)−1+‖β‖2}/(1+g)2. It is smaller than σ2tr(X⊤X)−1, the value for β^, iff ‖β‖2<(1+2g)σ2tr(X⊤X)−1.

Exercise (B2)

Let X be rank deficient, m0=0 and V0=τ−1I. For v∈N(X), show that v⊤mn=0 and v⊤Vnv=‖v‖2/τ. So the conditional posterior of v⊤β given σ2 equals its prior: the data do not update nonidentified directions. What happens to λ⊤mn for λ∈C(X⊤) as τ→0?

Solution. Vn−1v=(τI+X⊤X)v=τv, so Vnv=v/τ. Then v⊤mn=v⊤VnX⊤y=(Vnv)⊤X⊤y=(Xv)⊤y/τ=0, and v⊤Vnv=‖v‖2/τ. These are the prior mean and variance (per unit σ2) of v⊤β. For the second question, mn=(X⊤X+τI)−1X⊤y converges as τ→0 to the minimum-norm least squares estimate X+y (Proposition 6.10.1). This follows from the spectral decomposition of X⊤X: eigenvectors with eigenvalue d>0 get the factor 1/(d+τ)→1/d, and those with eigenvalue 0 get zero. Hence λ⊤mn→λ⊤X+y=λ⊤β^, the least squares estimate of the estimable function.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

Under the posterior of Theorem 7.6.2, let Y0=x0⊤β+ε0 be a future observation, with ε0∼N(0,σ2) independent of everything given σ2. Show that the posterior predictive distribution of Y0 is t2an(x0⊤mn, (bn/an)(1+x0⊤Vnx0)). Compare it with the frequentist prediction interval of Exercise (B2) under the flat prior.

Solution. Given σ2 and y, x0⊤β∼N(x0⊤mn,σ2x0⊤Vnx0), and ε0 is independent of it. So Y0∣σ2,y∼N(x0⊤mn,σ2(1+x0⊤Vnx0)). Thus (Y0,σ2)∣y is normal-inverse-gamma with p=1, mean x0⊤mn and V=1+x0⊤Vnx0, and Lemma 7.6.1 gives the t2an law. Under the flat prior, mn=β^, Vn=(X⊤X)−1, 2an=n−p and bn/an=s2. The equal-tailed predictive interval is then exactly the prediction interval of Exercise (B2).

Exercise (C2)

Split the data into two batches (y1,X1) and (y2,X2). Show that updating the prior with the first batch, and then using that posterior as the prior for the second batch, gives the same posterior as a single update with all the data. Which quantities in Eq. 7.6.2 make this obvious?

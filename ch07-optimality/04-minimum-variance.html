7.4  Sufficiency, completeness and minimum variance

The Gauss–Markov theorem compares least squares only with linear unbiased estimators, and Example (Uniform errors and the midrange) showed that the restriction matters: with uniform errors a nonlinear estimator wins easily. This section shows that under normality the restriction can be dropped. In the normal linear model, 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ has the smallest variance among all unbiased estimators of an estimable 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β, and s2 has the smallest variance among all unbiased estimators of σ2. The argument is the classical one through sufficiency and completeness. It also produces best unbiased estimators of nonlinear functions, such as σ itself. At the end we compare the variances with the Cramér–Rao lower bound, which least squares attains for 𝛃β and s2 does not attain for σ2.

Two facts from mathematical statistics are quoted without proof: the factorization criterion for sufficiency and the completeness of full-rank exponential families. Both are proved in standard texts such as Lehmann and Casella (1998) and Lehmann and Romano (2005). Everything built on them is proved here.

7.4.1. Sufficiency

Definition 7.4.1 (Sufficient and complete statistics)

Let Y have a distribution 𝛉Pθ from a family indexed by 𝛉θ∈Θ, and let T=T(Y) be a statistic.

  • (a) T is sufficient for 𝛉θ if the conditional law of Y given T is the same for every 𝛉θ.

  • (b) T is complete if, for every function h with 𝛉Eθ|h(T)|<∞ and 𝛉Eθh(T)=0 for all 𝛉θ∈Θ, we have 𝛉Prθ{h(T)=0}=1 for all 𝛉θ.

A sufficient statistic carries all the information about 𝛉θ in the data. Once T is known, the rest of Y is noise whose distribution does not involve the parameter. Completeness says that T carries no redundant information either: no nontrivial function of T has expectation zero for every parameter value. Equivalently, an unbiased estimator that is a function of T is unique.

Lemma 7.4.1 (Factorization criterion)

Suppose Y has a density 𝛉fθ(y) on Rn. Then T(Y) is sufficient iff there are functions 𝛉gθ and h, with h not depending on 𝛉θ, such that 𝛉𝛉fθ(y)=gθ(T(y))h(y) for all y and 𝛉θ.

Lemma 7.4.2 (Completeness in exponential families)

Suppose Y has a density of the form 𝛈𝛈𝛈𝛈fη(y)=c(η)h(y)exp⁡{η⊤T(y)},η∈E⊆Rk, with T(y)∈Rk. If E contains a nonempty open subset of Rk, then T(Y) is complete and sufficient.

Sufficiency follows from Lemma 7.4.1 with 𝛈𝛈𝛈gη(T)=c(η)eη⊤T. Completeness rests on the uniqueness of Laplace transforms: 𝛈Eηh(T)=0 on an open set of 𝛈η forces a certain two-sided Laplace transform to vanish on an open set, and hence h(T)=0 almost surely.

For the normal linear model, expand the exponent of the density: 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃‖y−Xβ‖2=y⊤y−2β⊤X⊤y+β⊤X⊤Xβ. So the density of Eq. 7.3.1 is

𝛃𝛃𝛃f(y)=(2πσ2)−n/2exp⁡{−β⊤X⊤Xβ2σ2}exp⁡{β⊤X⊤yσ2−y⊤y2σ2},
(7.4.1)

and the data enter only through X⊤y and y⊤y.

Proposition 7.4.3 (A complete sufficient statistic)

In the normal linear model Eq. 7.3.1, let Q be an n×r matrix whose columns are an orthonormal basis of C(X). Then T=(Q⊤Y, Y⊤Y) is complete and sufficient for 𝛃(β,σ2). The statistics (X⊤Y,Y⊤Y) and (MY,SSE) are one-to-one functions of T, so they are complete and sufficient too.

Proof. Since 𝛃Xβ∈C(X), 𝛃𝛄Xβ=Qγ with 𝛄𝛃γ=Q⊤Xβ∈Rr, and 𝛃𝛄β⊤X⊤y=γ⊤Q⊤y. By Eq. 7.4.1 the density has the form of Lemma 7.4.2 with k=r+1, 𝛈𝛄T(y)=(Q⊤y, y⊤y),η=(γ/σ2, −1/(2σ2)). As 𝛃β ranges over Rp, 𝛄𝛃γ=Q⊤Xβ ranges over all of Rr: the r×p matrix Q⊤X has rank r, because QQ⊤X=MX=X has rank r. Hence E=Rr×(−∞,0), which is open, and Lemma 7.4.2 applies. For the equivalent statistics: MY=QQ⊤Y (Proposition 6.3.2) and SSE=Y⊤Y−‖Q⊤Y‖2 are functions of T, and conversely Q⊤Y=Q⊤MY and Y⊤Y=SSE+‖MY‖2. Also X⊤Y=X⊤Q(Q⊤Y), and Q=XK for some K, since the columns of Q lie in C(X), so Q⊤Y=K⊤X⊤Y. A one-to-one function of a complete sufficient statistic is again complete and sufficient, since both definitions refer only to the information (the σ-field) the statistic generates.

The reduction is dramatic. An n-dimensional data vector is replaced by r+1 numbers: the fitted values (equivalently, the least squares estimates of the estimable functions) and the residual sum of squares. Under normality the residual vector, apart from its length, carries no information about the parameters. Its direction is uniformly distributed over the unit sphere of C(X)⊥ whatever 𝛃(β,σ2) is (Exercise (C1)). This is the precise sense in which the residuals are “just noise”, and it is also why they are useful for checking the model. Their pattern can reveal departures from normality precisely because, under normality, it has a known and parameter-free distribution.

7.4.2. Rao–Blackwell and Lehmann–Scheffé

Theorem 7.4.4 (Rao–Blackwell)

Let T be sufficient for 𝛉θ, and let δ(Y) be an unbiased estimator of 𝛉g(θ) with 𝛉Varθδ<∞ for all 𝛉θ. Then δ∗=E(δ∣T) is a statistic, it is unbiased for 𝛉g(θ), and 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉Varθ(δ)=Varθ(δ∗)+Eθ[(δ−δ∗)2]≥Varθ(δ∗) for every 𝛉θ, with equality iff δ=δ∗ with probability one.

Proof. The law of Y given T does not depend on 𝛉θ, so neither does E(δ(Y)∣T). It is therefore computable from the data, which makes it a statistic. By the law of total expectation (Proposition 2.5.4), 𝛉Eδ∗=Eδ=g(θ). By the law of total variance in the same result, Varδ=E{Var(δ∣T)}+Var(δ∗), and E{Var(δ∣T)}=E{E[(δ−δ∗)2∣T]}=E(δ−δ∗)2. This last term is zero iff δ=δ∗ almost surely.

Conditioning on a sufficient statistic averages out the part of an estimator that responds to parameter-free noise. It never hurts, and it helps unless the estimator was already a function of T.

Theorem 7.4.5 (Lehmann–Scheffé)

Let T be complete and sufficient for 𝛉θ. If ϕ(T) is unbiased for 𝛉g(θ) and has finite variance, then ϕ(T) is a uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator (UMVUE) of 𝛉g(θ): 𝛉𝛉Varθϕ(T)≤Varθδ for every unbiased δ and every 𝛉θ. It is the only unbiased function of T and, up to sets of probability zero, the only UMVUE.

Proof. If ϕ1(T) and ϕ2(T) are both unbiased, then 𝛉Eθ{ϕ1(T)−ϕ2(T)}=0 for all 𝛉θ, so ϕ1(T)=ϕ2(T) almost surely by completeness. Now let δ be any unbiased estimator with finite variance. By Theorem 7.4.4, E(δ∣T) is an unbiased function of T, so it equals ϕ(T) almost surely, and Varϕ(T)≤Varδ, with equality iff δ=ϕ(T) almost surely. (If δ has infinite variance there is nothing to prove.)

7.4.3. Best unbiased estimators in the normal linear model

Theorem 7.4.6 (Minimum variance unbiased estimation)

Assume the normal linear model Eq. 7.3.1 with n>r, and let s2=SSE/(n−r).

  • (a) For every 𝛌λ∈C(X⊤), 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^ is the UMVUE of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β.

  • (b) s2 is the UMVUE of σ2.

  • (c) More generally, every estimator of the form ϕ(MY,SSE) with finite variance is the UMVUE of its expectation. In particular, for k=n−r, bk=2kΓ(k+12)Γ(k2), s/bk is the UMVUE of σ, and 𝛃x0⊤β^+zs/bk is the UMVUE of 𝛃x0⊤β+zσ for every constant z and every x0∈C(X⊤).

Proof. By Proposition 7.4.3, (MY,SSE) is complete and sufficient, so by Theorem 7.4.5 it suffices to show that each estimator is an unbiased function of it with finite variance. (a) Write 𝛌𝛒λ=X⊤ρ. Then 𝛌𝛃𝛒λ⊤β^=ρ⊤MY is a function of MY, it is unbiased by Theorem 7.2.1(a), and its variance is finite. (b) s2 is a function of SSE, it is unbiased because E(SSE)=σ2(n−r) (Theorem 2.3.1), and it has finite variance. (c) The first statement is Theorem 7.4.5 again. For σ, note that SSE/σ2=Y⊤(I−M)Y/σ2∼χ2(k) by Theorem 4.3.2(a), since I−M is a projection of rank k and 𝛃(I−M)Xβ=0. If W∼χ2(k), with density wk/2−1e−w/2/{2k/2Γ(k/2)}, then EW=12k/2Γ(k/2)∫0∞w(k+1)/2−1e−w/2dw=2(k+1)/2Γ(k+12)2k/2Γ(k2)=2Γ(k+12)Γ(k2). Hence Es=σEW/k=bkσ, and s/bk is unbiased for σ, with variance at most Es2/bk2<∞. The last estimator is a sum of two unbiased functions of (MY,SSE).

Part (a) is the promised strengthening of Gauss–Markov. Under normality no unbiased estimator of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β, linear or not, beats least squares. The midrange of Example (Uniform errors and the midrange) cannot help when the errors are normal. Part (c) shows the method at work beyond linear estimators. The obvious estimate s of σ is biased, because Es<Es2 by Jensen’s inequality, and dividing by bk removes the bias.

Example (Estimating σ without bias)

The constant bk=E(s)/σ depends only on the residual degrees of freedom. For the stack loss regression, k=17 and b17=0.9854, so the UMVUE of σ is s/b17=1.0148s. The correction is 1.5% here. It is large for very small k, with b2=0.8862, and negligible for large k, with b200=0.9988.

import numpy as np
from scipy import special

def mean_s_factor(k):
    """b_k = E(s)/sigma when k s^2 / sigma^2 ~ chi^2(k)."""
    return np.sqrt(2 / k) * np.exp(special.gammaln((k + 1) / 2) - special.gammaln(k / 2))

for k in [2, 5, 10, 17, 50, 200]:
    b = mean_s_factor(k)
    print(f"k = {k:3d}   E(s)/sigma = {b:.4f}   UMVUE of sigma = s / {b:.4f}")

7.4.4. Rao–Blackwellization by reflection

The Rao–Blackwell theorem is not only a device for proofs. In the normal linear model we can carry out the conditioning explicitly, and it takes every linear unbiased estimator to least squares.

Example (Conditioning on the sufficient statistic)

Let a⊤Y be any LUE of an estimable 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β, such as the end-point slope of Example (Three linear unbiased estimators of a slope). Let R=2M−I, the reflection that fixes C(X) and reverses C(X)⊥. It is symmetric and orthogonal (R2=I) and RX=X. Consequently:

  • 𝛃𝛃RY∼Nn(RXβ,σ2RR⊤)=Nn(Xβ,σ2I) by Theorem 3.3.1, so RY has the same distribution as Y;
  • the sufficient statistic is unchanged: X⊤RY=X⊤Y and ‖RY‖=‖Y‖.

So the pairs (a⊤Y,T(Y)) and (a⊤RY,T(Y)) have the same joint distribution, where T=(X⊤Y,Y⊤Y). Hence E(a⊤Y∣T)=E(a⊤RY∣T), and averaging, 𝛌𝛃E(a⊤Y∣T)=E(a⊤Y+RY2|T)=E(a⊤MY∣T)=a⊤MY=λ⊤β^, because a⊤MY is a function of T and Ma=a∗ (Proposition 7.1.2). The Rao–Blackwell improvement of every LUE is the least squares estimate. The listing checks the identity 𝛌𝛃12(a⊤y+a⊤Ry)=λ⊤β^ for simulated data and the end-point slope.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 4.0, 5.0, 5.5, 7.0, 8.0, 9.0, 10.5, 12.0])
n = len(x)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x])
M = X @ np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T)
R = 2 * M - np.eye(n)                            # reflection: keeps C(X), flips C(X)-perp

rng = np.random.default_rng(71)
y = X @ np.array([2.0, 0.5]) + rng.normal(size=n)
y_ref = R @ y
print("X^T y unchanged:", np.allclose(X.T @ y_ref, X.T @ y))
print("y^T y unchanged:", np.isclose(y_ref @ y_ref, y @ y))

a_end = np.zeros(n)
a_end[[0, -1]] = [-1.0, 1.0]
a_end /= x[-1] - x[0]                            # the end-point slope estimator
a_ls = X @ np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, [0.0, 1.0])
print("average of a^T y over the two points:", (a_end @ y + a_end @ y_ref) / 2)
print("least squares slope:                 ", a_ls @ y)

The reflection argument uses the normal distribution only through its invariance under orthogonal maps that fix the mean. It makes concrete what “the residuals are noise” means. Reversing the residual vector changes neither the likelihood nor the sufficient statistic, so an estimator should not depend on the residuals’ direction. An LUE that uses the residuals is replaced by the average of its value at y and at the reflected data, and that average no longer involves the residuals.

7.4.5. The Cramér–Rao bound

A different route to optimality bounds the variance of every unbiased estimator from below and checks whether the bound is attained. We state the bound for the full-rank normal linear model, where every ingredient can be computed.

Write 𝛉𝛃θ=(β⊤,v)⊤ with v=σ2. The score is the gradient of the log-likelihood Eq. 7.3.2, evaluated at the random Y: 𝛃𝛆𝛆𝛆𝛆𝛃S=(∂ℓ/∂β∂ℓ/∂v)=(X⊤ε/v−n2v+ε⊤ε2v2),ε=Y−Xβ.

Proposition 7.4.7 (Score and information)

In Eq. 7.3.1 with rank(X)=p, ES=0 and 𝛉I(θ)=Cov(S)=(X⊤X/σ200⊤n/(2σ4)).

Proof. 𝛆ε∼Nn(0,vI). Hence 𝛆E(X⊤ε/v)=0 and 𝛆Cov(X⊤ε/v)=X⊤X/v by Theorem 2.2.1. Next, 𝛆𝛆ε⊤ε/v∼χ2(n) by Theorem 4.3.2(a) with P=I, so it has mean n and variance 2n. So the last coordinate has mean −n/(2v)+nv/(2v2)=0 and variance 2n/(4v2)=n/(2v2). Finally, by Theorem 4.2.3(c) with K=X⊤, A=I and 𝛍μ=0, 𝛆𝛆𝛆Cov(X⊤ε,ε⊤ε)=0.

Theorem 7.4.8 (Cramér–Rao bound in the normal linear model)

Assume Eq. 7.3.1 with rank(X)=p. Let δ(Y) have finite variance and satisfy 𝛉𝛉Eθδ=g(θ) for all 𝛉θ, with g differentiable. Then 𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛉𝛃𝛃Varθ(δ)≥∇g(θ)⊤I(θ)−1∇g(θ)=σ2gβ⊤(X⊤X)−1gβ+2σ4ngv2, where 𝛃𝛃gβ=∂g/∂β and gv=∂g/∂v.

Proof. Write 𝛉fθ for the density. Differentiating 𝛉𝛉g(θ)=∫δ(y)fθ(y)dy under the integral sign, which is justified because the normal family is an exponential family (Lehmann and Casella 1998, chapter 1), gives 𝛉𝛉𝛉∇g(θ)=∫δ(y)∇log⁡fθ(y)fθ(y)dy=E(δS)=Cov(δ,S), the last step because ES=0. For any constant vector c, 0≤Var(δ−c⊤S)=Varδ−2c⊤∇g+c⊤Ic. Choosing c=I−1∇g gives Varδ≥∇g⊤I−1∇g. The block-diagonal form of Proposition 7.4.7 gives the right-hand side.

For 𝛉𝛌𝛃g(θ)=λ⊤β the bound is 𝛌𝛌σ2λ⊤(X⊤X)−1λ, exactly the variance of 𝛌𝛃λ⊤β^. Least squares attains the bound, which gives a second proof of Theorem 7.4.6(a) in full rank. For g=σ2 the bound is 2σ4/n, while Var(s2)=σ4(n−p)2Var{χ2(n−p)}=2σ4n−p>2σ4n. So s2 does not attain the bound, although by Theorem 7.4.6(b) no unbiased estimator does better. The bound is simply not sharp here. Its ratio to the attained minimum, the efficiency of s2, is (n−p)/n. That is 0.810 for the stack loss regression, and it tends to one as n→∞ with p fixed. The same happens for σ: the UMVUE s/bk has variance 0.0298σ2 when k=17, against the bound σ2/(2n)=0.0238σ2, an efficiency of 0.798. The equality condition in the proof explains why. The bound is attained iff Var(δ−c⊤S)=0, that is, iff 𝛉δ=g(θ)+∇g⊤I−1S with probability one. For g=σ2 the right-hand side is 𝛆𝛆𝛃σ2+(2σ4/n){−n/(2σ2)+ε⊤ε/(2σ4)}=‖Y−Xβ‖2/n. This is not a statistic, because it involves the unknown 𝛃β, so no unbiased estimator of σ2 attains the bound.

Warning. The Cramér–Rao bound is a lower bound, not a target. An estimator that fails to attain it may still be the best unbiased estimator, as s2 is. When an estimator does attain it, the bound certifies optimality without any appeal to completeness. The Lehmann–Scheffé route is stronger in one respect: it needs no differentiability and covers every estimable function in every rank.

7.4.6. What changes without normality

Every result of this section uses the normal density. Without it the conclusions can fail. Under uniform errors the midrange beats Y¯ (Example (Uniform errors and the midrange)). Under Laplace errors the sample median has asymptotic variance σ2/(2n), half that of Y¯. Under the second-moment assumptions alone, the best that can be said for s2 is a result in the spirit of Gauss–Markov. Among quadratic unbiased estimators Y⊤AY of σ2 with A nonnegative definite, s2 has the smallest variance when the errors have zero excess kurtosis, or when all leverages are equal. Exercise (C2) outlines this result of Atiqullah (1962).

Exercise (C1)

In the normal linear model with n−r≥2, let U=(I−M)Y/‖(I−M)Y‖. Show that U is independent of (MY,SSE), and that its distribution (uniform on the unit sphere of C(X)⊥) does not depend on 𝛃(β,σ2). Hint: write (I−M)Y=Q2Z as in Exercise (C2), and use the rotation invariance of N(0,σ2I).

Solution. With Q2 as in Exercise (C2), (I−M)Y=Q2Z with Z∼Nn−r(0,σ2I), and Z is independent of MY by Theorem 3.4.1, since Cov(MY,Z)=σ2MQ2=0. Then SSE=‖Z‖2 and U=Q2Z/‖Z‖. For any orthogonal H, HZ has the same law as Z, so the law of Z/‖Z‖ is invariant under all rotations. The only such law on the sphere is the uniform one. The independence of the direction Z/‖Z‖ and the length ‖Z‖ follows from the polar form of the density of Z, which is a function of ‖z‖ only. Hence U is independent of (MY,SSE), and its law involves neither 𝛃β nor σ2.

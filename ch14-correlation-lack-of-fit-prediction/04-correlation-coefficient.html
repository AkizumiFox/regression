14.4  The correlation coefficient and Fisher’s z

With a single regressor, the multiple correlation reduces to the ordinary correlation ρ of the pair (X,Y), and R2 to the square of the sample correlation r=∑i(Xi−X¯)(Yi−Y¯)∑i(Xi−X¯)2∑i(Yi−Y¯)2. Keeping the sign, this section develops two routes to inference about ρ: the exact distribution of r, and Fisher’s transformation to a nearly normal variable whose variance does not depend on ρ. Throughout, (Xi,Yi), i=1,…,n, are independent draws from a bivariate normal distribution with correlation |ρ|<1, and n≥3.

14.4.1. The exact distribution of r

Proposition 14.4.1 (Exact law of the sample correlation)

Let T=rn−2/1−r2 and δ=ρ/1−ρ2.

  • (a) Given the X values, T∼t(n−2,δV), where V=∑i(Xi−X¯)2/σX2.

  • (b) V∼χ2(n−1), and for −1<c<1,

    Pr(r≤c)=E[Pr{t(n−2,δV)≤cn−2/1−c2}].
    (14.4.1)

  • (c) If ρ=0, then T∼t(n−2) and r2∼Beta(12,n−22), independently of the X values.

  • (d) The law of r is stochastically increasing in ρ: for each c∈(−1,1), Pr(r≤c) is a strictly decreasing function of ρ.

Proof.

  • (a) Let β^1 be the least squares slope of Y on X and t1 its t statistic for the value zero. By Theorem 9.5.4(c) (see also Exercise (B3)), with only the intercept as the other column, r2=t12/(t12+n−2), and t1 has the sign of β^1, which is the sign of r. Solving, t1=T. Given the X values the pairs follow a normal simple regression with slope β1=ρσY/σX and error variance σ2=σY2(1−ρ2) (Eq. 3.5.2), so by Corollary 7.5.2 with d=0, T is noncentral t with n−2 degrees of freedom and noncentrality β1Sxx/σ=ρSxx/(σX1−ρ2)=δV.

  • (b) V∼χ2(n−1) by Corollary 4.4.5. Since T is an increasing function of r, r≤c iff T≤cn−2/1−c2, and Eq. 14.4.1 is the expectation of the conditional probability.

  • (c) With δ=0 the conditional law is t(n−2) for every design; apply Lemma 14.1.1. Then r2=T2/(T2+n−2) is the k=1 case of Theorem 14.3.2(b).

  • (d) Write a t(ν,θ) variable as (Z+θ)/W/ν (Definition 4.1.3). For θ1<θ2 and any u, Pr{t(ν,θ1)≤u}−Pr{t(ν,θ2)≤u}=Pr{uW/ν−θ2<Z≤uW/ν−θ1}>0. Since δ=ρ/1−ρ2 is strictly increasing in ρ, for every c and every V>0 the conditional probability in Eq. 14.4.1 strictly decreases in ρ, and so does its expectation.

Fisher (1915) found the density of r as an infinite series; for computation the mixture Eq. 14.4.1, an average of noncentral t probabilities over quantiles of V, is enough. Part (c) is the familiar test of ρ=0, the t test of a zero slope, symmetric in the two variables. Part (d) makes exact intervals possible, by the inversion used for ρ2 in Section 14.3.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import optimize, stats

def r_cdf(c, rho, n, m=4000):
    """Exact P(r <= c) for n iid bivariate normal pairs with correlation rho."""
    if c < 0:                                                # law of -r is that for -rho
        return 1 - r_cdf(-c, -rho, n, m)
    t = c * np.sqrt(n - 2) / np.sqrt(1 - c ** 2)            # increasing in c
    if rho == 0:
        return stats.t.cdf(t, n - 2)
    V = stats.chi2.ppf((np.arange(m) + 0.5) / m, n - 1)     # equal-probability nodes
    delta = rho / np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2)
    return np.mean(stats.nct.cdf(t, n - 2, delta * np.sqrt(V)))

When ρ≠0 the distribution of r is skewed towards zero, because r cannot pass ±1. For ρ=0.8 and n=15, 200000 simulated samples give a mean of 0.7886, a median of 0.811, a skewness of −1.40 and a variance of 0.01195 (panel (a) of Figure 14.4.1). The variance depends strongly on ρ: the standard deviation of r is 0.267 at ρ=0 and 0.032 at ρ=0.95 (panel (c)). An interval r±constant×standard error would inherit both problems.

14.4.2. Fisher’s transformation

Fisher (1921) proposed to work instead with

z=tanh−1⁡r=12log⁡1+r1−r,ζ=tanh−1⁡ρ.
(14.4.2)

To see why this helps we need the large-sample behaviour of r, and for that three standard tools from probability theory, used here without proof (see van der Vaart 1998, chapters 2 and 3).

Remark (Three limit theorems).

  • (i) Central limit theorem. If W1,W2,… are independent copies of a random vector with mean μ and covariance matrix Γ, then n(W¯n−μ)→N(0,Γ) in distribution.
  • (ii) Slutsky’s lemma. If Un→U in distribution and Vn→c in probability, then Un+Vn→U+c, and VnUn→cU for scalars. (iii) Delta method. If n(Tn−θ)→N(0,Γ) and g is differentiable at θ with gradient ∇g, then n(g(Tn)−g(θ))→N(0,∇g⊤Γ∇g).

The limiting variance of r needs fourth moments of the bivariate normal distribution.

Lemma 14.4.2 (Fourth moments of a standard bivariate normal pair)

Let (X,Y) be bivariate normal with means 0, variances 1 and correlation ρ. Then EX4=3, EX3Y=3ρ and EX2Y2=1+2ρ2. Consequently the covariance matrix of (X2,Y2,XY) is Γ=(22ρ22ρ2ρ222ρ2ρ2ρ1+ρ2).

Proof. Write Y=ρX+1−ρ2U with U∼N(0,1) independent of X; the pair has the right means, variances and covariance, and it is normal by Theorem 3.3.1. Using EX2=1, EX4=3 (the fourth moment of a standard normal) and the vanishing of odd moments, EX3Y=ρEX4=3ρ and EX2Y2=ρ2EX4+(1−ρ2)EX2EU2=3ρ2+1−ρ2=1+2ρ2. The covariances follow by subtracting products of means, EX2=EY2=1 and EXY=ρ: for instance Var(XY)=1+2ρ2−ρ2 and Cov(X2,XY)=EX3Y−ρ=2ρ.

Theorem 14.4.3 (Fisher’s z transformation)

As n→∞, n(r−ρ)→N(0,(1−ρ2)2)andn(z−ζ)→N(0,1) in distribution. The same limits hold with n−3 in place of n. The limiting variance of z does not depend on ρ: tanh−1 is a variance-stabilizing transformation for r.

Proof. The correlation is unchanged by the maps X↦a+bX, Y↦c+dY with b,d>0, so we may assume EX=EY=0 and VarX=VarY=1. Let W¯n be the average of Wi=(Xi2,Yi2,XiYi), whose mean is θ=(1,1,ρ) and whose covariance is Γ of Lemma 14.4.2. With divisor n, the sample variances and covariance are W¯n−(X¯2,Y¯2,X¯Y¯). Since nX¯ converges in distribution and X¯→0 in probability, nX¯2→0 in probability, and similarly for the other two products, so by Slutsky’s lemma the centring does not affect the limit. The divisor cancels in r=g(sample moments), where g(a,b,c)=c/ab, and ∇g(θ)=(−ρ/2,−ρ/2,1). By the central limit theorem and the delta method, n(r−ρ) is asymptotically normal with variance ∇g⊤Γ∇g=ρ24(2+2+4ρ2)−2⋅ρ2(2ρ+2ρ)+(1+ρ2)=ρ2+ρ4−4ρ2+1+ρ2=(1−ρ2)2. The derivative of tanh−1 at ρ is 1/(1−ρ2), so a second application of the delta method gives variance 1 for z. Finally n−3/n→1, and Slutsky’s lemma allows the substitution.

The transformation solves g′(ρ)=1/(1−ρ2), the condition for a limiting standard deviation of one (Exercise (B1)). Why the approximation is so good for small n is another matter: Fisher (1921) showed that the mean of z exceeds ζ by about ρ/(2(n−1)) and that its variance is close to 1/(n−3), and the distribution of z is nearly symmetric even when that of r is strongly skewed. In the simulation with ρ=0.8 and n=15, z has mean 1.1295 against ζ=1.0986 and ζ+ρ/(2(n−1))=1.1272, variance 0.0811 against 1/(n−3)=0.0833, and skewness 0.01. Its standard deviation is 0.288 at ρ=0 and 0.283 at ρ=0.95 (panel (c)).

14.4.3. Intervals and tests for a correlation

The approximation z≈N(ζ,1/(n−3)) gives the interval

tanh⁡(z−zα/2n−3) ≤ ρ ≤ tanh⁡(z+zα/2n−3),
(14.4.3)

with zα/2 the upper α/2 point of N(0,1). It is asymmetric about r, as it should be, and it never leaves (−1,1). The exact interval inverts Eq. 14.4.1, using part (d) of Proposition 14.4.1 exactly as for ρ2: its limits are the values of ρ at which the observed r is the 97.5% and the 2.5% point.

How good is the approximation? For n=10 pairs, the coverage of the 95% interval Eq. 14.4.3 over 200000 samples is 0.949, 0.949 and 0.952 at ρ=0, 0.5 and 0.9. The interval r±1.96(1−r2)/n, which uses the asymptotic standard error of Theorem 14.4.3 directly, covers only 0.839, 0.838 and 0.834.

def sample_r(rho, n, reps, rng):
    x = rng.normal(size=(reps, n))
    y = rho * x + np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2) * rng.normal(size=(reps, n))
    xc = x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    yc = y - y.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return np.sum(xc * yc, axis=1) / np.sqrt(np.sum(xc ** 2, axis=1) * np.sum(yc ** 2, axis=1))

rng = np.random.default_rng(1443)
n_c, reps_c, zq = 10, 200_000, stats.norm.ppf(0.975)
for rho_c in (0.0, 0.5, 0.9):
    r_c = sample_r(rho_c, n_c, reps_c, rng)
    z_c = np.arctanh(r_c)
    fisher = np.abs(z_c - np.arctanh(rho_c)) <= zq / np.sqrt(n_c - 3)
    naive = np.abs(r_c - rho_c) <= zq * (1 - r_c ** 2) / np.sqrt(n_c)
    print(f"rho = {rho_c}: Fisher z covers {fisher.mean():.4f}, naive covers {naive.mean():.4f}")

Because z has an approximately known variance, it also compares correlations. With independent samples of n1 and n2 cases, the hypothesis ρ1=ρ2 is tested by referring z1−z21/(n1−3)+1/(n2−3) to the standard normal distribution. The t statistic of Proposition 14.4.1(c) remains the exact test of ρ=0; for any other null value ρ0, either (z−tanh−1⁡ρ0)n−3 or the exact law Eq. 14.4.1 is used.

Example (Poverty and graduation)

Across the 50 states, the correlation between the poverty rate and the high-school graduation rate is r=−0.7613. Its t statistic for ρ=0 is −8.13 on 48 degrees of freedom, far out in the tail. The Fisher interval Eq. 14.4.3 is (−0.858,−0.613) and the exact interval is (−0.855,−0.609), almost the same, and both are noticeably asymmetric about r.

data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data.drop(index="District of Columbia")
x, y = data["poverty"].to_numpy(), data["hs_grad"].to_numpy()
n_s = len(x)
r_s = np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]
t_s = r_s * np.sqrt(n_s - 2) / np.sqrt(1 - r_s ** 2)
lo_z, hi_z = np.tanh(np.arctanh(r_s) + np.array([-1, 1]) * zq / np.sqrt(n_s - 3))
# exact interval: the rho at which the observed r sits at the 97.5% and 2.5% points
lo_x = optimize.brentq(lambda rh: r_cdf(r_s, rh, n_s) - 0.975, -0.99, 0.99)
hi_x = optimize.brentq(lambda rh: r_cdf(r_s, rh, n_s) - 0.025, -0.99, 0.99)
print(f"r = {r_s:.4f}, t = {t_s:.2f} on {n_s - 2} df")
print(f"Fisher z interval ({lo_z:.3f}, {hi_z:.3f}); exact interval ({lo_x:.3f}, {hi_x:.3f})")

Warning. Everything in this section uses bivariate normality, and not only through the conditional linear model. The asymptotic variance (1−ρ2)2/n comes from the fourth moments of Lemma 14.4.2. For other distributions it can be quite different, and then neither z nor the exact interval is reliable (Exercise (B2)). The t test of ρ=0 is more robust: by Theorem 14.1.2 it needs only that Y given X follow a normal linear model with constant variance, and when X and Y are independent this holds as soon as Y is normal.

14.4.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

A sample of n=28 pairs has r=0.62. Compute the 95% Fisher interval for ρ.

Solution. z=tanh−1⁡0.62=0.7250 and 1.96/25=0.392, so ζ lies in (0.333,1.117), and ρ in (tanh⁡0.333,tanh⁡1.117)=(0.321,0.806). The interval is longer below r than above.

Exercise (A2)

Two independent samples give r1=0.45 with n1=60 and r2=0.20 with n2=80. Test ρ1=ρ2 at level 0.05.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Suppose n(Tn−θ)→N(0,s(θ)2) for every θ in an interval, with s positive and continuous. Show that if g′(θ)=1/s(θ), then n(g(Tn)−g(θ))→N(0,1). Solve g′(ρ)=1/(1−ρ2) and recover Eq. 14.4.2.

Solution. By the delta method the limiting variance is g′(θ)2s(θ)2=1. With partial fractions, 1/(1−ρ2)=12[1/(1+ρ)+1/(1−ρ)], whose antiderivative vanishing at zero is 12[log⁡(1+ρ)−log⁡(1−ρ)]=tanh−1⁡ρ.

Exercise (B2)

Let X and U be independent standard normal variables and Y=XU. Show that X and Y are uncorrelated but not independent, and that nr→N(0,3). What happens to the level of the test that rejects ρ=0 when |T|>tn−2,0.025 in large samples? Which assumption of Theorem 14.1.2 fails?

Solution. EXY=EX2EU=0, but E(X2Y2)=EX4EU2=3≠EX2EY2=1, so they are dependent. Repeat the proof of Theorem 14.4.3 at ρ=0: the gradient is (0,0,1), so the limiting variance of nr is Var(XY)/(VarXVarY)=3. Since T≈nr for large n, the test rejects with probability tending to 2{1−Φ(1.96/3)}=0.258, not 0.05. Given X, Y is normal with mean zero but variance X2: the errors are not homoscedastic, so the conditional model of Definition 14.1.1 fails.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

For a general pair (X,Y) with finite fourth moments, standardized to means 0 and variances 1, show that n(r−ρ) is asymptotically normal with variance (1+ρ22)EX2Y2+ρ24(EX4+EY4)−ρ(EX3Y+EXY3). Check that it reduces to (1−ρ2)2 under normality, and explain why no transformation of r stabilizes the variance across all distributions.

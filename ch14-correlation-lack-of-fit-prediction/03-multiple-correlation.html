14.3  Multiple correlation

The population squared multiple correlation 𝛔𝛔ρY⋅X2=σXY⊤ΣXX−1σXYσY2 is the largest squared correlation between Y and a linear combination of X (Proposition 2.5.3). Section 9.5 studied its sample counterpart R2 for a fixed design (Theorem 9.5.1, Theorem 9.5.2). With random rows, R2 estimates ρY⋅X2. This section finds its exact distribution under multivariate normality, and uses it to test ρY⋅X2=0, to build an interval and to measure the bias.

Throughout, (Xi⊤,Yi), i=1,…,n, are independent draws from a (k+1)-variate normal distribution with ΣXX positive definite and σY2>0, and n≥k+2. By Eq. 3.5.2, 𝛃Yi=α+β⊤Xi+ei with independent ei∼N(0,σ2) independent of the regressors, where

𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃σ2=σY2(1−ρY⋅X2),β⊤ΣXXβ=σY2ρY⋅X2,soβ⊤ΣXXβσ2=ρY⋅X21−ρY⋅X2.
(14.3.1)

The ratio on the right is the population signal-to-noise ratio. We write ρ2 for ρY⋅X2 when no confusion can arise.

14.3.1. The sample multiple correlation as an estimator

Let SXX, sXY and sY2 be the sample covariances, with any common divisor. The fit with an intercept has 𝛃𝛃SSR=β^⊤Wβ^, where W is the centred cross-product matrix of the regressors and 𝛃β^=W−1∑i(Xi−X¯)(Yi−Y¯) (Theorem 6.6.2 with X2=1). Dividing by SST gives

R2=sXY⊤SXX−1sXYsY2,
(14.3.2)

the population formula with sample moments in place of population moments. Under normality it is more than a plug-in.

Proposition 14.3.1 (Maximum likelihood with normal rows)

Suppose the sample covariance matrix of the rows is nonsingular. The maximum likelihood estimates of 𝛃(α,β,σ2) are the least squares estimates and SSE/n, and the maximum likelihood estimate of ρY⋅X2 is R2.

Proof. The joint density of a row factorizes as 𝛍𝛃f(x;μX,ΣXX)f(y∣x;α,β,σ2), a normal density for the regressors times the conditional normal density of Theorem 3.5.2. The map from 𝛍(μ,Σ) to 𝛍𝛃(μX,ΣXX,α,β,σ2) is one-to-one onto the set where ΣXX is positive definite and σ2>0, with inverse 𝛔𝛃σXY=ΣXXβ, 𝛃𝛃σY2=σ2+β⊤ΣXXβ, 𝛃𝛍μY=α+β⊤μX. So the log-likelihood is a sum of two terms with separate parameters, and each can be maximized on its own. The conditional term is the likelihood of a normal linear model with model matrix [1,X1], maximized by least squares and SSE/n (Theorem 7.3.1).

In the marginal term the quadratic form is minimized over 𝛍μX at x¯, leaving −n2[log⁡detΣXX+tr(ΣXX−1S)] with S=W/n positive definite. If λ1,…,λk>0 are the eigenvalues of S1/2ΣXX−1S1/2, then log⁡detΣXX+tr(ΣXX−1S)−log⁡detS−k=∑j=1k(λj−log⁡λj−1)≥0, with equality iff every λj=1, that is, iff ΣXX=S. By invariance of maximum likelihood under reparameterization, the estimate of 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃ρ2=β⊤ΣXXβ/(σ2+β⊤ΣXXβ) is 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃β^⊤Sβ^/(SSE/n+β^⊤Sβ^)=SSR/(SSE+SSR)=R2.

The rest of the section concerns the sampling distribution of this estimator.

14.3.2. The distribution of R-squared

Theorem 14.3.2 (Distribution of R2 with normal rows)

Under the assumptions above, let F=n−k−1kR21−R2 and λ=ρ2/(1−ρ2).

  • (a) Given the regressors, F∼F(k,n−k−1,γ) with 𝛃𝛃γ=β⊤Wβ/σ2, where W is the centred cross-product matrix of the regressors.

  • (b) If ρ2=0, then F∼F(k,n−k−1) and R2∼Beta(k2,n−k−12), independently of the regressors. The overall F test of the regression is an exact size-α test of ρ2=0, which under joint normality is the hypothesis that Y is independent of X.

  • (c) If ρ2>0, then γ=λV with V∼χ2(n−1), and for 0<c<1

    Pr(R2≤c)=E[Pr{F(k,n−k−1,λV)≤(n−k−1)ck(1−c)}].
    (14.3.3)

  • (d) For each c∈(0,1), Pr(R2>c) is a strictly increasing function of ρ2∈[0,1).

Proof.

  • (a) The centred regressor matrix has rank k with probability one, because W∼Wk(n−1,ΣXX) (Lemma 14.2.3) is nonsingular when n−1≥k (the nonsingularity step in the proof of Lemma 14.2.4 needs only m≥k). Given the regressors, the model is a normal linear model with r=k+1 and mean 𝛃α1+X1β. By Theorem 9.5.2(a), F∼F(k,n−k−1,γ) with 𝛃γ=‖(M−P1)(α1+X1β)‖2/σ2. Since (M−P1)1=0 and 𝛃𝛃(M−P1)X1β=(I−P1)X1β, this is 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃β⊤X1⊤(I−P1)X1β/σ2=β⊤Wβ/σ2.

  • (b) ρ2=0 means 𝛔σXY=0, so 𝛃β=0 and γ=0 for every design. By Lemma 14.1.1, F∼F(k,n−k−1) independently of the regressors, and so is R2=kF/(kF+n−k−1), which is Beta(k/2,(n−k−1)/2) by Theorem 9.5.2(b). For jointly normal variables, zero covariance is independence (Theorem 3.4.1).

  • (c) By the proof of Lemma 14.2.3, W=(KX1)⊤(KX1), where the n−1 rows of KX1 are independent Nk(0,ΣXX). So 𝛃KX1β has independent 𝛃𝛃N(0,β⊤ΣXXβ) entries, and 𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃𝛃β⊤Wβ=‖KX1β‖2=(β⊤ΣXXβ)V with V∼χ2(n−1). By Eq. 14.3.1, γ=λV. Since R2≤c iff F≤(n−k−1)c/(k(1−c)), taking expectations of the conditional probability from (a) gives Eq. 14.3.3.

  • (d) Fix v>0 and f>0. By Theorem 4.1.4, Pr{F(k,n−k−1,λv)>f} is strictly increasing in λ, and λ=ρ2/(1−ρ2) is strictly increasing in ρ2. If ρ12<ρ22, the conditional tail probabilities therefore differ by a positive amount for every value of V>0, and so do their expectations.

Part (c) is Fisher’s (1928) distribution of the multiple correlation coefficient, reached here without any integration. Given the design, R2 is a transformed noncentral F variable, and the design enters only through the single number 𝛃𝛃β⊤Wβ, which is a scaled chi-squared variable because the regressors are normal. Figure 14.3.1 shows the resulting densities for n=25 and k=4, computed from Eq. 14.3.3 by averaging over a grid of quantiles of V.

Example (How biased is R-squared?)

With n=25, k=4 and 100000 simulated samples, the averages of R2 are 0.1669, 0.4026 and 0.6500 for ρ2=0, 0.3 and 0.6, against exact values 0.1667, 0.4026 and 0.6498. The exact values come from the formula E(R2)=1−n−k−1n−1(1−ρ2)2F1(1,1;n+12;ρ2), which follows from Fisher’s (1928) distribution of R2 (see Olkin and Pratt 1958), where 2F1 is the hypergeometric function; it can also be computed from Eq. 14.3.3. The bias shrinks as ρ2 grows. The adjusted R2 of Eq. 9.5.1 averages 0.0003, 0.2831 and 0.5800: unbiased under the null (Proposition 9.5.3(e)), but biased downwards when ρ2>0. Neither is unbiased everywhere. The standard deviations of R2 are 0.104, 0.146 and 0.118: with 25 cases, a single R2 says little about ρ2.

import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import optimize, special, stats

def r2_cdf(r, rho2, n, k, m=4000):
    """P(R^2 <= r) for iid normal rows: average the conditional noncentral F over V."""
    df2 = n - k - 1
    f = df2 * r / (k * (1 - r))
    if rho2 == 0:
        return stats.f.cdf(f, k, df2)
    V = stats.chi2.ppf((np.arange(m) + 0.5) / m, n - 1)      # equal-probability nodes
    return np.mean(stats.ncf.cdf(f, k, df2, rho2 / (1 - rho2) * V))

Part (d) says that the overall F test is unbiased and has power increasing in ρ2, as a test of ρ2=0 against random-regressor alternatives. For n=25 and k=4 it rejects when R2>0.364, and its power is 0.182, 0.377, 0.599 and 0.922 at ρ2=0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.5. A population in which the regressors account for a fifth of the variance of the response is detected less than half the time.

14.3.3. An interval for the squared multiple correlation

Part (d) also makes an exact confidence interval possible. For observed R2=r, let ρL2 be the value at which Pr(R2≤r)=0.975 and ρU2 the value at which it equals 0.025 (with ρL2=0 if Pr(R2≤r)<0.975 already at ρ2=0). Because the probability decreases in ρ2, [ρL2,ρU2] covers the true ρ2 exactly when R2 lies between the 2.5% and 97.5% points of its law at that ρ2, which has probability 0.95. This is the usual inversion of a family of tests (Proposition 12.1.2).

Example (Violent crime in the states)

For the 50 states in 2009, regress the violent crime rate on the high-school graduation rate, the poverty rate, the percentage of single-parent households, the percentage of white residents and the urbanization rate. Then R2=0.4607, the adjusted value is 0.3994, and F=7.52 on 5 and 44 degrees of freedom, with p-value 3.6×10−5. Read with random regressors, the 95% interval for ρ2 is (0.175,0.610). The interval is wide: five regressors and fifty cases leave much room for chance. It also treats the states as a random sample, which they are not; it describes hypothetical replications of a process generating rows like these, and here, as in Example (Poverty and graduation), no more can be claimed.

data = sm.datasets.statecrime.load_pandas().data.drop(index="District of Columbia")
regs = ["hs_grad", "poverty", "single", "white", "urban"]
y = data["violent"].to_numpy()
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y))] + [data[c].to_numpy() for c in regs])
fit = sm.OLS(y, X).fit()
n_s, k_s = len(y), len(regs)
R2_obs = fit.rsquared
print(f"R^2 = {R2_obs:.4f}, adjusted {fit.rsquared_adj:.4f}, F = {fit.fvalue:.2f},"
      f" p = {fit.f_pvalue:.2g}")

# 95% interval for rho^2: the values at which the observed R^2 is not extreme
def limit(prob):
    g = lambda rho2: r2_cdf(R2_obs, rho2, n_s, k_s) - prob
    return 0.0 if g(1e-12) < 0 else optimize.brentq(g, 1e-12, 0.999)

lower, upper = limit(0.975), limit(0.025)
print(f"95% interval for rho^2: ({lower:.3f}, {upper:.3f})")

Remark (Fixed or random?). Under a fixed design the expectation of R2 depends on the chosen regressor values (Exercise (B1)). With random rows R2 estimates ρY⋅X2, which still depends on the regressor distribution: a narrower range gives a smaller ρ2. The null law of the F test is the same in both readings.

14.3.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

A regression on k=3 random regressors from n=40 cases has R2=0.25. Compute the overall F statistic and its degrees of freedom. Under the null hypothesis ρ2=0, what are the mean and the distribution of R2?

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let R be the sample correlation matrix of (Y,X1,…,Xk), assumed nonsingular, and let rYY be the (1,1) entry of R−1. Show that R2=1−1/rYY, and that 1−R2=detR/detRXX, where RXX is the correlation matrix of the regressors.

Solution. Eq. 14.3.2 is unchanged when every variable is standardized, so R2=rXY⊤RXX−1rXY. By Theorem 1.6.4, the leading entry of R−1 is the inverse of the Schur complement 1−rXY⊤RXX−1rXY=1−R2, so rYY=1/(1−R2). By Theorem 1.6.3(b), detR=detRXX(1−R2).

Exercise (B2)

Show that R2 is unchanged if Y is replaced by aY+b with a≠0, or the regressors by B⊤X+c with B nonsingular. Show that ρY⋅X2 has the same invariance.

Exercise (B3)

Show that R2→ρY⋅X2 with probability one as n→∞, without assuming normality, provided the rows are independent and identically distributed with finite second moments and ΣXX positive definite.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1)

With normal rows, show that the likelihood ratio statistic for ρY⋅X2=0 against an unrestricted alternative is (1−R2)n/2, so the likelihood ratio test is the overall F test. Hint: under the null the covariance matrix is block diagonal; use the proof of Proposition 14.3.1 and Exercise (B1).

Solution. With S the covariance matrix of the rows with divisor n, the maximized log-likelihood of a (k+1)-variate normal sample is −n2[log⁡detS+(k+1)(1+log⁡2π)], by the argument in the proof of Proposition 14.3.1 applied to all k+1 variables. Under the null, Y and X are independent, the likelihood factorizes, and the maximum is the same expression with log⁡(sY2detSXX) in place of log⁡detS. The likelihood ratio is therefore (detS/(sY2detSXX))n/2=(1−R2)n/2, by Theorem 1.6.3(b) as in Exercise (B1). It is a decreasing function of R2, hence of F.
